Overview

The chamber smells like floor wax and burnt coffee. Congresswoman Olivia Edmunds stands at her desk on the House floor, one hand flat on a folder of poll numbers, the other holding a phone that will not stop buzzing. Her seatmate from Sandpoint is mid-argument two rows over. Somewhere behind her, a clerk is stacking amendment copies loud enough to hear over the gavel.

Her phone lights up again: a text from her own kitchen table, four hundred miles south. mom did u vote yet. It’s from her fourteen-year-old, who has been counting down the months to a learner’s permit since she turned thirteen.

Edmunds grew up on a dairy farm outside Coolidge, on roads with no bus service and no sidewalks, where a fifteen-year-old who couldn’t drive was a fifteen-year-old who couldn’t get to work. She now spends her weeknights on a different kind of road — the ER (Emergency Room) at Banner Hospital, stitching up whatever the highway sends in, some of it worse than others.

“The House recognizes the gentlewoman from Queen Creek,” the Speaker says.

The tally board behind him is still blank. Every member on the floor is watching to see which way she stands.

She has the folder. She has the numbers. She has about forty seconds before the clerk calls her name.

She opens the folder.

Background

House Bill 3320 has been introduced in the Arizona House of Representatives by a bipartisan group of legislators. The bill repeals Arizona’s Graduated License Program (GLP) — the state’s current tiered system for teen drivers (see Appendix A) — and replaces it with a single, universal minimum age of 18 for any Class D driver’s license, permit included. Under the bill, no one under 18 could legally drive at all, supervised or not.

Support for some change is broad in the House. The real fight is over how much change. The chamber splits along a rural/urban lineA figure of speech for a political split, not a geometry line: representatives divide based on whether their district is a dense city/suburb or a spread-out countryside area, because that changes what transportation options a teenager actually has.See the real source →: urban representatives, whose districts have buses, ride-shares, and short trip distances, are largely for the bill; rural representatives, whose districts do not, are largely against it.

Congresswoman Olivia Edmunds (R–Queen Creek, meaning Republican, representing the Queen Creek district) has represented her district for over five years. She works nights as a nurse at Banner Hospital in Queen Creek in addition to her legislative duties, and she has four children — two of whom are inside the exact age window HB 3320 would change. A recent poll of her constituents found broad support for raising the minimum age somewhat, but no consensus on how far (see Appendix D).

The two positions on the floor have real, opposing arithmetic behind them, not just opinion. Representatives supporting the bill point to a genuine cost families avoid: two more years without a teen driver on the family policy (see Appendix C). Representatives opposing it point to a genuine cost families take on instead: two more years in which someone else has to drive that teenager everywhere the teenager used to drive themselves (see Appendix C). Both sides also point to real national data on driver age and fatal-crash involvement, though the two sides read that same data differently (see Appendix B).

Several other places already draw this line differently than Arizona does, which both camps have cited on the floor (see Appendix E). Every sourced figure in this case, and the one stated modeling assumption it relies on, is documented in full (see Appendices F and G).

Edmunds has not committed publicly. With five years of seniority (time served in the seat, which tends to earn a member more influence over undecided colleagues) and a reputation for careful votes, she is widely expected to move a bloc of undecided rural members whichever way she goes — toward AYE, raising the age to 18, or toward NAY, keeping Arizona’s current minimum of 15½.

The final appendix (see Appendix H) lists the math vocabulary and “how to…” skills this case exercises, which Challenge 1 sends every Hero out to research.

Appendices

Appendix A — House Bill 3320 and Arizona’s Current Law

What HB 3320 changes. The bill repeals Chapter 17, Title 33 of the Arizona Code — "Title" and "Chapter" are just how Arizona's law books are organized into numbered sections, the way a textbook is organized into chapters; Chapter 17, Title 33 happens to be the one covering driver training courses — and amends Section 49-303 so that no one under 18 may be issued any Class D license, instruction permit, or driving privilege. It also repeals the current three-stage Class D learner’s-permit structure outright.

Arizona’s current law (unchanged unless HB 3320 passes):

StageMinimum ageWhat it allows
Graduated Instruction Permit15 years, 6 months [S1]Supervised driving only, licensed adult 21+ in the vehicle
Class G Graduated Driver License16 years [S1]Independent (unsupervised) driving, with passenger/night restrictions for the first 6 months (limits on how many non-family passengers may ride along, and a curfew on driving during certain late-night hours)
Restrictions lifted / Class D available18 years [S1]Full, unrestricted driving privileges
“(1) As an operator of a vehicle requiring a class D driver’s license, is under the age of eighteen (18) years…”

Under current Arizona law, a teen’s first day of independent, unsupervised driving is the day they turn 16 [S1]. Under HB 3320, that day would not arrive until 18. That is the 2-year window this case’s cost comparison (Appendix C) is built around.

Appendix C — The Real Cost of Two Years

Side 1 — the cost Option A avoids. Insurers charge more to cover a new teen driver because teens statistically file more claims (see Appendix B) — so adding a 16-year-old driver to a family auto policy raises the average annual premium by $4,050 [S3]. Under Option A, an Arizona family with a 16-to-17-year-old pays this for zero years instead of two.

Side 2 — the cost Option A creates. A parent who has to drive their teenager everywhere the teenager would otherwise have driven themselves is putting real extra miles — and real extra dollars — on the family vehicle. Two figures make that cost calculable:

FigureValue
Full per-mile cost of operating a mid-size sedan — every real ownership cost combined: depreciation (the car losing resale value as it ages), fuel, maintenance, insurance, and financing (loan interest, if the car isn't paid off in cash)66.37¢/mile [S4]
U.S. average annual miles driven, per car11,026 miles/year [S5]

No public dataset directly measures how many extra miles a parent drives chauffeuring a non-driving teenager (see Appendix F, MA1). Concretely, picture it as trips the teen would otherwise have made alone: a daily round trip to school, plus weekly practices, a part-time job, or seeing friends — each one becomes a mile a parent now drives instead, on top of the parent's own normal driving.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    Look again at Appendix B. The main table is a rate per 100,000 people of a given age — not a rate per mile driven, and not a rate per licensed driver. Appendix B also cites a separate, real statistic (S8, AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety): measured per mile driven, drivers ages 16–17 crash nearly twice as often as drivers ages 18–19.

    Two real, correctly-cited statistics about nearly the same age group point in opposite directions depending on which denominator you pick. Neither one is an exact match for Congresswoman Edmunds’s actual 15½-vs-18 comparison — S8’s 16–17-vs-18–19 bands are the closest real per-mile data available, not a perfect fit.
