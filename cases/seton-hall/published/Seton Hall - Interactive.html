Overview

The room is a hotel conference floor in midtown Manhattan, phones confiscated at the door, blinds drawn. Bernadette McGlade has been in this chair for four years now, and she still isn’t used to it — ten people deciding, in private, which college basketball programs get to keep playing and which go home. On the wall, a name: Seton Hall. Win-loss record. Offensive rating. Defensive rating. The strength of every team they’ve played.

“We’ve been on this one for twenty-five minutes,” says the committee chair. “Time to vote. Is Seton Hall one of the best teams in the country? Yeas.”

Five hands go up.

“Nays.”

Five hands go up.

Every head in the room turns to Bernadette. She hasn’t voted yet. Down the hall, someone’s phone keeps buzzing against a table — the news out of Ohio, out of Utah, the word pandemic passed room to room in whispers nobody in here has had time to process. Not yet. Right now there is only the tie, and her hand, and the chart on the wall.

“Bernadette,” the chair says. “It’s you.”

Background

Bernadette McGlade is the Commissioner of the Atlantic-10 Conference and, in the 2019-20 season, a sitting member of the NCAA Division I Men’s Basketball Committee — a 10-person body of conference and university administrators who serve staggered terms selecting, seeding, and bracketing the 68-team field for the NCAA Tournament, “March Madness.”

The committee’s job runs all season. There are 353 Division I teams, and because each plays only about 31-35 games, no two teams’ schedules overlap enough to compare records directly. To correct for that, the committee leans on opponent-adjusted statistics: not just how many points a team scores and allows, but how good the teams it played were on offense and defense. Those four numbers — Team Offensive Rating, Team Defensive Rating, Opponents’ Offensive Rating, and Opponents’ Defensive Rating — are defined in full in Appendix B, which also carries the real, opponent-adjusted numbers for the 25 teams that sat closest to the top-16 cutoff line that season.

What actually happened, disclosed up front: on March 11-12, 2020, before the committee could finish its work, the COVID-19 pandemic shut down the rest of the college basketball season. The NCAA Tournament was canceled outright — the first cancellation in its history — and no official bracket, seed list, or committee vote was ever finalized or released. This case is a reconstruction, not a transcript: it uses the real, dated season statistics that existed in the field on the day the season stopped to ask the question the committee never got to finish answering. The specific 5-5 tie in the Overview is a dramatized frame for the dilemma, not a leaked vote count.

Two real, independent rankings did exist on that frozen day — see Appendix A. Seton Hall finished the regular season 21-9 (13-5 Big East), a share of the Big East regular-season title. Whether that record and that rating profile is genuinely a top-16 résumé, or genuinely just short of one, is what you have to decide.

Appendix C — The Full 353-Team Field, and a Data-Quality Note

The bubble field in Appendix B is drawn from a full 353-team dataset covering every Division I team’s win-loss record and all four rating columns for the 2019-20 season. A cleaned copy is available alongside this case (data/team_stats_2019-20_full_field.csv).

Known data-quality issue, disclosed rather than fixed by guessing: 83 of the 353 teams’ win-loss records (about 1 in 4) were corrupted by spreadsheet auto-formatting in the original source file — a text value like “12-15” was silently converted into the calendar date December 15. The original value cannot be recovered from the source file. They are marked CORRUPTED in the full-field CSV rather than guessed at. None of the 25 teams in Appendix B are affected.

    Reveal the Twist

    Before you vote again — here are the two real, dated national rankings that existed on the actual day this season stopped. Nobody made these up for this case.

    The final AP media poll had Seton Hall at No. 15 — one spot inside the cutoff. The NCAA’s own real NET system, the actual tool the real committee uses, had Seton Hall at No. 15 too, in the final week it was tracked. Both are real. Neither is a landslide — and it’s not one ranking offering a wider safety margin than the other. It’s the same razor-thin edge, confirmed twice.

    If your Power Score was built only from Team Offensive and Team Defensive Rating — the two numbers that feel most obviously “Seton Hall’s own” — go back and check whether it used the Opponents’ columns at all. What just broke in your model? Were you wrong — or just missing information your formula never asked for?
