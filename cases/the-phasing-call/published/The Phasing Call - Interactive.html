Overview

Talia Brooks has the tracking readout up on the left monitor and the launch calendar up on the right, and the cursor has been parked on the same cell for six minutes.

Meridian Launch Systems' mission control room is quiet this late — just the low hum of the environmental system and, somewhere down the hall, a printer finishing a long job. Talia is the trajectory analyst who signs the rendezvous profile that goes into the flight plan, and right now there are two profiles, not one. The first plan on her left monitor is short and steep: launch, climb, and close on the client's station same day. The second plan, on the right, is patient: launch into a lower parking orbit and let the vehicle spend days catching up, orbit by orbit, before it ever gets close.

Her phone buzzes, face down on the console. The launch site's weather office, probably, with tomorrow's hold forecast. She doesn't pick it up yet. She already knows roughly what it will say, because she asked for exactly this number when she asked for the callback.

What she doesn't know yet, not with numbers she'd sign her name to, is which profile actually leaves the vehicle in better shape to dock — not just which one gets there faster, but which one leaves enough reserve, and enough room in the calendar, if tomorrow's launch slips. The short profile is unforgiving about exactly when it can lift off. The patient profile is forgiving about the launch day — but every extra orbit spent catching up eats into the same fixed reserve the short profile barely touches.

Talia pulls the readout and the calendar onto one screen and reaches for her calculator.

Background

Talia Brooks is the trajectory analyst at Meridian Launch Systems, a small commercial launch company under contract to fly a cargo vehicle to a client's space station in low Earth orbit. She is responsible for recommending the rendezvous profile the mission flies once the vehicle reaches its initial parking orbit — and once she signs off, that profile is what gets written into the flight plan and the launch-day procedures (see Appendix A).

Two rendezvous profiles are on the table for this mission, and both start from a parking orbit below the client's station and are aimed at the same docking port (see Appendix A).

The Fast-Track

A direct, few-orbit rendezvous profile: the vehicle closes on the station in a small number of orbits, same day, using a rapid sequence of catch-up burns. Reaching the station quickly leaves the largest possible share of the vehicle's fixed onboard autonomous-flight/consumables allowance untouched at docking — but the profile only closes cleanly if the vehicle reaches its parking orbit within a narrow band of initial phase angle relative to the station, which in turn narrows which calendar days a launch can actually be attempted (see Appendices B and D) — a real, documented engineering constraint on short rendezvous profiles [S1].

The Phasing Climb

A multi-orbit parking-orbit phasing rendezvous profile: the vehicle spends many more orbits — days, not hours — gradually catching up to the station's position before beginning final approach. Because the vehicle can absorb a much wider range of initial phase angle by simply flying more or fewer phasing orbits, far more calendar days become viable launch attempts — but every extra day spent phasing is a day drawn against the same fixed autonomous-flight/consumables allowance, leaving less reserve at docking (see Appendices B, C and D).

The client's station-keeping contract fixes a hard docking deadline and requires the vehicle to retain a minimum reserve of its autonomous-flight/consumables allowance at the moment of docking, for contingencies such as a failed first approach (see Appendix A) — either profile must satisfy both limits to be flyable at all. Meridian's vehicle has a fixed total autonomous-flight/consumables allowance regardless of which profile is flown; only how much of it is spent reaching the station changes between the two profiles (see Appendix C). Where a figure below is not a matter of public record, it is stated as an explicit modeling assumption (see Appendix E) rather than guessed, and every sourced figure is listed in Appendix F. The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case asks you to research are listed in Appendix G — where Challenge 1 sends you.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which profile to recommend; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 · Polar-to-rectangular conversion (D1)

    Convert the station's tracked position (Appendix A: bearing 48°, range 210 km) from polar to rectangular using x = r·cos(θ), y = r·sin(θ).

    Step 2 · Parametric equations and eliminating the parameter (D2/D3)

    Each profile is modeled as uniform circular motion at radius r = 210 km, sweeping angle at the Appendix B catch-up rate ω = 11.2°/hour, arriving at the station's D1 bearing (48°) exactly when its own Appendix B transit time elapses: x(t) = r·cos(θ0 + ωt), y(t) = r·sin(θ0 + ωt), where t is the parameter (elapsed hours since launch). Eliminating t (using sin²+cos²=1) gives the same rectangular equation for both profiles: x²+y² = r². Solve each profile's own equations for the elapsed time t at which (x(t), y(t)) reaches the D1 position — this is a check against Appendix B's own stated transit time, not a new figure to look up.

    Your answer

    Solving cos(θ0+ωt) = cos(48°) has infinitely many solutions differing by full 360° revolutions — the Phasing Climb's much longer transit means its correct t requires recognizing extra full turns, not just the first (principal-value) solution. Use the domain each profile is meant to close within (Appendix B) to pick the right one.

    Step 3 · Autonomous-flight/consumables reserve (D4)

    Reserve hours remaining = total allowance (Appendix C) − modeled transit time (Appendix B). Percent used = 100 × transit ÷ total allowance; percent remaining = 100 − percent used.
