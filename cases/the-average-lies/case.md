## Overview

The calculator on Ayo Bankole's screen has already decided. A green banner stretches across the top: **You're on track.** Below it sits a single box, *Expected annual return*, and the number she typed there a minute ago is the only reason the banner is green.

Across the desk, Mara Okafor, an eighth-grader, has not touched the mints in the bowl. A check from her grandmother is folded in her jacket pocket, and her sneakers don't quite reach the floor. Her mother is on the phone out in the lobby, where the carpet smells like cleaner and the coffee has been burnt since morning.

"The calculator says we have plenty," Mara says. "Why do you look worried?"

Ayo doesn't answer right away. On her second monitor are two buttons, side by side, each one a way of investing the gift for the four tuition bills that will start when Mara is a freshman in college. She has run the calculator both ways. It said yes both times.

She puts the cursor between the buttons and thinks about what the calculator isn't telling an eighth-grader.

## Background

Ayo Bankole is a financial planner at a credit-union advice desk. She does not sell products on commission. Her job is to tell a family, in writing, what a plan can promise and what it cannot.

Mara's grandmother has given the family $50,000 [MA1] to pay for four years of in-state public college tuition and fees, with the first bill due 5 [MA2] years from now and the next three due at the start of each year after (SEE APPENDIX A). She wants whatever is left after the fourth bill to go toward Mara's graduate school. If the money runs short on any bill, the family must borrow the unpaid part of that bill, and this case counts the dollars borrowed.

Ayo has two ways to invest the gift. **The Growth Mix** puts 70% [MA3] in a broad index of large U.S. stocks and the rest in 10-year U.S. Treasury bonds. **The Steady Mix** puts 30% [MA4] in stocks and the rest in bonds. Either mix is restored to its shares every January and held until the last bill is paid (SEE APPENDIX C).

Many online college-savings calculators ask for a single number: an assumed steady annual return. This case models that number as each mix's own long-run compound average yearly return, computed from the history in Appendix B, a typical single-number input. The tuition bills themselves grow each year; you will choose the growth rate within the band measured in Appendix A, and the case uses the published sticker price, not the lower price after aid.

Appendix B gives the real yearly return on each investment, year by year. A *window* is any run of consecutive calendar years as long as the wait plus the four bills. The rules for timing, borrowing and counting leftover are in Appendix C, and Appendix D is a blank balance table to build the work on. Appendix E lists the stated assumptions and the ones you must choose and defend, Appendix F lists every source, and Appendix G lists the "how to…" questions you will research.

## Dilemma

You are Ayo Bankole.

Do you:

**A.)** Recommend **The Growth Mix** — invest the gift mostly in stocks and hold it until the last bill is paid **OR**

**B.)** Recommend **The Steady Mix** — invest the gift mostly in bonds and hold it until the last bill is paid

## Appendix C — The Two Mixes and the Counting Rules

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D3, D4, D5). The mixes are rules, not forecasts. The pool is held in the two investments in Appendix B and restored to the shares below every January.

| Item | Growth Mix | Steady Mix |
|---|---|---|
| Share in stocks | 70% [MA3] | 30% [MA4] |
| Share in 10-year Treasury bonds | 30% [MA3] | 70% [MA4] |

**Counting rules.** Each window starts on January 1 of its first calendar year. Each January, any bill due that year is paid first, and then the remaining balance earns that year's return. If the balance is smaller than the bill, the family borrows the missing part and the balance becomes zero; borrowed dollars are counted but not carried into later bills. The leftover is the balance at the end of the last calendar year of the window, one year after the last bill. A window is as long as the wait to the first bill plus the four bills (Appendix A).

- **How much leftover is worth wanting (D3).** Choose a leftover amount within the range the case allows: $15,000 to $30,000 [MA5]. If you care only about paying the bills, you may choose to ignore leftover and say so.
- **The share of windows you require (D4).** State how often the bills must be paid in full for a plan to count as safe, and defend that number.
