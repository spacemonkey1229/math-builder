## Overview

The review room is a converted equipment closet behind the pool deck, and it smells of chlorine and warm electronics.

Two monitors sit side by side on a folding table. On the left, a swimmer's hand slides toward a yellow wall. On the right, a different angle of the same instant. The Chief Judge has watched both so many times that her eyes sting. Outside, the arena is still loud; somebody has started a chant in a language she doesn't speak.

A protest form lies face-up beside the keyboard, the ink still shiny. The team leader who filed it stands near the door and says nothing at all, which is somehow louder than shouting would be. On the printout taped to the wall, the two results sit one above the other, and the gap between them is the smallest the scoring system can write.

Everyone who has to live with this call is waiting on one thing: not a verdict about two athletes, but a ruling about which instrument gets the last word when the touchpad and the human eye could tell different stories.

She taps the space bar, and the footage freezes on a single frame. Then she pulls the rulebook toward her and opens the folder with the two possible rulings.

## Background

The Chief Judge is the senior official ruling on a protest at a swimming final. Her authority covers one thing: deciding which kind of evidence orders the finish when the electronic result is challenged. She is accountable to every swimmer in every final, not only this one, because whatever rule she applies tonight is the rule the next close finish will be judged under.

**This decision point is constructed.** In the real 2008 Olympic 100 m butterfly final, Michael Phelps was timed in 50.58 and Milorad Čavić in 50.59 (SEE APPENDIX C). Serbia protested; the referee reviewed high-speed video and the result was upheld, and **FINA's ruling stands**. This case does not ask you to re-try that ruling. It asks you to reason about the evidence the officials faced and about the rule that should govern evidence like it.

What the record does and does not say is set out in Appendix A. In short: the electronic result is documented, the rule text is documented (SEE APPENDIX B), but **who first touched the wall is claimed differently by different named sources**, and no source found publishes a measured pad delay (SEE APPENDIX F).

The two rulings in front of the Chief Judge:

- **Pad Ranks.** The automatic equipment orders the finish, as the rule is written. The reported times differ, so the swimmer with the lower reported time is ranked ahead, applied identically to every race.
- **Contact Ranks.** Where the pad and the eye could disagree, high-speed video of first hand contact orders the finish, because first contact is what a race is meant to reward.

There is no "dead heat" ruling available. The reported times are not equal, and the rules award a shared placing only to equal times at one-hundredth of a second (SEE APPENDIX B). The question is which instrument orders the finish, not whether the finish is a tie.

One idea runs through the numbers. A touchpad registers a touch a short time after the hand arrives. If that delay is the same for both swimmers, it cancels out when the two are compared. Only a **difference** in delay between the two lanes can make the pad's order differ from the order of first contact. How big that difference could plausibly be is **not measured in any source found**; this case asks you to state a range and defend it (SEE APPENDICES E AND F). Every figure here is cited (SEE APPENDIX G) or declared an assumption (SEE APPENDIX F). The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case asks you to research are listed in Appendix H.

## Dilemma

You are the Chief Judge.

Do you:

**A.)** Rule **Pad Ranks** — the touchpad result orders the finish, as the rule is written **OR**

**B.)** Rule **Contact Ranks** — first hand contact on high-speed video orders the finish

## Appendix C — How a Finish Works, and the Race Figures

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D1, D3).

**How a finish works, in plain words.**

- **Butterfly finish.** Both hands reach forward and touch the wall at the end of the race. A swimmer can be **stroking** (still pulling with the arms right up to the wall) or **gliding** (arms already stretched out, coasting in).
- **Touchpad ('the pad').** A large plate fixed to the wall at the end of each lane, which is what a swimmer touches (the yellow wall in the Overview is the pad). Picture a thin plate that has to be pressed in a little before it closes an electrical contact and stops that lane's clock. A light graze might not close it; a firm press does. So the moment the pad registers comes slightly **after** the moment a hand first reaches the wall: the time for the plate to be pressed far enough. That delay is the **pad lag**. In this case's model, the lag in a lane depends on how the hand arrives: a hand still driving into the wall (stroking) presses firmly at once, while an outstretched hand coasting in (gliding) may rest on the plate and need longer to press it in. Why the two lanes' lags could differ is exactly the open question; no source measures it (SEE APPENDIX F).

- **Differential lag.** The pad lags in both lanes. A lag that is the same in both lanes cancels when you compare the two. Only the **difference** between the two lanes' lags matters for the order.
- **Automatic equipment vs timekeepers.** The pads and the clock they stop are the automatic equipment. Timekeepers are officials with handheld stopwatches, who are the back-up.
- **FINA** is the international governing body that wrote the swimming rules. A **protest** is a formal written complaint to the referee, who decides it. The **Chief Judge** in this case is the senior official ruling on it.
- **Placing, tie and dead heat.** A placing is a finishing position (first, second). A dead heat is a tie, where two swimmers share a placing.
- **Frame rate.** A video is a series of still frames. At 60 frames per second a new still image is taken every 1/60 of a second; anything that happens between two frames cannot be seen exactly. To judge first contact, a judge steps through the footage frame by frame and looks for the first frame in which a hand is on the wall. That is why the gap between frames limits how exactly the moment can be placed, and why a judge cannot be exactly sure which frame to call (the extra band J).
- **Thousandths.** The clock can count finer than a hundredth of a second, for example 50.584. The rule says the last digit is not recorded or used, so the printed result is 50.58. (Whether a real clock drops that digit or rounds it is part of what D3 asks you to consider.) Heats are the earlier rounds of an event; this was a final, so SW 13.4.2 below is shown only for completeness.
- **Team leader.** The official who speaks for a country's swimmers and is allowed to file a protest.
- **Referee and Chief Judge.** The referee is the official who rules on protests in a real meet. In this constructed case the Chief Judge is the senior official holding that ruling role, and is a role, not a real person.

## Appendix E — The Differential-Lag Table and the Reliability Rule

**The reliability rule (a model, used in D5).** Call a finish "close" if the true first-contact margin c is equally likely to be anywhere from −W to +W seconds. An instrument with an **indecision band** of b seconds orders any finish with |c| < b correctly only half the time, and orders the rest correctly. So its probability of ordering a close finish correctly is

P = 1 − b ÷ (2 × W)

- Option A (Pad Ranks): b = |Δ|.
- Option B (Contact Ranks): b = (time between frames) + J, where J is the extra band a judge needs to call first contact from the footage.

**Percent difference** means (larger P − smaller P) ÷ smaller P × 100.

**A worked example with made-up numbers (not this race).** Suppose W = 0.04 s and an instrument's band is b = 0.01 s. The finishes with true margin between −0.01 s and +0.01 s are 0.02 ÷ 0.08 = 25% of all close finishes. Inside that zone the instrument cannot tell the order, so it is right only half the time (it loses half of 25% = 12.5%). Outside the zone it is always right. So P = 1 − 0.125 = 0.875, which is also 1 − 0.01 ÷ (2 × 0.04). The pad's band is |Δ| because it can only misorder a finish whose true margin is smaller than the difference between the two lanes' delays; the footage's band is the gap between frames plus the extra band J because a moment that falls between two frames, or a hand-contact a judge cannot pin to one frame, cannot be placed any more exactly than that. In short: the pad can only misorder two hands whose true arrival times differ by less than the difference between the lanes' delays, and footage can only misorder two arrivals that fall within about one frame (plus the judge's reading band) of each other.

**Scenario values for D5 (declared, not measured; SEE APPENDIX F):** Δ = 0.010 s, J = 0.005 s, frame rate 240 frames per second, W = 0.05 s.
