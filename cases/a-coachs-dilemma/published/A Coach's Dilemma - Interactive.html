Overview

Priya Shah has run this exact printout through the copier twice, like a third pass might change the numbers. Two hours before tip-off (the opening jump ball that starts the game), the visiting locker room at STAPLES Center smells like fresh tape and cold Icy Hot. Down the hall, Coach Kevin McHale is running through matchups on a whiteboard with the assistants. Priya's the analytics coordinator. Nobody in that room needs to like her memo. They just need her math to survive being asked about.

Her Rockets are down two games to one in the second round, and the number that matters most today isn't on the whiteboard — it's a free-throw percentage belonging to a Clippers center who can protect a rim better than he can make a foul shot. Hack him deliberately, all series, and you trade clean defense for two guaranteed free throws you like the odds on. Play him straight, and you let the best offense in the building run its actual offense.

Priya taps her pen against the page. Both memos are already written — she drafted both on the flight, because she didn't trust herself to only build the case for the answer she wanted first. One says hack him. One says don't. In four minutes, an assistant coach is going to knock on this door and ask which folder she's handing over.

She squares the two folders on the table, one on top of the other, and waits for the knock.

Background

Priya Shah is the Houston Rockets' in-house analytics coordinator during their real 2015 Western Conference Semifinals series against the Los Angeles Clippers (see Appendix A) — the NBA's real playoff round played right after the first round, with the winner of each best-of-seven series advancing toward the championship. Head coach Kevin McHale makes the final in-game call, but Priya's memo is what he reads walking onto the floor — her job is to hand him one clear recommendation, not a shrug. An NBA game is played in four 12-minute quarters (48 minutes total, not counting stoppages) — Appendix C's “fourth quarter” scenario means the last of those four, when a close game is usually decided.

The Clippers' center — the tallest player on the floor, whose main defensive job is protecting the basket (“the rim”) against close shots and grabbing rebounds — DeAndre Jordan, is one of the most efficient scorers in the league near the rim and one of the least reliable from the free-throw line — a real, well-documented gap that opposing coaches around the NBA were exploiting that season by intentionally fouling him away from the ball, a tactic sportswriters nicknamed “Hack-a-Jordan” (see Appendix A). Under real NBA rules, an intentional non-shooting foul sends the fouled player to the line for two free throws, win or lose the following possession outright (see Appendix C) — unlike some other levels of basketball, there is no “one-and-one” here: Jordan always gets both attempts.

Priya's recommendation comes down to two named options for how Houston defends the Clippers' half-court offense (a possession where the defense has time to get set up in its own end before the play starts, the normal, slower kind of possession — as opposed to a fast-break “transition” possession, described below) for the meaningful stretches of Game 4:

Hack-a-Jordan — instruct Houston's centers to intentionally foul Jordan the moment he touches the ball away from the basket, sending him to the line for two free throws instead of letting the Clippers run a possession.

Play It Straight — defend every Clippers possession honestly, contest every shot, and never intentionally foul.

Both options carry a real, sourced cost the naive version of this math misses. Fouling gives up nothing if Jordan bricks both shots — except that a missed second free throw is a live ball (any player from either team is free to grab it right away, the same as a missed regular shot) rather than a dead ball (play stops and the ball has to be passed back in from out of bounds, giving both defenses a moment to reset) — so the Clippers can crash the boards (fight for the rebound) for it just like a missed field goal; a stopped clock also means Houston's own offense loses whatever transition chance it would have had off a clean stop — a transition possession is a fast break down the court before the defense can get organized, and it scores, on average, more efficiently than a slower half-court possession does (see Appendix D). Playing it straight risks nothing on the transition front, but it also means Houston's primary rim-protecting center is out there committing ordinary defensive fouls in live-ball situations for the entire game — and the real NBA foul-out rule is unforgiving (see Appendix C).

Every number Priya has to work with — Jordan's real shooting splits, the Clippers' real offensive efficiency, and the real foul-out rule — is in Appendices B and C. The vocabulary and the “how to…” skills this case asks of you are listed in Appendix F — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

Appendix C — The Six-Foul Rule and the Fourth-Quarter Scenario

The real rule. Under real NBA rules, a player is disqualified — “fouls out” — after committing 6 personal fouls in a 48-minute game, and must leave the floor for the rest of the game once the 6th is called [S4]. There is no way back in. Unlike some college and high-school rulebooks, an NBA player who is intentionally fouling away from the ball is charged a personal foul exactly like any other foul — it counts toward the same 6.

The real free-throw rule for an intentional foul. An intentional (non-shooting) foul in the NBA sends the fouled player to the line for two free throws, regardless of whether he makes the first one — there is no “one-and-one” the way there is in NCAA men's basketball [S4]. Jordan always shoots both.

The fourth-quarter planning scenario (D4). This is a stated scenario for you to compute with, not a claim about what actually happened in any specific real game: suppose Houston's primary center — the player McHale designates to do the fouling — enters the fourth quarter of a hard-fought Game 4 having already picked up 4 personal fouls over the first three quarters, an ordinary and realistic total for a physical center in a physical series. Use the real 6-foul rule above to work out how much room that leaves him.

    Reveal the Twist

    One more thing. This case is set before Game 4 actually tips off — but Game 4 already happened, in real life, on May 10, 2015. Here's the real box score. Houston ran Hack-a-Jordan hard. DeAndre Jordan went to the free-throw line 34 times in that one game — 28 of those attempts came in the FIRST HALF ALONE, a real NBA playoff record — and made 14 of them. Houston's primary center, the one doing most of the fouling, picked up personal foul after personal foul and fouled out with 18 minutes and 22 seconds played — barely more than a third of the game — and drew a technical foul on the same play, ejecting him. The Clippers won 128-95. Houston lost by 33.

    What this invalidates: the comfortable read that the corrected modeled edge for Hack-a-Jordan means the strategy “worked” that night. It did not — but that does not mean D3's math was wrong. A real, defensible expected-value edge is still just an edge, not a guarantee, and one real game is a tiny sample. The foul-out risk D4 asked you to quantify in the abstract happened for real, faster and worse than the case's own stated scenario — Houston's primary center didn't even make it out of the first three quarters.

    The follow-up (the whole point): Was the recommendation wrong, or did the dice just land badly? What's the difference between those two things, and how would you even tell? Separate process (was the modeled edge a real, honestly-computed one, given what was knowable before Game 4?) from outcome (Houston got blown out anyway). A good process can still produce a bad single-game outcome, and a bad process can still get lucky once.

    Bonus real color: Houston lost Game 4 by 33 but came back to win the series 4-3 anyway (Games 5, 6, and 7 all went to Houston). That fact isn't needed for any of this case's math — it's real, and a good reminder that one bad night doesn't retroactively make a defensible pre-game process wrong.
