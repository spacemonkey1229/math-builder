Overview

"Timeout!" Coach John Cavenaugh's whistle cuts through 25,000 screaming voices as his players jog toward the bench, chests heaving. Four seconds. That's all that's left on the scoreboard clock. His Memphis Tigers cling to a two-point lead, and Kansas has the ball, already jogging it in from the sideline judge's hands.

Cavenaugh crouches at the center of the huddle. Nine faces stare back at him, sweat-soaked, waiting. Assistant coach Marcus taps the whiteboard, already half-drawn with an inbounds set that doesn't matter yet — not until Cavenaugh says the word.

Two words, really. Foul them sends Kansas to the free-throw line on purpose — a one-and-one, a shooter alone at the stripe with no one else touching the ball. Play defense means trusting five tired legs to contest whatever look Kansas manufactures in the final seconds, a three to win it outright or a two to force overtime.

Marcus leans in. "Coach. Clock's not stopped for us."

Cavenaugh's eyes go to the scorer's table, then back to the nine players in front of him — the players who have to execute whatever he says next, in the next three seconds, with the National Championship hanging on it.

He opens his mouth.

Background

The Memphis Tigers, led by head coach John Cavenaugh (Appendix A), face the Kansas Jayhawks in the final of the men's college basketball championship tournament. It is a back-and-forth game all night — Kansas pulls ahead, Memphis answers, and with under a minute left Kansas claws back within two points of Memphis's lead. Now, with the clock reading under four seconds and Memphis ahead 62-60, Kansas has the ball, trailing by exactly the margin a single three-pointer erases (Appendix B).

Coach Cavenaugh has to choose, in the next few seconds, between two ways to defend a two-point lead with almost no time on the clock:

The Foul — instruct his players to intentionally foul a Kansas ball-handler the instant the inbounds pass is caught. Under the current NCAA men's rule, a team that has committed its seventh foul of the half sends the other team to the free-throw line for a one-and-one: the fouled player shoots a single free throw, and only if that first attempt is made does the player earn a second attempt (Appendix D). Memphis is already in the bonus, so this option is live.

The Stand — instruct his players to switch onto the ball, deny the catch where possible, and contest whatever shot Kansas manages to get off before the clock reads zero.

Both choices carry real risk. A foul concedes free points with certainty and depends entirely on which Kansas player ends up at the line and how well that player shoots free throws (Appendix C). Playing defense concedes nothing for free, but a contested three-pointer that goes in loses the game outright — there is no overtime to fall back on the way there is if Kansas only ties it from the free-throw line.

Every Kansas player who logged minutes in the championship game (Appendix B) has a full season of free-throw, two-point, and three-point shooting on record (Appendix C) — the numbers Coach Cavenaugh has to weigh in the three seconds before he answers Marcus.

Appendix A — Coach John Cavenaugh

This case is fiction. Coach John Cavenaugh, the Memphis Tigers, and this specific championship game are invented for this case study — they are not real people, teams, or a real recorded game.

In this story, John Cavenaugh has coached the Memphis Tigers for over a decade, building the program from a middle-of-the-pack team into a nationally ranked contender. This season, Memphis went undefeated in conference play, spent multiple weeks ranked in the national top 3, and won three straight conference tournament games to reach the national final against a Kansas Jayhawks team with a long history of tournament success.

A note on the source: the underlying decision — foul or play defense, up two, with seconds left — is a real, famous coaching dilemma from the 2008 NCAA Men's Basketball Championship, when Kansas beat Memphis in overtime after Memphis missed the front end of a one-and-one free throw late and Kansas hit a game-tying three-pointer. This case fictionalizes the names, the exact score, and every player statistic — it does not claim to reproduce that real game's real numbers.

Appendix D — Modeling Assumptions

The one-and-one rule. What is a one-and-one? Under current NCAA men's basketball rules, once a team has committed 7 fouls in a half, every subsequent foul sends the fouled player to the free-throw line for a one-and-one: one free throw is attempted, and the shooter earns a second attempt only if the first is made. Miss the first attempt and the shooting stops there — and importantly, the ball stays live after a missed front-end attempt, recoverable by either team, not an automatic dead-ball turnover.

Assumption 1 — season percentage as single-attempt probability. Each Kansas player's season shooting percentage (Appendix C) is treated as the probability that player makes any single attempt of that shot type on the final possession — a real simplification, stated openly.

Assumption 2 — the one-and-one is two dependent shots at the same probability. If Memphis fouls a player with season free-throw percentage p, that player must make the first free throw (probability p) to even attempt the second (probability p). The probability Kansas converts both and ties the game is therefore p × p = p2 — not p. Missing either shot, in this simplified model, means Memphis wins outright.

Assumption 3 — the final possession. Memphis leads 62-60 with under 4 seconds left and Kansas has the ball. A made Kansas 2-point shot ties the game (sending it to overtime); a made Kansas 3-point shot wins it outright for Kansas. This case does not require modeling what happens in a hypothetical overtime period.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    One more thing. You've been assuming that if Memphis fouls and the front end of the free throw is missed, the possession is over — Memphis wins. That's not the rule. A missed front-end free throw is a live ball. Either team can rebound it. If Kansas's shooter misses the first free throw, Kansas can still crash the boards, grab the miss, and put it back up — no whistle, no new foul required, just basketball.

    This doesn't undo your Step 3 math — p² is still the probability of the clean, two-make sequence. But it means the true probability Kansas survives a missed front end is higher than "Memphis automatically wins," by an amount this case gives you no real data to compute.
