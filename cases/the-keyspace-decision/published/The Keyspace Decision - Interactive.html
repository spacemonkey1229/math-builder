Overview

The dashboard on Renata Cho's second monitor is still counting. Nine hundred and forty login attempts against the same account in the last six minutes, all from different IP addresses, all failing, all aimed at a mid-level accounts-payable clerk named in the alert as d.ferreira. The coffee she poured before the page went off is still too hot to drink. Somewhere down the row a teammate's headset mic clicks on and off — a normal Tuesday call, nothing wrong in his corner of the floor.

Renata is Bramwell Analytics' security operations analyst, and the identity policy on her screen carries her name in the change log, not the CISO's. Two draft policies sit open in adjacent tabs. One would make every employee's password longer. The other would leave passwords short and bolt a second factor onto every login. Both drafts are dated for the board meeting where the fiscal-year security budget gets signed, and both are dated before the auditor's follow-up visit that flagged the current policy as a finding last quarter — a finding with her name on the remediation line.

The clerk's account survives the attempt; the credential-stuffing tool moves on to the next name on its list within the hour. It is not this attack Renata is actually worried about. It is the one that doesn't show up on a dashboard at all — a password typed once into a fake login page, months before anyone notices.

Her manager's message is still sitting unread in the corner of the screen: Which one are we bringing to the board Thursday?

Renata pulls both drafts onto one monitor and starts reading them side by side.

Background

Renata Cho is the security operations analyst at Bramwell Analytics, a mid-size firm whose employees each hold a single directory account used to sign into email, finance systems, and the firm's client-data platform. She does not own the budget and cannot compel the board, but she authors the password-and-authentication standard that the whole company runs on, and the remediation plan for the auditor's finding is filed under her name (see Appendix D). If the finding recurs at the next audit, the follow-up escalates to her director.

Two draft standards are under consideration, each meant to fully replace Bramwell's current password policy (see Appendix A):

The Long Wall would require every account password to be at least 14 characters, drawn from a stated character set, with no second authentication factor required at login.

The Second Gate would allow account passwords as short as 8 characters, drawn from the same character set, but would require a second authentication factor — a phone app or hardware key — on every login, for every account, with no exceptions.

Both standards would apply to the same population of employee accounts (see Appendix E, MA1). Both must also satisfy one constraint neither draft can avoid: Bramwell's accounts-payable system is a legacy platform that Bramwell does not control the code of, and it has never been updated to accept anything past its original design — it stores only the first 8 characters of whatever password it is given, drawn only from lowercase letters and digits, no matter how long or how complex the password an employee actually types (see Appendix A). Whatever the company-wide standard becomes, the accounts-payable system's real, effective password protection is capped at that smaller keyspace, for every employee who touches it.

An attacker who wants into a Bramwell account has more than one way in. One path is to guess the password directly, at a rate a modern offline cracking rig can sustain (see Appendix B). The other path is to steal or phish the password from the employee, or to reuse one leaked from a breach of some other site the employee also used it on. Public breach research measures how often real account takeovers happen by each of these routes, and separately measures how much a second authentication factor actually blocks — even when the attacker already has a correct password (see Appendix C).

Each draft standard carries its own annual cost to implement and support across Bramwell's whole employee population (see Appendix D). Where a needed figure is not a matter of public record, it is stated as an explicit modeling assumption (see Appendix E) rather than guessed, and every sourced figure is listed in Appendix F. A curious reader who wants to go beyond the required math will find further real-world reading in Appendix G. The vocabulary and the "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in Appendix H — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

Appendix B — Offline Attack Benchmark
Value
Sustained guess rate, modern offline attack against the company's stored password hashes138,675 hashes/second — a rented 16× Nvidia RTX 5090 GPU fleet (two racks of eight) cracking bcrypt at work factor 10, the 2026 benchmark rig [S1]
Seconds per year (for the compound-unit conversion in D2)31,536,000 (365 × 24 × 60 × 60)

Worked check. At this rate, an exhaustive search of the Second Gate's nominal 8-character, 70-symbol keyspace (576,480,100,000,000 combinations) takes 576,480,100,000,000 ÷ 138,675 s⁻¹ = 4,157,053,470 s ÷ 31,536,000 s/yr ≈ 131.8 years — matching Hive Systems' own published figure of 132 years for an 8-character password drawn from the full character mix [S1], to within rounding. This is the same benchmark rate used for every crack-time figure in this case.

Appendix C — Breach-Vector Data
Value
Share of real-world breaches in which credential abuse — stolen, phished, or reused credentials — appears anywhere in the attack chain39% [S2]
Share of real-world breaches whose first known access vector was credential abuse13% under the 2026 report's revised category boundaries, or 16% on the prior year's category boundaries (comparable to earlier editions)

Rate at which a second authentication factor blocks account takeover, even when the attacker already holds a correct, un-phished password98.56% (risk reduction measured specifically on accounts whose password had already leaked) [S3]

A figure this case does not use, and why. Published breach research does not isolate "pure brute-force guessing against an unknown password" as its own, separately measured category — Verizon's own 2026 report folds credential stuffing, password reuse, and brute-force guessing together with phished and stolen credentials into one "credential abuse" bucket, and states plainly that it cannot separate them further. Rather than invent a brute-force-only percentage that does not exist in the source, D4 uses the 39% full-chain credential-abuse share as the case's stated allocation of "the channel where the attacker holds a valid credential, however obtained," and treats the complementary 61% as the channel where D2's crack-time figures are what actually governs the outcome.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — D1: Keyspace (Fundamental Counting Principle)

    Use the Fundamental Counting Principle: total passwords = (character-set size) raised to (password length), applied once per character position. Compute all three keyspaces from Appendix A.

    Step 2 — D2: Time to Crack (compound-unit conversion)

    Convert each D1 keyspace into years, using Appendix B's rate: keyspace ÷ (hashes/second) = seconds, then seconds ÷ (seconds/year) = years.

    Step 3 — D3: Collision Bound (Pigeonhole Principle)

    Using the Pigeonhole Principle: how many employees, sharing the legacy system's reduced keyspace, would guarantee two identical passwords? Compare that to Bramwell's actual AP-system headcount (Appendix E, MA1 × MA2).

    Step 4 — D4: Breach-Vector Weighting

    Combine Appendix C's credential-abuse share (39%) and MFA-blocking rate (98.56%) with your D2 crack-time figures into an annual probability of compromise for each option. Option A gets no MFA reduction on the credential-abuse channel; Option B's credential-abuse channel is reduced by the MFA-blocking rate.

    Step 5 — D5: The Primary Metric

    Invert each D4 probability to get expected years to compromise, then divide by each option's annual per-employee cost (Appendix D). The case's primary metric is expected years to compromise, per dollar of annual cost — higher is better.

    This calculator will tell you which of your two typed-in numbers is larger — it will never tell you the case's own answer key. If your D1–D4 arithmetic has an error, this step's "larger number" may not match what a correct solve produces.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    Everyone in this room has now computed D2. Read your Long Wall crack-time number out loud. Now your Second Gate number. The Long Wall's nominal keyspace crushes the Second Gate's — by a factor big enough that dividing D2's raw crack-time by cost alone makes The Long Wall look like the obvious winner, by an enormous margin.

    That number is real. Every digit of it is correctly computed. And it is the wrong answer. It assumes the only way into Bramwell's systems is guessing the password from scratch, offline, against stolen hashes. Verizon's 2026 breach report says that in 39% of real breaches, the attacker already had a valid credential — stolen, phished, or reused — before they ever touched a login screen. A fourteen-character password does nothing to stop that.
