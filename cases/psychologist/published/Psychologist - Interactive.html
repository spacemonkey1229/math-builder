Overview

Harriet Gluberton drops into the leather chair behind her mahogany desk, still hearing the courtroom in her ears — a witness's voice cracking, the gavel, the particular hush that falls over a room once someone has said something nobody can take back. She sat through it again today, dictating testimony no one in that room will ever forget. It's the reason she started this school in the first place.

On the desk sits a thick manila folder her assistant Paul left an hour ago. A sticky note is stuck to the front: “Need your final three by end of day tomorrow. — P.”

She opens it. Inside are printed test sheets, one stapled packet per applicant, each one a kid somewhere right now with no idea their whole future might turn on a spreadsheet. A handful of seats remain open at the Academy For Courtroom Reporters this year. A handful of names go out tomorrow morning; everyone else gets a form letter.

Harriet built AFCR from nothing but her own signature on a loan, and every graduate who has ever sat in the well of a courtroom taking down testimony carries a piece of her name with them. She funds the whole program herself. If AFCR stops producing the best, none of it means anything.

She flips past the memory-test scores, then the typing sheet, running a finger down each applicant's row of numbers. Somewhere in these columns of raw data is the answer to who becomes the next great court reporter — if only the numbers agreed with each other.

She pulls the spreadsheet toward her and opens a blank ranking sheet beside it.

Background

Harriet Gluberton founded the Academy For Courtroom Reporters (AFCR) in Boise, Idaho more than two decades ago, and she has personally funded every dollar of its tuition since. AFCR has built its reputation on training some of the country's most decorated court reporters — professionals who, at the top of the field, are certified to capture live courtroom dictation at speeds up to 225 words per minute at 95% or better accuracy [S1]. Harriet alone decides who is admitted, and she alone answers for the decision if that reputation ever slips.

Applications arrive by the hundreds every year. AFCR narrows the pool with four short-term memory tests and one typing-speed test (see Appendix A). This year, twenty-seven applicants made it to Harriet's desk, and their results are recorded in Appendix B. Only three seats remain open.

Two ways of reading that data are on the table.

The Standout Slate admits whichever three applicants post the single highest normalized score in any one of the five measured categories — rewarding an applicant who is truly exceptional in at least one dimension, even if the rest of their profile is ordinary.

The Balanced Slate admits whichever three applicants post the highest composite score — the average of their normalized performance across all five categories — rewarding an applicant who is strong and consistent everywhere, even if they never post the single best number in any one test.

Because the five categories are measured on entirely different scales — four memory tests scored out of however many words an applicant recalls, and typing speed measured in words per minute — neither ranking can be built from the raw scores directly. Appendix C states exactly how this case converts every raw score onto a shared 0–100 scale, and exactly what it assumes about weighting the five categories. Read it before ranking anyone — a different normalization or weighting choice can change who lands in either slate.

Every applicant's age is also listed in Appendix B, alongside their scores. Neither slate under consideration uses it.

Harriet has one day to send Paul her three names.

Appendices

Appendix A — Test Descriptions

AFCR administers five tests to every finalist applicant.

Four short-term memory tests, each scored on how many items an applicant correctly recalls:

  • Test 1 — Text Only. Recall a list of twenty words shown only as on-screen text.
  • Test 2 — Text + Audio (×3). Recall a list of twenty words shown as text, with each word also read aloud three times.
  • Test 3 — Audio + Text (font adjustments). Recall a list of twenty words shown as text with visual/font changes, each word read aloud once.
  • Test 4 — Audio + Visual. Recall a list of twenty objects shown as images, with each object's name spoken aloud once.

Typing Speed is measured in words per minute (WPM), counted only at 98% accuracy or higher.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Normalize one raw score (D1)

    Pick any applicant and any category. Convert their raw score to a percent of that category's pool max (Appendix B's bottom row) — Appendix C's MA1. Round to one decimal place.

    Step 2 — Single-best and composite score for one applicant (D2)

    Using the same applicant (or a different one), compute their single-best normalized score (the highest of their five category percentages) and their composite score (the mean of the five, per Appendix C's MA2 equal weighting). Drag the slider to explore MA2's sensitivity — what happens to the composite if typing counted for more or less than an equal fifth?
    1

    Tolerance is ±0.2 — small rounding differences are fine.

    Step 3 — Roster composite average (D3, D4)

    Pick any three applicants — your own proposed slate, or one you're testing — and compute the average of their three composite scores (equal weighting, MA2 default). This calculator won't tell you if your three names are “the” right slate; it only checks your arithmetic once you've decided who to compare. Use it to build and compare as many candidate rosters as you want for D3, and to pull the numbers for your D4 bar graph.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    “Harriet's phone rings. It's Paul. 'Before you send the list — Sophia just called. She's withdrawing. She accepted a full scholarship at a rival transcription program an hour ago.' Sophia — the applicant nearly every Hero in this room ranked at or near the top of both lists — is gone. You have five minutes. Recompute both slates without her.”

    One thing to decide first: should the category pool maxes (Appendix B's bottom row) be recomputed now that Sophia is out of the pool, or should they stay fixed at their original values? Appendix C's MA1 states the basis is fixed once, from the full original field, and is not recomputed when an applicant later withdraws — but a Hero is free to argue that convention should change now that the person who was setting several of those maxes is gone.
