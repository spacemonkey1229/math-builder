## Background

Priya Chandrasekaran is a zoning analyst in a mid-sized city's Department of City Planning. A **zoning analyst** reviews whether a proposed development follows the city's zoning code and writes the department's official recommendation on what should be allowed; a community board is a real, local advisory body of residents that a city's planning department reports to before a project moves forward. Priya's recommendation memo becomes the department's official position at Thursday's meeting, and the board will hold her to whatever number she puts in it. Every parcel in the city belongs to a **zoning lot** — the legal unit of land zoning rules are applied to, which can be one parcel or several combined — and every zoning lot sits in a **zoning district**, a designated area (like this case's R7-2) with its own rulebook for how much can be built and how it must be arranged on the land.

The assemblage in question is three adjoining parcels on a block shaped, in part, by a diagonal avenue that predates the city's numbered street grid — a real and common pattern in gridded cities, where an older diagonal road cuts through the rectangular blocks laid out later and leaves oddly angled, irregular parcels behind at every crossing (SEE APPENDIX A). All three parcels sit in an R7-2 residence district — in real NYC zoning, the letter and number (R7) name a whole family of similar residential districts allowing roughly the same building bulk, and the suffix (-2) picks out one specific variant within that family with its own exact FAR and yard numbers; this case only ever needs R7-2's own two numbers, not the wider naming scheme — a real zoning designation that sets two rules load-bearing to this case (SEE APPENDIX B): a **floor area ratio (FAR)** — the maximum total interior floor area a building may contain, as a multiple of the lot's own land area, so a building can legally contain more square feet of floor than the lot has square feet of land by building upward across multiple stories — and a **required yard**: a strip of open land, measured a fixed depth in from a lot line, that a building is not allowed to occupy, so light and air reach the building and its neighbors. Concretely, a building's total floor area equals its footprint (the part of the lot it's actually allowed to build on) multiplied by the FAR; dividing that total floor area by the size of one typical apartment then gives the number of apartments the building could hold — that chain (footprint → floor area → apartment count) is exactly what Challenge 3's D4 asks you to run for each option.

Priya has exactly two development paths available, and department policy requires she recommend one before Thursday. **Plat As-Is** keeps the three existing irregular parcels exactly as **platted** — "platted" means the parcel's legal boundary lines are the ones already on file with the city, so no property lines change and the standard permit review — the ordinary process where the city checks a building application against the zoning rules already on the books before issuing a construction permit, with no separate ownership filing required first — can begin immediately. But each odd-shaped parcel loses a different amount of its own area to its required yard, because the required yard runs the full length of a parcel's rear lot line — and a parcel with an unusual shape, especially a **concave** one with a notch cut into it, has more rear-lot-line length per square foot of area than a compact, regular shape does, so more of it falls inside the required-yard strip and cannot be built on (SEE APPENDIX C). **Replat & Consolidate** merges the three parcels into a single, larger rectangular zoning lot before anything can be built. Merging requires filing a **zoning lot merger** — a real, formal city process, filed with the planning department as a recorded legal document (the way a property deed is recorded), in which every current owner of the parcels being combined must sign an agreement — which adds real months to the timeline before construction can even start. But a single larger regular lot has proportionally less rear-lot-line length, so less of its area falls inside the required yard, and the design includes a public plaza that earns the project a real, city-recognized **floor-area bonus** — extra buildable floor area a city grants in exchange for the developer providing public open space (SEE APPENDIX D).

Department policy doesn't just want the bigger eventual apartment count, though — it wants to know how much housing each option actually delivers within the city's stated planning window. So the primary number Priya's memo has to report is each option's **dwelling-unit-years**: the number of apartments an option could eventually hold, multiplied by however many years remain in the planning horizon once that option's own lead time to construction start is subtracted. A design that holds more apartments but starts months later doesn't automatically win — it only wins if those extra apartments are worth more, over the years they're actually available for people to live in, than the head start the other option gets from starting sooner.

For this case, treat every lot line that is not on the block's front street as a rear lot line requiring the full yard depth — a simplification of the real, more detailed rules that classify a parcel's lot lines, disclosed here rather than hidden (SEE APPENDIX F). Every figure in this case is either cited to a real source (SEE APPENDIX G) or disclosed as a stated Modeling Assumption (SEE APPENDIX F). The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in Appendix H.

## Appendix A — Why This Block Is Shaped Like This

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D5). Many gridded American cities have at least one older, diagonal road that predates the rectangular street grid laid over it later. Where that diagonal crosses the grid, it slices ordinary rectangular blocks into smaller, irregular pieces — triangles, trapezoids, and odd-angled parcels that were never intentionally designed, just left over. In Manhattan, this exact pattern — a pre-grid diagonal road (Broadway) crossing the 1811 street grid — produced some of the city's most famous public spaces (Union Square, Madison Square, Herald Square, Times Square, Columbus Circle), all originally just the geometric leftovers where the angle met the grid, not planned civic space [S1]. This case's assemblage is a constructed scenario built on that same real pattern, not a specific real address.

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D1, D2). All dimensions are for this case's constructed scenario (SEE APPENDIX F).

| Parcel | Shape | Area (sq ft) | Front lot line (street) | Rear/notch lot-line length requiring a yard | Interior angles (°), in order around the parcel |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | Pentagon (convex) | 11,000 | 80 ft | 40 ft | 90, 90, 141.3, 128.7, **?** (Challenge D1) |
| L2 | Hexagon (**concave** — two notch corners) | 7,900 | 60 ft | 80 + 60 + 64 = 204 ft | 90, 77.5, 192.5, 90, 51.3, 218.7 |
| L3 | Pentagon (convex) | 11,100 | 60 ft | 80 ft | 102.5, 90, 90, 90, 167.5 |

**How to read the "rear/notch lot-line length" column:** for L1 and L3, this is the length of the single lot line farthest from the front street. For L2 — the concave parcel — this case treats the required yard as running along *three* lot lines: the one farthest from the front street, plus the two lot lines that form the inward notch, because a concave (re-entrant) lot line is treated the same way a real rear lot line is for the purposes of this assignment's required yard (a simplification of the real, more detailed "inner court" open-space provisions many zoning codes apply to re-entrant building and lot shapes — SEE APPENDIX F). Working out each parcel's required-yard area is a simple multiplication, not a geometric overlay: multiply each qualifying lot-line length by the 30 ft yard depth (Appendix B) and add those products together — you do not need to reason about whether the resulting yard strips physically overlap at a corner; this case's model doesn't require that level of geometric precision (SEE APPENDIX F, MA2).

**What L2 actually looks like, in words, for anyone working from the table alone:** start at the left end of L2's own 60 ft front lot line and walk the boundary clockwise. The first side runs back from the street; then, partway back, the boundary suddenly turns *inward* toward the center of the parcel instead of continuing outward — that inward turn is the first notch corner (a reflex angle, meaning the parcel's interior bends around it by more than a straight line, more than 180°). The boundary then runs across to a second point deeper inside the parcel's own footprint, turns again — the second notch corner — and continues back out to the rear before closing the shape. The net effect is a six-sided lot shaped roughly like a wide "V" bitten out of one side of an otherwise ordinary lot: two of its six corners point in toward the parcel instead of out away from it, which is what "concave" means for a shape and is also exactly why its total rear/notch lot-line length (204 ft, split across three separate boundary segments: 80 + 60 + 64) is so long relative to its 7,900 sq ft of area.
