Overview

Priya Chandrasekaran has the survey map spread across two desks because it won't fit on one.

The assemblage — three parcels the city has been trying to develop for a decade — sits exactly where it always has: on the wedge-shaped block where the old diagonal avenue slices through the numbered grid, the way it has for two hundred years. Nobody drew these lot lines on purpose. They're what was left over after the angle cut through.

The community board meeting is in nine days. Her director wants one recommendation, not a menu. Priya has spent the morning with a protractor and a calculator, and the honest truth is that the map on the left desk and the map on the right desk both work — they just don't agree with each other at all.

Her phone buzzes. It's the director: "Which one are we bringing Thursday?"

Priya doesn't answer yet. She picks up the protractor again.

Background

Priya Chandrasekaran works as a zoning analyst for a mid-sized city's Department of City Planning — her actual day job is reading a proposed development against the city's zoning code line by line and writing the department's official recommendation on what should be allowed. That recommendation goes into a recommendation memo: a short, formal written document that becomes the department's real position once it's filed, the same way a lawyer's brief or a doctor's referral letter is the official record of a professional judgment, not just an opinion tossed out in conversation. On Thursday, Priya's memo is the department's position — nobody softens it afterward.

That memo goes to a community board: a real, local advisory body made up of residents from the neighborhood, appointed (not elected) to review development proposals and weigh in before a project moves forward. A city's planning department doesn't have to do whatever a community board says, but it does have to show up, present its numbers, and defend them in front of the board and the public — which is exactly the room this case's Discussion is standing in for.

Every parcel of land in the city belongs to a zoning lotThe legal unit of land zoning rules apply to — it can be one parcel or several merged together. — the legal unit that zoning rules actually get applied to, which can be a single parcel or several parcels merged together — and every zoning lot sits inside a zoning district, a designated area (like this case's R7-2) with its own rulebook for how much can be built and how it has to be arranged on the land. A parcel's boundary lines, once they're on file with the city, are described as platted: "platted" just means the property lines are the official, recorded ones, so no survey or legal filing has to happen before a standard building permit review can start.

The assemblage in this case is three adjoining parcels on a block shaped, in part, by a diagonal avenue that predates the city's numbered street grid — a real and common pattern in gridded cities, where an older diagonal road cuts through rectangular blocks laid out later and leaves oddly angled, irregular parcels behind at every crossing (see Appendix A). All three parcels sit in an R7-2 zoning districtA real New York City residential zoning designation that sets a maximum floor area ratio and required yard depths for that area., a real New York City residential zoning designation that sets two rules load-bearing to this case (see Appendix B): a floor area ratio (FAR)The ratio of a building's total floor area to the size of the land it's built on — a building can legally contain more square feet of floor than the lot has square feet of land by building upward. — the maximum total interior floor area a building may contain, as a multiple of the lot's own land area, so a building can legally hold more square feet of floor than the lot has square feet of land by building upward across multiple stories — and a required yardA strip of open land, measured a fixed depth in from a lot line, that a building may not occupy — required so light and air reach the building and its neighbors.: a strip of open land, measured a fixed depth in from a lot line, that a building is not allowed to occupy, so that light and air reach the building and its neighbors. Concretely, a building's total floor area equals its footprint (the part of the lot it's actually allowed to build on) multiplied by the FAR; dividing that total floor area by the size of one typical apartment then gives the number of apartments the building could hold — that chain (footprint → floor area → apartment count) is exactly what Step 4 of the Working Notebook below asks you to run for each option.

Priya has exactly two development paths available, and department policy requires she recommend one before Thursday. Plat As-Is keeps the three existing irregular parcels exactly as platted — no property lines change, so the standard permit review can begin immediately. But each odd-shaped parcel loses a different amount of its own area to its required yard, because the required yard runs the full length of a parcel's rear lot line — and a parcel with an unusual shape, especially a concave one with a notch cut into it, has more rear-lot-line length per square foot of area than a compact, regular shape does, so more of it falls inside the required-yard strip and cannot be built on (see Appendix C). Replat & Consolidate merges the three parcels into a single, larger rectangular zoning lot before anything can be built. Merging requires filing a zoning lot mergerA real, formal city filing, requiring every current owner's signature, that combines separate parcels into one legal zoning lot. — a real, formal city process, filed with the planning department as a recorded legal document (the way a property deed is recorded), in which every current owner of the parcels being combined must sign an agreement — which adds real months to the timeline before construction can even start. But a single larger regular lot has proportionally less rear-lot-line length, so less of its area falls inside the required yard, and the design includes a public plaza that earns the project a real, city-recognized floor-area bonusA city-recognized zoning benefit: a developer who provides a public plaza of at least a minimum size earns extra buildable floor area in exchange. — extra buildable floor area a city grants in exchange for the developer providing public open space (see Appendix D).

Department policy doesn't just want the bigger eventual apartment count, though — it wants to know how much housing each option actually delivers within the city's stated planning window. So the primary number Priya's memo has to report is each option's dwelling-unit-years: the number of apartments an option could eventually hold, multiplied by however many years remain in the planning horizon once that option's own lead time to construction start is subtracted. A design that holds more apartments but starts months later doesn't automatically win — it only wins if those extra apartments are worth more, over the years they're actually available for people to live in, than the head start the other option gets from starting sooner.

For this case, treat every lot line that is not on the block's front street as a rear lot line requiring the full yard depth — a simplification of the real, more detailed rules that classify a parcel's lot lines, disclosed here rather than hidden (see Appendix F). Every figure in this case is either cited to a real source (see Appendix G) or disclosed as a stated Modeling Assumption (see Appendix F). Appendix A — Why This Block Is Shaped Like This

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D5). Many gridded American cities have at least one older, diagonal road that predates the rectangular street grid laid over it later. Where that diagonal crosses the grid, it slices ordinary rectangular blocks into smaller, irregular pieces — triangles, trapezoids, and odd-angled parcels that were never intentionally designed, just left over. In Manhattan, this exact pattern — a pre-grid diagonal avenueWhere an older road that predates a city's rectangular street grid cuts across it, it leaves behind irregular, oddly-angled parcels — the real pattern this case's assemblage is based on. (Broadway) crossing the 1811 street grid — produced some of the city's most famous public spaces (Union Square, Madison Square, Herald Square, Times Square, Columbus Circle), all originally just the geometric leftovers where the angle met the grid, not planned civic space [S1]. This case's assemblage is a constructed scenario built on that same real pattern, not a specific real address.

How to read the "rear/notch lot-line length" column: for L1 and L3, this is the length of the single lot line farthest from the front street. For L2 — the concave parcel — this case treats the required yard as running along three lot lines: the one farthest from the front street, plus the two lot lines that form the inward notch, because a concave (re-entrant) lot line is treated the same way a real rear lot line is for the purposes of this assignment's required yard (a simplification of the real, more detailed "inner court" open-space provisions many zoning codes apply to re-entrant building and lot shapes — see Appendix F). Working out each parcel's required-yard area is a simple multiplication, not a geometric overlay: multiply each qualifying lot-line length by the 30 ft yard depth (Appendix B) and add those products together — you do not need to reason about whether the resulting yard strips physically overlap at a corner; this case's model doesn't require that level of geometric precision (see Appendix F, MA2).

What L2 actually looks like, in words (for anyone working from the table alone, before looking at the picture below): start at the left end of L2's own 60 ft front lot line and walk the boundary clockwise. The first side runs back from the street; then, partway back, the boundary suddenly turns inward toward the center of the parcel instead of continuing outward — that's the first notch corner, a reflex angle, meaning the parcel's interior bends around it by more than a straight line (more than 180°). The boundary then runs across to a second point deeper inside the parcel's own footprint, turns again — the second notch corner — and continues back out to the rear before closing the shape. The net effect is a six-sided lot shaped roughly like a wide "V" bitten out of one side of an otherwise ordinary lot: two of its six corners point in toward the parcel instead of out away from it, which is what "concave" means for a shape, and is also exactly why its total rear/notch lot-line length (204 ft, split across three separate boundary segments: 80 + 60 + 64) is so long relative to its 7,900 sq ft of area.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Classify the parcels and find L1's missing angle

    Using Appendix C: which parcel is concave? Then use the sum-of-interior-angles theorem ((n−2)×180°) on Parcel L1 (a pentagon: n = 5) to find its fifth interior angle from the four given: 90, 90, 141.3, 128.7.

    Step 2 — Buildable footprint, Option A (Plat As-Is)

    Using each parcel's area, its rear/notch lot-line length, and the 30 ft required yard depth (Appendix B), find each parcel's required-yard area and buildable footprint, then the combined total and percentage.

    Step 3 — Buildable footprint and plaza area, Option B (Replat & Consolidate)

    Using the consolidated lot's 200 × 150 ft dimensions and its 200 ft rear lot line (Appendix D), find the buildable footprint and percentage. Then use the regular-polygon area formula (½ × perimeter × apothem) on the octagon plaza.

    Step 4 — Dwelling-unit-years comparison
