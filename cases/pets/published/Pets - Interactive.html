Overview

“Toby, can you please pass the spatula?” Chloe asks, reaching for a second helping of their mom’s lasagna. She sets the pan down and glances at her brother across the table.

“So, Mom,” Chloe says, careful with the words, like she’s rehearsed them. “Toby and I worked really hard on our apprenticeships this summer. We think we’ve shown we’re responsible. You know how we’ve talked about getting a pet?”

Toby jumps in before she can finish. “We’d pay for everything ourselves. With what we made this summer.”

Their mom sets down her fork. She’s quiet for a second — the kind of quiet that means she’s actually thinking about it, not the kind that means no.

“You two have shown a lot of maturity,” she says finally. “I’d be willing to support that. But pets are expensive, and they’re a long-term commitment. What kind are you two thinking?”

Chloe and Toby glance at each other. “We’re not totally sure yet,” Chloe admits.

“Come up with a real plan,” their mom says, “and pitch it to me next week. Numbers and all. Then we’ll talk.”

Toby is already pulling out his phone, opening a blank note. Chloe grabs a napkin and starts writing dollar signs down the side of it before the lasagna’s even cold.

Background

Toby (12) and Chloe (13) Thompson are the youngest of six children; their four older siblings have moved away for work and college. Their mom is a widow of 8 years who works from home as a teacher for the online school Williamstown Academy. The family lives in a 4-bedroom, 1,955-square-foot home on a ¼-acre lot in the suburbs of Redmond, Washington, and travels about three times a year, for no more than a week at a time, to visit family.

Years ago the Thompsons owned a goldendoodle named Tikky Tikky Tembo. She was hit by a car and died nearly 8 years ago, and the loss — combined with the demands of a household of seven — kept the family from taking on another pet since. Toby and Chloe have wanted a real pet for years; they’ve “raised” stuffed animals since early childhood and would rather start with a young animal than an older one.

This summer, Toby and Chloe completed apprenticeships and together earned $1,620. Chloe’s apprenticeship turned into ongoing part-time work paying $140–$180 per month.

Their mom has told them she will support the pet only if they cover its costs from that money — and she has one non-negotiable condition of her own: whichever pet they choose, they must also carry pet insurance for it. That is not optional, and it is not up for pitch (see Appendix D).

Chloe’s favorite cat breed is a Scottish Fold. Toby’s favorite dog breed is a Beagle. (A neighbor’s Rainbow Lorikeet, which the two have helped board once or twice a year, came up too — but neither Toby nor Chloe has ever said it’s a pet they’d choose for themselves, only one they enjoy visiting, so it never became a real option for this pitch.) Both of them want a young animal — a puppy or a kitten — not an adult rescue.

Because a dog and a cat do not, on average, live the same number of years, “which one costs more, total” is not a fair question by itself — a Beagle that lives three years longer than a Scottish Fold will always rack up a bigger lifetime number, even if it costs about the same per year it’s alive. Whatever pitch Toby and Chloe bring to their mom has to account for that (see Appendices A, B, and C). Every dollar figure in this case that isn’t a direct citation is a disclosed, stated Modeling Assumption (see Appendix E) — none is invented quietly. The vocabulary and “how to…” skills this case will ask you to research are listed in Appendix F.

Challenges

  1. Research and find an online resource that answers one of the "how to…" questions from Appendix F. Post a link to the resource as your submission.
  2. Post 250+ "excellent" words pitching a firm stance on which order you'd choose and why. Your pitch must use the resource from Challenge 1 and include at least one original visual display of the data.
  3. Your pitch must include:
    • D1. A Day-1 startup cost table for your chosen pet — purchase price plus every one-time item — with a labeled total, and a stated comparison against the Thompsons’ $1,620.
    • D2. A Year-1 total cost calculation (Day-1 total plus 12 months of ongoing costs), computed both with and without Mom’s required pet insurance.
    • D3. A full-lifetime total cost calculation for your chosen pet, using its real expected lifespan, with insurance included every year.
    • D4. A cost-per-year-of-expected-lifespan calculation for BOTH pets, dividing each pet’s lifetime total by its own expected lifespan, explaining why this is a fairer comparison than the raw lifetime totals.
    • D5. Two original visual displays: a bar graph comparing both options’ cost-per-year-of-life figures, and a pie chart breaking your chosen pet’s lifetime total down by category.
    • D6. A one-paragraph recommendation on Mom’s insurance requirement, stating what percent of your chosen pet’s Year-1 total the insurance premium adds.
  4. An appendix showing evidence of your original thinking — pictures, screenshots, notes, work. Doesn't count toward your word count.
  5. Participate twice or more in the case study discussion. If absent, post 500 "excellent" words on the questions above instead.

Appendix C — Why the Two Pets Don’t Live the Same Number of Years

A Beagle’s average lifespan is commonly reported at 10–15 years (most sources cluster around 12–15) [S2]. A Scottish Fold’s average lifespan is different from what you might expect: a healthy Scottish Fold can live 13–16 years, but the breed carries a genetic condition (a cartilage disorder linked to the same gene that causes its trademark folded ears) that shortens the breed-wide average to about 10 years [S5]. This is real, documented, and not something either pet’s breeder would put in an ad.

This matters directly for D4: comparing the two pets’ total lifetime cost side by side is not the same question as comparing what a year of each pet actually costs.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Setup — pick which pet you're calculating

    Use Appendix E's stated values (MA1–MA10) for your inputs below — those are the single numbers this case uses, not the full cited ranges in Appendices A/B/D.

    Step 1 — Day-1 startup cost (D1)

    Purchase price + one-time setup cost = Day-1 total. Then compare it to the Thompsons' $1,620.

    Step 2 — Year-1 total cost (D2)

    Day-1 total + 12 months of ongoing costs. Compute it twice: with insurance, and without.

    Step 3 — Lifetime total and cost-per-year, BOTH pets (D3/D4)

    Lifetime total = Day-1 + (annual total with insurance × expected lifespan). Cost-per-year = lifetime total ÷ lifespan. Do this for BOTH options, not just the one you're pitching.

    In your write-up, explain in your own words why comparing the two per-year figures is a fairer question than comparing the two raw lifetime totals.

    Step 4 — Insurance as a percent of Year-1 (D6)

    What percent of your chosen pet's Year-1 total does the insurance premium add? (insurance ÷ Year-1-with-insurance × 100)

    Step 5 — Your two visual displays (D5)

    This notebook doesn't draw your charts for you — build them in the tool of your choice (Sheets, graph paper, etc.) using the numbers you just calculated above.
    • A bar graph comparing Option A's and Option B's cost-per-year-of-life figures (Step 3).
    • A pie chart breaking your chosen pet's lifetime total down by category: purchase price, one-time supplies, food/grooming/vet (ongoing), insurance.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    Everyone’s been comparing total lifetime dollars. But here’s something neither pet’s breeder puts in an ad: a Scottish Fold’s folded ears come from a real genetic mutation — the same mutation also affects cartilage all over its body. It’s a documented health condition, not folklore, and it’s a real reason the breed’s average lifespan runs shorter than a healthy individual’s best case. A Beagle is expected to live about 13 years. A Scottish Fold, on average, about 10 (see Appendix C).

    Go back to your Step 3 numbers. Does the per-year comparison — not the raw lifetime totals — change your recommendation, or not? And a harder question: is a lower cost-per-year a genuinely good thing here, when the reason it's lower is a shorter expected life caused by a real health condition?
