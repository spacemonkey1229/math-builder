Overview

Dana Okafor doesn't look up from the plotting table when the phone rings a second time. The sector command center hums with fluorescent light and the low static of a marine-band scannerA radio receiver tuned to the VHF channels ships, the Coast Guard, and other mariners use to talk to each other and call for help — it's always on in a command center, playing in the background., and taped to the corner of Dana's monitor is a photo of a niece who FaceTimes every Sunday — Dana already texted home to say tonight's shift is running long. On the whiteboard behind Dana, someone chalked the case number, and under it, in block capitals nobody has erased since the watch changed overA "watch" is a work shift on a ship or at a command center; crews work in rotating shifts around the clock, and "the watch changed over" just means the previous shift handed off to the next one.: PERSON IN WATER.

The helicopter crew chief's voice comes through the headset clipped and flat: fuel state, daylight remaining, one search box, not two. Somewhere past the harbor entrance a family is standing on a dock refreshing a phone with no new messages. Dana pulls two transparencies off the light table — one gridded box traced in blue grease pencil, one in red — and lays them side by side over the chart. Both came off the same radar fix. Both used a real, published drift model. They do not agree.

Dana's finger hovers between them. Behind Dana, the watch officer leans in, waiting on the word that sends a helicopter one way and not the other, and there will not be time, after this decision, to be told it was the wrong one.

Background

Dana Okafor is the on-duty Search and Rescue Mission Coordinator (SMC)The person at a Coast Guard command center responsible for planning a search pattern and committing rescue assets to it — the role that decides where a helicopter or boat actually goes. at a U.S. Coast Guard sector command center, responsible for planning search patterns and accountable to the sector commander for making the best use of every asset committed to a case, including the fuel and flight hours of any aircraft launched (Appendix A). Some time ago, a fishing vessel radioed that a crew member had gone overboard; at the moment of that report, the vessel's own radar recorded the person's position as a bearingThe compass direction from one point to another, measured in degrees clockwise from true north (so 065° true means a little east of northeast) — the angle half of a bearing-and-range fix. and range measured from the vessel (Appendix B) — the only fix anyone has.

Since that last fix, a helicopter crew has been standing by, and it now has enough fuel and remaining daylight to fully search exactly one candidate search box before it must return to base (Appendix A). Coast Guard search planning projects how far, and in what direction, the person (and the personal flotation device (PFD)A life jacket or vest that keeps a person floating and upright in the water — it changes how, and how fast, a person drifts. they were wearing) has likely drifted since the last fix, using published leewayHow fast, and at what angle off the wind, a floating object is pushed by wind alone — a real, measured Coast Guard drift-modeling quantity, different from current-driven drift. drift-rate data for that specific category of object in the water (Appendices C and D). Two such projections are on the table tonight, built from the same starting fix, and they do not agree:

The Windward Datum — the search box generated by the wind-driven leeway drift model, which projects drift using the object's published leeway coefficients: how fast, and at what angle off the wind, this category of object is carried by wind alone (Appendix C).

The Set-and-Drift Datum — the search box generated by the current-driven drift model, which instead projects drift using the surface current's own speed and direction at the scene — what mariners call the current's "set and drift"Nautical shorthand for a current's direction (the "set") and speed (the "drift") — the current-driven counterpart to wind-driven leeway. (Appendix D).

Both models start from the same converted radar fix (Appendix B) and the same elapsed search time (Appendix A); both are real, Coast Guard-published methods; and both are defensible for this category of object. They are not, however, the same box, and the helicopter's remaining fuel and daylight rule out searching both fully (Appendix A). Dana's recommendation will be judged on one number: the estimated probability of detection each candidate box offers for every flight-hour the helicopter has left (Appendix E). Every sourced and assumed figure in this case is listed in Appendices F and G, and the vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in Appendix H — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which datum to recommend; that part is still yours.

    D1 — Rectangular-datum conversion

    Convert the vessel's bearing/range fix (Appendix B: 065° true, 0.60 NM from the vessel) into an (east, north) offset from the vessel, then add it to the vessel's own position (10.0, 6.0) to get the radar-fix datum.

    D2 — Parametric drift models

    For each option, write the drift velocity's east/north components (the coefficients of t in x(t) and y(t)), using that model's own stated drift speed and direction.

    D3 — Eliminated-parameter search boxes

    Eliminate t (using Appendix A's elapsed time, t = 2.5 hr) to find each option's drifted datum, its search radius R = 1.1 × E(t), and the resulting box's area (a square of side 2R centered on the datum).

    D4 — Graphed polar boundary

    Confirm the polar radius r(θ) you'd graph for each option's outer search boundary matches the R you found in D3 (a circle of that radius around the drifted datum). In your write-up, say in words why the real total-probable-error boundary is not necessarily uniform by bearing, even though this case models it as a circle.

    D5 — Probability-of-detection decision table

    Using Appendix A's flight-hours, your D3 areas, and Appendix E's coverage-factor/POC data, find each option's coverage factor, POD, and POD per flight-hour.
