Case Study: The Search Box

## Overview

Dana Okafor doesn't look up from the plotting table when the phone rings a second time. The
sector command center hums with fluorescent light and the low static of a marine-band scanner,
and taped to the corner of Dana's monitor is a photo of a niece who FaceTimes every Sunday — Dana
already texted home to say tonight's shift is running long. On the whiteboard behind Dana, someone
chalked the case number, and under it, in block capitals nobody has erased since the watch
changed over: PERSON IN WATER.

The helicopter crew chief's voice comes through the headset clipped and flat: fuel state,
daylight remaining, one search box, not two. Somewhere past the harbor entrance a family is
standing on a dock refreshing a phone with no new messages. Dana pulls two transparencies off the
light table — one gridded box traced in blue grease pencil, one in red — and lays them side by
side over the chart. Both came off the same radar fix. Both used a real, published drift model.
They do not agree.

Dana's finger hovers between them. Behind Dana, the watch officer leans in, waiting on the word
that sends a helicopter one way and not the other, and there will not be time, after this
decision, to be told it was the wrong one.

## Background

Dana Okafor is the on-duty Search and Rescue Mission Coordinator (SMC) at a U.S. Coast Guard
sector command center, responsible for planning search patterns and accountable to the sector
commander for making the best use of every asset committed to a case, including the fuel and
flight hours of any aircraft launched (SEE APPENDIX A). Some time ago, a fishing vessel radioed
that a crew member had gone overboard; at the moment of that report, the vessel's own radar
recorded the person's position as a bearing and range measured from the vessel (SEE APPENDIX B) —
the only fix anyone has.

Since that last fix, a helicopter crew has been standing by, and it now has enough fuel and
remaining daylight to fully search exactly one candidate search box before it must return to base
(SEE APPENDIX A). Coast Guard search planning projects how far, and in what direction, the person
(and the personal flotation device they were wearing) has likely drifted since the last fix, using
published drift-rate data for that specific category of object in the water (SEE APPENDICES C and
D). Two such projections are on the table tonight, built from the same starting fix, and they do
not agree:

**The Windward Datum** — the search box generated by the wind-driven leeway drift model, which
projects drift using the object's published leeway coefficients: how fast, and at what angle off
the wind, this category of object is carried by wind alone (SEE APPENDIX C).

**The Set-and-Drift Datum** — the search box generated by the current-driven drift model, which
instead projects drift using the surface current's own speed and direction at the scene — what
mariners call the current's "set and drift" (SEE APPENDIX D).

Both models start from the same converted radar fix (SEE APPENDIX B) and the same elapsed search
time (SEE APPENDIX A); both are real, Coast Guard-published methods; and both are defensible for
this category of object. They are not, however, the same box, and the helicopter's remaining fuel
and daylight rule out searching both fully (SEE APPENDIX A). Dana's recommendation will be judged
on one number: the estimated probability of detection each candidate box offers for every
flight-hour the helicopter has left (SEE APPENDIX E). Every sourced and assumed figure in this
case is listed in **APPENDICES F and G**, and the vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will
ask of you are listed in **APPENDIX H** — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

## Dilemma

You are Dana Okafor.
Do you:
A.) Send the helicopter to the Windward Datum — the search box generated by the wind-driven
leeway drift model OR
B.) Send it to the Set-and-Drift Datum — the search box generated by the current-driven drift
model

**Supplemental — for context.** This case's methodology (leeway drift modeling combined with a
Monte-Carlo/optimal-search-factor search plan) is the same real methodology the Coast Guard used
in the well-documented, publicly reported 2013 rescue of Long Island lobsterman John Aldridge, who
survived roughly 11–12 hours in open water after falling overboard without a life jacket and was
found and hoisted to safety by a Coast Guard helicopter after a SAROPS-guided search: ["Lost at sea:
A harrowing story of survival," CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/lost-at-sea-a-harrowing-story-of-survival/)
(accessed 2026-08-30). This is offered as evidence that both candidate models in this case rest on
methodology that has found people, in keeping with this case's framing around the successful-rescue
decision rather than peril (SEE FACILITATOR.md).
