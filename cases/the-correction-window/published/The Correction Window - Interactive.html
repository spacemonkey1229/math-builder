Overview

The rock walls fill both edges of Mara Yun's control-tablet feed, close enough that the drone's downwash kicks up a fine dust off the sandstone. Her headset crackles: base camp confirming the light is going, and that this is the only pass the battery budget allows before the crew has to fall back for the night. Somewhere below the canyon rim, a thermal camera is sweeping side to side, hunting for the one heat signature that matters — Dana Osei, the trail counter who didn't check in after yesterday's rockslide warning, and who has been out in dropping temperatures for going on two days now.

Mara built this drone's flight code herself, on a folding table at the trailhead, because the stock firmware couldn't hold a tight enough line between these walls. Her laptop still has both candidate builds open in side-by-side terminal windows, each one already compiled, already flashedLoading a compiled program onto a microcontroller's permanent memory, so it runs that exact code every time the board powers on — the last step between writing flight code on a laptop and a drone actually flying it.Open a source on this → to a spare flight computer — the same kind of onboard board described in Appendix A, just a spare one sitting on the bench rather than the one already mounted in the drone — both tested that morning. They fly almost identically in calm air. It's the gusts coming off these specific walls — the ones the canyon is actually known for — that make the two builds behave differently, and Mara has maybe ninety seconds of usable light left to pick one and launch.

A gust rattles the drone on the monitor. Her thumb hovers between two identical-looking USB drives, each labeled only with a build number in her own handwriting — each drive holds one candidate build's compiled code, ready to load onto that spare flight computer for the one pass she'll actually fly.

Background

Mara Yun is a volunteer search-and-rescue drone operator and the team's only member who also writes flight-control firmware. She is accountable for two things at once on this mission: keeping the drone flying a safe, steady line down the canyon, and keeping its thermal camera pointed usefully at the walls and floor long enough to find Dana. Both jobs depend on the same flight-control loop, running on the drone's onboard flight computer (see Appendix A). A drone holds its distance from a wall the same way a cyclist leans to stay upright on a turn: to drift a little closer to the wall or a little farther away, it briefly tilts sideways, which points part of its rotor thrust sideways instead of straight down; leveling back out stops that sideways drift once the offset is right. So "correcting the drone's tilt" and "holding a fixed sideways offset from the canyon's rock wall (see Appendix B)" describe the same physical motion, dozens of times a second, without drifting into the wall or wandering out into open air where the camera's angle stops covering useful ground.

That correction loop needs to know, every single cycle, how much sideways thrust to command for the drone's current tilt angle. A rotor's thrust always points straight out of the drone's own "up" direction; tilt that direction sideways by an angle θ and simple trigonometry splits the same total thrust into two parts — a piece still holding the drone up, and a sideways piece equal to the total thrust multiplied by sin(θ). That is the whole reason this case is about approximating sin(θ) specifically, and not some other function: it is the exact multiplier the correction loop needs, every cycle, to convert "how far the drone is tilted" into "how much sideways push that tilt is actually producing." A full, textbook-accurate sine function call is too slow to fit inside the loop's strict per-cycle time budget on this flight computer (see Appendix A) — profiling on the bench showed it alone eating a share of the budget that left too little margin for the thermal camera's own processing, which runs on the same chip. (The chip's 168 MHz clock speed and its 8 kHz correction-loop rate, both in Appendix A, are two different numbers on purpose: the loop only needs to correct the drone's tilt 8,000 times a second, but each one of those corrections still has to fit inside a shrinking slice of the chip's much faster 168-million-tick-per-second clock, shared with everything else the chip is doing.) Mara's fix, in both builds, is to replace the full sine calculation with a Taylor polynomial approximation of sin(θ) — a short, fixed sequence of multiplications and additions that a flight computer can finish fast enough, at the cost of not being perfectly accurate at every possible tilt angle.

The two builds differ only in how many terms of that polynomial they keep (see Appendix C). Build 3 truncates after the cubic term. Build 5 keeps one more term. Every extra term buys more accuracy across a wider range of tilt angles — polynomials of this general algebraic form, and how many terms it actually takes to get there, is exactly what Challenge 1's "how to…" research (see Appendix F) and Appendix D task Mara's pitch with deriving — but every extra term also costs more of the flight computer's strictly limited per-cycle time (see Appendix A), time that competes directly with the thermal camera sweep that is the entire reason this flight exists. The canyon's own gusts, and how often they push the drone to a large tilt angle versus a small one, are laid out in Appendix E, along with the correction-error tolerance the team is willing to accept given how close the offset in Appendix B already is. All figures needing a citation or a stated assumption are marked and resolved in Appendix G. Mara has to pick the one build that gets the drone through this canyon and gives the camera its best real chance at finding Dana before dark — not the one that merely looks more mathematically precise on paper.

Appendix D — Taylor Series Reference

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D1).

The general of a function f(θ) about θ = a is:

f(θ) = f(a) + f′(a)(θ − a) + f″(a)(θ − a)² ⁄ 2! + f‴(a)(θ − a)³ ⁄ 3! + f(4)(a)(θ − a)4 ⁄ 4! + f(5)(a)(θ − a)5 ⁄ 5! + …

For this case, a = 0 (a Maclaurin series), and f(θ) = sin(θ). Challenge 3 (D1) asks you to compute f(0), f′(0), f″(0), f‴(0), f(4)(0), and f(5)(0) yourself, using the fact that each derivative of sin(θ) cycles through sin(θ), cos(θ), −sin(θ), −cos(θ) and back — and to use those values in the general formula above to build both Appendix C polynomials from scratch.
