Overview

Marcus Alvarez has two browser tabs open, and a phone that keeps lighting up on the desk beside his keyboard.

The first tab holds Meridian Trust & Custody's real fee ladder — the actual schedule that decides what a client pays, rung by rung, each rung computed off whatever balance survived the rung before it. Marcus has walked this exact sequence by hand for institutional clients before. Done right, it takes real, unhurried time.

The second tab holds Meridian's own Quick-Quote Reference Card — one flat rate, printed once, never recalculated per client. Marcus could read that single number over the phone and be done before the client on the other end finishes their coffee.

The phone belongs to Cassidy, over in Institutional Relationships, who is on the line with a pension fund's CFO right now, promising a number soon. Every few minutes another text lands: *any update?* This client is large enough that today's quote sets the tone for a multi-year custody relationship — and Meridian is not the only bank in the room. A competing custodian is already circling with a number of its own.

Marcus's cursor sits between the two tabs.

Cassidy needs an answer before that call ends, and Marcus has exactly one number to send back.

Background

Marcus Alvarez is a Securities Settlement Operations Analyst on the custody operations team at Meridian Trust & Custody Company, a custody bank that holds and administers institutional clients' securities and charges an annual custody fee for the service. Sales has an inbound institutional client — a pension fund moving its assets under custody (AUC) to Meridian — and needs a defensible fee quote today, before the deal goes to a competing custodian (SEE APPENDIX A).

Meridian's real, internal fee schedule is a fee ladder of four rate tiers. Unlike a standard bracket-style schedule, where each tier's rate applies to a fixed dollar range of the original balance, Meridian's ladder applies each tier's rate to whatever balance is left after the previous tier's own fee has already been subtracted — each tier's charge depends recursively on the tier before it (SEE APPENDIX C). Computing it exactly, tier by tier, with documentation a client's own finance team and Meridian's compliance desk can both check, takes real analyst time Marcus does not have before Cassidy's call ends (SEE APPENDIX A).

Meridian also keeps a Quick-Quote Reference Card: a single flat effective rate, set once per AUC size band by the pricing desk, meant to be read off in seconds when a fast answer is what the sales desk needs (SEE APPENDIX B). It is not recalculated for any specific client's exact balance.

Two options are on Marcus's screen. The Quick-Quote is the flat card rate, applied directly to this client's AUC — fast enough to make Cassidy's deadline, but an approximation of what the real ladder would actually charge. The Full Ladder is the exact recursive tier-by-tier computation — defensible down to the last cent, but not something Marcus can finish before Cassidy needs an answer (SEE APPENDIX A).

Real, publicly disclosed custody fee schedules from other custodian banks confirm that fee rates in this business genuinely are quoted in fractions of a basis point at the scale of a large institutional account, and that they genuinely are structured as multi-tier ladders — Meridian's own ladder's rate magnitudes sit inside that real, disclosed range (SEE APPENDIX E). Getting a custody fee quote wrong is not a hypothetical risk in this industry: a real, documented case exists of a major custody bank paying tens of millions of dollars over a fee-billing dispute with its own clients (SEE APPENDIX F). Full source citations for every figure in this case are in Appendix G, and the full standards bridge for this case is in Appendix H.

The recursive rule: B₀ = the client's starting AUC. For each tier k = 1, 2, 3, 4: this tier's

fee is Feeₖ = rₖ × Bₖ₋₁, and the balance carried into the next tier is Bₖ = Bₖ₋₁ − Feeₖ. The Full

Ladder's total annual fee is B₀ − B₄ (the sum of all four tiers' fees).

*Note: this is what makes the ladder genuinely recursive rather than a standard marginal-bracket

schedule — each tier's rate applies to the balance left over after the previous tier's own fee has

already been taken out, not to a fixed dollar range of the original AUC. Producing this computation

fully documented takes the analyst time stated in Appendix A.*

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Walk the Full Ladder's recursion (D1)

    Appendix C's rule: each tier's fee is charged on the balance remaining after the previous tier's own fee was already subtracted — not a flat sum of rate × total AUC. Starting from Appendix A's client AUC and Appendix C's four tier rates, walk the recursion one tier at a time: fee = rate × current balance; balance = balance − fee. Enter your final total fee.

    Step 2 — Quick-Quote fee, the gap, and scale invariance (D2–D4)

    Compute the Quick-Quote Card's flat-rate fee (rate × AUC), then the absolute dollar gap and the percent gap against your Step 1 recursive fee. Then try a different AUC in Step 1 and recompute here — does the percent gap change?

    Does the percent gap depend on the AUC you entered in Step 1? Try two different AUC values and compare.

    Reveal the Twist

    A real SEC enforcement record: a major custody bank was found to have overcharged clients for years through an undisclosed markup, collecting $170 million in overcharges before it was caught — and had to pay back tens of millions more once it was (Appendix F, S3).

    That case wasn't a quoting shortcut like the Quick-Quote Card — it was undisclosed. But it raises the real question for D5: is a disclosed, bounded, known error acceptable in a way an undisclosed one never can be, even if the dollar gap looks similar on a given day? Revisit your D5 recommendation with that distinction in mind.
