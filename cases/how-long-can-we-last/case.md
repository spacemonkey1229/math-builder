# Case Study: How Long Can We Last

MS | Math | Case Study | 2026-27 | Adventure

---

## Overview

Renata Osei sits cross-legged on the kitchen floor, back against the dishwasher, laptop balanced on her knees because the table is covered in mail. Two browser tabs are open. One is their savings account, balance sitting exactly where it landed after last month's transfer. The other is a brokerage sign-up page she has restarted three times without finishing, because every time she gets to the contribution amount field, she stops.

Julian is on the porch, phone to his ear, half of a conversation drifting through the screen door — something about a project timeline, a client who "might need to push things." Renata doesn't need to hear the other half to know what that tone means. It has meant the same thing every few months since he went independent: work is about to get quiet again for a while.

She pulls up the number they've been saving toward for over a year now — the one a podcast host, a coworker, and a banner ad on three different websites have all told her is the number. She has hit it. According to everything she's read, she's done. She should be moving that brokerage tab from "abandoned draft" to "submitted" tonight.

Instead she opens a blank spreadsheet, types "last time work got quiet, it lasted —" and stops typing, because she genuinely doesn't remember. She minimizes the brokerage tab instead of closing it, pours the last of the coffee, and starts pulling up bank statements from the porch, one slow month at a time, to find out.

## Background

Renata Osei works full-time as a home health aide, paid a steady salary with a predictable deposit on the same day every two weeks. Her husband, Julian, has worked for the past several years as an independent graphic design and marketing consultant — paid by the project, on client timelines he does not control, with no employer, no salary, and no guaranteed minimum (SEE APPENDIX B).

The household's essential monthly expenses — housing, food, utilities, healthcare, transportation, and insurance — total a fixed amount every month regardless of how either paycheck lands (SEE APPENDIX A). Renata's salary alone does not cover that full amount; the household depends on Julian's freelance income to close the gap and, in an ordinary month, to leave something left over.

The couple built a joint emergency fund over the past year specifically to protect against the months Julian's freelance pipeline runs dry — a real, recurring pattern in his business, not a hypothetical one (SEE APPENDIX B). Now that the fund has reached a size that matches "the number" cited by nearly every article, podcast, and banner ad Renata has ever encountered — three months of the household's essential expenses — she and Julian must decide whether that number is actually the right one for their specific situation, or whether it only sounds right because it is the number everyone quotes (SEE APPENDIX C).

Whichever fund size they land on, all money saved beyond it is money the couple has agreed to start investing in a joint brokerage account instead of continuing to add to savings. The two options under debate go by the names Renata and Julian have been using at the kitchen table for weeks: **the Rule-of-Thumb Fund** and **the Real-Stretch Fund**. Neither can be built or verified without doing the underlying math — SEE APPENDIX C for how each is calculated, APPENDIX D for the assumptions behind the household's numbers, and APPENDIX F for where every figure in this case comes from (optional further reading is in APPENDIX E). The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in **Appendix G** — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

## Dilemma

You are Renata Osei.

Do you:

**A.)** Stop adding to savings once the fund reaches **the Rule-of-Thumb Fund** — the widely quoted flat target of three months of the household's essential expenses — and invest everything saved beyond that starting now **OR**

**B.)** Keep adding to savings until the fund reaches **the Real-Stretch Fund** — a target sized specifically to this household's own documented pattern of freelance income dry spells — before investing anything beyond it

## Appendix C: The Dry Spell, and How Each Fund Is Calculated

**The dry spell this case tests against:** based on the household's own multi-year invoicing history, Julian's freelance income has gone to **$0 for 7 consecutive months** during the worst stretch on record [MA2]. Renata's salaried income keeps arriving on schedule throughout — it is Julian's income, not the household's total income, that stops.

**How the Rule-of-Thumb Fund (Option A) is calculated:** the flat, widely quoted rule — save 3 months of the household's total essential expenses (Appendix A), regardless of where the household's income actually comes from.

**How the Real-Stretch Fund (Option B) is calculated:** size the fund to cover the household's actual monthly shortfall during a dry spell — essential expenses minus Renata's income alone, since Julian's income is $0 — for the full 7 months documented above.

Both calculations use a proportion: a fund that provides a fixed number of dollars of coverage per month of protection, scaled up to however many months are needed. Challenge 3's D3 and D4 ask you to carry out both calculations and then test each fund against the 7-month dry spell above.

## Appendix D: Modeling Assumptions

> These figures are **not sourced** from an outside publication. They are stated scenario assumptions chosen by the case author, each with a real basis named below. A Hero may challenge them in discussion — that is a legitimate move.

| # | Assumption | Value used | Basis | Sensitivity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MA1 | Julian's slow-month invoice total, as a percent of his typical month | 21% | This case's own household record (Appendix B), chosen to give Challenge 3's D2 a clean, calculable relationship. No public dataset publishes a "percent of typical month" figure for an individual freelancer's worst month specifically. The general magnitude (a swing near 21%) is informed by JPMorgan Chase Institute's finding that the vast majority of young, bottom-income-quintile, or Western workers experience month-to-month total-income swings exceeding 30% [S4] — establishing that a swing in this broad range is a realistic magnitude for a volatile earner, though this case's specific 21% figure and its "percent of typical" framing are this case's own chosen value, not a direct citation. | Changing this percent changes the *derived* value of Julian's typical monthly income in D2, which does not otherwise appear anywhere else in this case — it plays no role in sizing either fund in D3/D4. |
| MA2 | Length and severity of Julian's documented dry spell | 7 consecutive months at $0 freelance income | No public dataset reports an exact consecutive-month "income fully stops" duration for freelance workers generally. This case's 7-month, complete-stop scenario is informed by two real findings: the Federal Reserve's 2024 household survey, which found 56% of self-employed adults (excluding gig-platform work) report their income varies from month to month, versus 26% of people who work for someone else [S3] — establishing that this kind of variability is a persistent pattern, not a one-off; and the JPMorgan Chase Institute's 2018 Online Platform Economy report, which found 58% of transportation (rideshare-style) platform earners had their platform earnings concentrated in three or fewer months of the year [S5] — indicating that many non-salaried earners go multiple consecutive months with little or no income from a given income source. Neither source reports this exact combination of length and severity for a project-based freelance consultant specifically; the specific 7-month, $0 scenario is this case's own stated choice, disclosed here rather than presented as a direct citation. | This is the single assumption most likely to change which option wins. A shorter or less severe dry spell narrows the gap between the two funds; a longer or more severe one widens it. |
