# Case Study: The Locks Window

MS | Math | Case Study | 2026-27 | Adventure

*(Band: MS-core. This case uses precalculus trigonometry — the Pythagorean identity, inverse
trigonometric functions, and solving a trig equation over a restricted interval — and is written
for a Hero who already knows the sine and cosine functions and is ready to use them to make a
real, timed decision.)*

---

## Overview

Mara Ellison presses her palm flat against the cold glass of the underground viewing room and
doesn't move.

Above her, through a wall of dark water and one thick pane, Chinook are throwing themselves up
the fish ladder's concrete steps, tails cutting silver arcs against the current. Through the
window in front of her, she can just make out their bodies mid-strike — if she can kill the
glare sitting on top of that glass right now, the water column and the fight of the fish are
visible instead of two panes of reflected sky. But raise her eyeline past the window's upper
edge, to where the river runs open above the ladder, and there's the wider shot her director
actually wants: the whole migration, breaking the surface in open water. Same polarizer. Same
rotation ring. One frame.

She's shot glass before. She's shot open water before. She has never had to choose which one
loses.

Her camera assistant crouches beside the tripod, one hand hovering near the polarizer's rotation
ring, waiting on her word. Somewhere above them, a docent's tour group presses against the
railing, narrating the run to a row of strangers who don't know that the two surfaces in front of
Mara's lens don't share a filter setting — not close enough to matter, not far apart enough to
ignore.

Mara puts two fingers on the ring and doesn't turn it yet.

---

## Background

Mara Ellison is the director of photography for a single-day segment of a conservation
documentary tracking the Chinook salmon run through the Ballard Locks fish ladder in Seattle,
filmed as part of a project built around the endangered Southern Resident orca population's
dependence on Chinook as a food source (SEE APPENDIX F). She has full authority over every
camera and lens decision on this shoot day; she does not have authority over the schedule — the
underground-window sequence has to be captured inside the single visit window her production has
booked at the Locks (SEE APPENDIX C), and the day's peak Chinook activity through the ladder does
not wait for a second take.

Her camera is fitted with a single circular polarizing filter (SEE APPENDIX B), rotated to one
fixed angle before she calls "rolling." A polarizing filter nulls specular glare most completely
at one specific angle of incidence for a given reflecting surface — that surface's Brewster angle
(SEE APPENDIX A) — and at every other angle, some fraction of that glare passes back into the
lens. Mara is looking at two reflecting surfaces in the same frame: the underground viewing
room's glass window, and, at a different angle above and behind it, the open river surface where
the migration itself is visible in a wider shot. Glass and water do not share a Brewster angle
(SEE APPENDIX A), and the sun's actual position at the moment she rolls (SEE APPENDIX C) sets the
real angle of incidence striking each surface — which is what determines how close her one
rotation setting comes to nulling each surface's glare.

**The Glass Call.** Rotate the polarizer to null glare on the underground viewing room's glass
window, prioritizing the crisp, glare-free shot of Chinook fighting upstream through the window.

**The River Call.** Rotate the polarizer to null glare on the river surface, prioritizing the
wider establishing shot of the migration above and behind the window.

One filter. One rotation angle. Whichever surface she doesn't prioritize keeps some of its glare
— Malus's Law (SEE APPENDIX D) says how much, and it isn't a straight-line trade (SEE APPENDIX E
for why this particular week's run matters more than most).

Where this case's exact camera position or window geometry can't be pinned to a public floor
plan, that gap is stated openly as a Modeling Assumption (SEE APPENDIX G). Every external source
cited anywhere in this case is collected in Appendix H. The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this
case asks you to research are listed in Appendix I — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

## Appendix D: Malus's Law and the Rotation Model

The intensity of light transmitted through a polarizing filter, as a function of the angle θ
between the filter's transmission axis and the incoming light's plane of polarization, follows
Malus's Law:

$$I = I_0 \cos^2\theta$$

where $I_0$ is the intensity of the fully polarized component of the reflected glare at a given
surface, and $\theta$ is the angular offset between the polarizer's transmission axis and that
surface's own null-glare orientation.

**Why one setting cannot null both surfaces.** Because the glass and the water surface sit at
different Brewster angles (Appendix A), and the polarizer has exactly one rotation angle at any
moment, a setting chosen to null the glass leaves the rotation ring offset from the water
surface's own null point by the angular gap between the two Brewster angles — and vice versa.
Challenge D2 asks you to solve for the exact rotation angle that nulls each surface, over the
interval 0° to 90°.

**The double-angle identity.** Because Malus's Law is a $\cos^2\theta$ relationship rather than a
linear one, the size of that angular gap does not translate directly into a proportional glare
difference. The identity

$$\cos^2\theta = \frac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2}$$

— itself a direct consequence of the Pythagorean identity — is what Challenge D3 asks you to
verify and then use to express each surface's transmitted-glare fraction in an alternate,
checkable form.

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| $I_0$ at the glass window (fraction of incident light reflected as polarized glare at its Brewster angle) | 7.83% [S4, derived] |
| $I_0$ at the river surface | 3.86% [S4, derived] |

*Derivation: at Brewster's angle, only the s-polarized component reflects (the Fresnel p-reflectance is exactly zero there — that is the definition of the angle). For unpolarized sunlight, the fraction of the total incident intensity reflected as glare is therefore half the s-polarized reflectance, $R_s/2$, using the Fresnel formula $R_s = \left|\frac{n_1\cos\theta_i - n_2\cos\theta_t}{n_1\cos\theta_i + n_2\cos\theta_t}\right|^2$ evaluated at each surface's own Brewster angle from Appendix A [S4].*

## Appendix F: The Real Optics, Equipment, and Data Behind This Case

Several real, published facts sit behind this case's dilemma.

**Brewster's angle and the refractive indices of water and glass are real, standard optics
content**, taught in any introductory optics course and published in optics handbooks and
references (SEE APPENDIX H, sources S1–S4).

**The Tiffen 138mm UltraPol Circular Polarizer is a real, currently-sold filter** with a
published manufacturer spec sheet (SEE APPENDIX H, source S5).

**The NOAA Solar Calculator is a real, publicly available tool** that computes deterministic sun
azimuth and elevation for any given coordinates, date, and time (SEE APPENDIX H, sources S6–S7).

**The Ballard Locks Chinook run-count time series is real, publicly collected data**, maintained
by a regional watershed program and by Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife's ongoing Lake
Washington counts (SEE APPENDIX H, sources S9–S10).

**Chinook salmon are a real, documented primary prey source for the Southern Resident orca
population**, and that population is real and formally listed as endangered (SEE APPENDIX H,
source [S11]).

None of these real facts, on its own, produces this case's central mathematical tension — that
comes from combining two close-but-different Brewster angles with the non-linear $\cos^2\theta$
shape of Malus's Law. Mara Ellison and this specific shoot day are a scenario construction built
on real optics, real equipment, and real environmental data (SEE APPENDIX G).
