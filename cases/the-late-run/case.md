# Case Study: The Late Run

MS | Math | Case Study | 2026-27 | Adventure

*(Band: MS-core. This case uses precalculus trigonometry — the Pythagorean identity, inverse
trigonometric functions, and solving a trig equation over a restricted interval — and is written
for a Hero who already knows the sine and cosine functions and is ready to use them to make a
real, timed decision.)*

---

## Overview

Mara Ellison has the production calendar open on one screen and a blank booking form on the
other, and her cursor has been sitting on the date field for four minutes.

The underground viewing room at the Ballard Locks only lets her crew in for one day. Not one day
per run — one day, period, for the whole film. Behind her, a printed still from last year's scout
visit is taped to the wall: the glass, the water column, a single Chinook mid-strike against the
current, glare sitting across the pane like a second, unwanted subject in the frame. She knows
exactly what her polarizer can and can't do about that glare — she's run the math twice already
this morning. What she doesn't know is which day to point the math at.

Her production coordinator is refreshing his own screen down the hall, waiting to lock the crew's
travel and the fish ladder's viewing-room reservation the second she says a date out loud. Two
real windows are open on her calendar: one in the losing light of late summer, one three weeks
later, at the edge of what the season still has left to give her.

She has shot glass before. She knows precisely how clean this filter can get her, on this window,
on any given afternoon — that part isn't the question anymore. The question is which afternoon.

Mara picks up the phone and doesn't dial yet.

---

## Background

Mara Ellison is the director of photography for a single-day segment of a conservation
documentary tracking the salmon runs through the Ballard Locks fish ladder in Seattle, filmed as
part of a project built around the endangered Southern Resident orca population's dependence on
Chinook salmon as a food source (SEE APPENDIX F). She has full authority over every camera and
lens decision on this shoot day; she does not have authority over the calendar — the production
gets exactly one underground-viewing-room shoot day for the entire film, and she has to commit to
a date weeks before she rolls a single frame (SEE APPENDIX C).

Her camera is fitted with a single circular polarizing filter (SEE APPENDIX B), rotated to one
fixed angle before she calls "rolling." A polarizing filter is most effective at removing
reflected glare from a surface when the light striking that surface arrives at the surface's
Brewster angle (SEE APPENDIX A) — the one incidence angle at which reflected light becomes fully
polarized, and therefore fully blockable by a correctly rotated filter. At every other incidence
angle, some of the reflected light is *not* polarized at all, and no amount of rotating the ring
removes it (SEE APPENDIX D). The viewing room's glass window doesn't move and doesn't change
material between one shoot day and another — but the sun does. The real angle at which sunlight
strikes that glass depends entirely on the date and the time of day Mara books (SEE APPENDIX C),
and that angle is what decides how much of the window's own glare her filter can actually remove,
no matter how well she rotates it.

**The August Call.** Book the shoot for August 19, 2026 — the real, verified single best Chinook
day of this year's run — and roll at 2:00 PM.

**The September Call.** Book the shoot for a date inside the real, documented Coho peak-viewing
window at the Locks — the last two weeks of September — and roll at 2:00 PM.

One filter. One glass window. Two real dates, two real sun angles, two different ceilings on how
much of the window's glare a perfectly-tuned polarizer can remove (SEE APPENDIX D) — and only one
of these two runs is even still swimming by the time the other one shows up (SEE APPENDIX E).

Where this case's exact camera position or window orientation can't be pinned to a public floor
plan, that gap is stated openly as a Modeling Assumption (SEE APPENDIX G). Every external source
cited anywhere in this case is collected in Appendix H. The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this
case asks you to research are listed in Appendix I — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

## Appendix D: The Rotation Model — Malus's Law, Snell's Law, and the Fresnel Reflectance

**Why a polarizer cannot always fully null a surface's glare.** Reflected sunlight is not always
100% polarized. At a surface's Brewster angle (Appendix A), the reflected light is fully
polarized and a correctly rotated filter can remove all of it. At any other incidence angle, the
reflected light is a mix of a polarized part and an unpolarized part, found using the Fresnel
reflectance formulas for s- and p-polarized light:

$$R_s = \left|\frac{n_1\cos\theta_i - n_2\cos\theta_t}{n_1\cos\theta_i + n_2\cos\theta_t}\right|^2 \qquad R_p = \left|\frac{n_1\cos\theta_t - n_2\cos\theta_i}{n_1\cos\theta_t + n_2\cos\theta_i}\right|^2$$

where $\theta_i$ is the real angle of incidence (Appendix C) and $\theta_t$ is the transmitted
(refracted) angle from Snell's Law, $n_1 \sin\theta_i = n_2\sin\theta_t$. The total reflectance
for unpolarized sunlight is $R = (R_s+R_p)/2$; the *unremovable* part — what no polarizer rotation
can block — is $R_p/2$. (At the Brewster angle, $R_p = 0$ by definition, so the unremovable part
is zero — this is the special case Appendix A describes.)

**Malus's Law and the rotation-offset angle.** Let $\theta$ be the polarizer's rotation *offset*
from the setting that best removes the window's glare at a given shoot's real incidence angle
($\theta = 0°$ is the best possible setting; $\theta = 90°$ is fully rotated away from it). The
total glare transmitted to the lens follows a Malus's-Law-shaped relationship:

$$I(\theta) = \frac{R_p}{2} + \left(R - \frac{R_p}{2}\right)\sin^2\theta$$

This is the equation Challenge D2 asks you to solve for $\theta$, at each shoot date's own $R$ and
$R_p$ values, for the stated production tolerance below. The Pythagorean identity
($\sin^2\theta = 1-\cos^2\theta$) is what Challenge D3 asks you to verify and then use to express
this same relationship in its alternate, checkable form.

## Appendix F: The Real Optics, Equipment, and Data Behind This Case

Several real, published facts sit behind this case's dilemma.

**Brewster's angle, Snell's Law, and the Fresnel reflectance equations are real, standard optics
content**, taught in any introductory optics course and published in optics handbooks and
references (SEE APPENDIX H) [S1][S2][S10].

**The Tiffen 138mm UltraPol Circular Polarizer is a real, currently-sold filter** with a published
manufacturer spec sheet (SEE APPENDIX H) [S3].

**The NOAA Solar Calculator is a real, publicly available tool** that computes deterministic sun
azimuth and elevation for any given coordinates, date, and time (SEE APPENDIX H) [S8][S9].

**The Ballard Locks Chinook and Coho run-count and run-timing data are real**, maintained by the
Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife's ongoing Lake Washington counts program and
corroborated by independent Seattle-area salmon-viewing sources (SEE APPENDIX H) [S5][S6][S7].

**Chinook salmon are a real, documented primary prey source for the Southern Resident orca
population**, and that population is real and formally listed as endangered (SEE APPENDIX H)
[S12].

None of these real facts, on its own, produces this case's central mathematical tension — that
comes from combining the real, physically-grounded gap between a fixed material's Brewster angle
and the real sun-incidence angle on two different real calendar dates. Mara Ellison and this
specific shoot-day decision are a scenario construction built on real optics, real equipment, and
real environmental and biological data (SEE APPENDIX G).
