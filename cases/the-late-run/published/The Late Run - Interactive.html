Overview

Mara Ellison has the production calendar open on one screen and a blank booking form on the other, and her cursor has been sitting on the date field for four minutes.

The underground viewing room at the Ballard Locks only lets her crew in for one day. Not one day per run — one day, period, for the whole film. Behind her, a printed still from last year's scout visit is taped to the wall: the glass, the water column, a single Chinook mid-strike against the current, glare sitting across the pane like a second, unwanted subject in the frame. She knows exactly what her polarizer can and can't do about that glare — she's run the math twice already this morning. What she doesn't know is which day to point the math at.

Her production coordinator is refreshing his own screen down the hall, waiting to lock the crew's travel and the fish ladder's viewing-room reservation the second she says a date out loud. Two real windows are open on her calendar: one in the losing light of late summer, one three weeks later, at the edge of what the season still has left to give her.

She has shot glass before. She knows precisely how clean this filter can get her, on this window, on any given afternoon — that part isn't the question anymore. The question is which afternoon.

Mara picks up the phone and doesn't dial yet.

Background

Mara Ellison is the director of photography for a single-day segment of a conservation documentary tracking the salmon runs through the Ballard Locks fish ladder in Seattle, filmed as part of a project built around the endangered Southern Resident orca population's dependence on Chinook salmon as a food source (see Appendix F). She has full authority over every camera and lens decision on this shoot day; she does not have authority over the calendar — the production gets exactly one underground-viewing-room shoot day for the entire film, and she has to commit to a date weeks before she rolls a single frame (see Appendix C).

Her camera is fitted with a single circular polarizing filter (see Appendix B), rotated to one fixed angle before she calls "rolling." A polarizing filter is most effective at removing reflected glare from a surface when the light striking that surface arrives at the surface's Brewster angle (see Appendix A) — the one incidence angle at which reflected light becomes fully polarized, and therefore fully blockable by a correctly rotated filter. At every other incidence angle, some of the reflected light is not polarized at all, and no amount of rotating the ring removes it (see Appendix D). The viewing room's glass window doesn't move and doesn't change material between one shoot day and another — but the sun does. The real angle at which sunlight strikes that glass depends entirely on the date and the time of day Mara books (see Appendix C), and that angle is what decides how much of the window's own glare her filter can actually remove, no matter how well she rotates it.

The August Call

Book the shoot for August 19, 2026 — the real, verified single best Chinook day of this year's run — and roll at 2:00 PM.

The September Call

Book the shoot for a date inside the real, documented Coho peak-viewing window at the Locks — the last two weeks of September — and roll at 2:00 PM.

One filter. One glass window. Two real dates, two real sun angles, two different ceilings on how much of the window's glare a perfectly-tuned polarizer can remove (see Appendix D) — and only one of these two runs is even still swimming by the time the other one shows up (see Appendix E).

Where this case's exact camera position or window orientation can't be pinned to a public floor plan, that gap is stated openly as a Modeling Assumption (see Appendix G). Appendix D — The Rotation Model — Malus's Law, Snell's Law, and the Fresnel Reflectance

Why a polarizer cannot always fully null a surface's glare. Reflected sunlight is not always 100% polarized. At a surface's Brewster angle (Appendix A), the reflected light is fully polarized and a correctly rotated filter can remove all of it. At any other incidence angle, the reflected light is a mix of a polarized part and an unpolarized part, found using the Fresnel reflectance formulas for s- and p-polarized light:

Rs = | (n1cosθi − n2cosθt) / (n1cosθi + n2cosθt) |²    Rp = | (n1cosθt − n2cosθi) / (n1cosθt + n2cosθi) |²

where θi is the real angle of incidence (Appendix C) and θt is the transmitted (refracted) angle from Snell's Law, n1sinθi = n2sinθt. The total reflectance for unpolarized sunlight is R = (Rs+Rp)/2; the unremovable part — what no polarizer rotation can block — is Rp/2. (At the Brewster angle, Rp = 0 by definition, so the unremovable part is zero — this is the special case Appendix A describes.)

Malus's Law and the rotation-offset angle. Let θ be the polarizer's rotation offset from the setting that best removes the window's glare at a given shoot's real incidence angle (θ = 0° is the best possible setting; θ = 90° is fully rotated away from it). The total glare transmitted to the lens follows a Malus's-Law-shaped relationship:

I(θ) = Rp/2 + (R − Rp/2)·sin²θ

This is the equation Challenge D2 asks you to solve for θ, at each shoot date's own R and Rp values, for the stated production tolerance below. The Pythagorean identity (sin²θ = 1 − cos²θ) is what Challenge D3 asks you to verify and then use to express this same relationship in its alternate, checkable form.

Appendix F — The Real Optics, Equipment, and Data Behind This Case

Several real, published facts sit behind this case's dilemma. Brewster's angle, Snell's Law, and the Fresnel reflectance equations are real, standard optics content, taught in any introductory optics course and published in optics handbooks and references [S1][S2][S10]. The Tiffen 138mm UltraPol Circular Polarizer is a real, currently-sold filter with a published manufacturer spec sheet [S3]. The NOAA Solar Calculator is a real, publicly available tool that computes deterministic sun azimuth and elevation for any given coordinates, date, and time [S8][S9]. The Ballard Locks Chinook and Coho run-count and run-timing data are real, maintained by the Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife's ongoing Lake Washington counts program and corroborated by independent Seattle-area salmon-viewing sources [S5][S6][S7]. Chinook salmon are a real, documented primary prey source for the Southern Resident orca population, and that population is real and formally listed as endangered [S12].

None of these real facts, on its own, produces this case's central mathematical tension — that comes from combining the real, physically-grounded gap between a fixed material's Brewster angle and the real sun-incidence angle on two different real calendar dates. Mara Ellison and this specific shoot-day decision are a scenario construction built on real optics, real equipment, and real environmental and biological data.

    Step 1 · Brewster-angle worksheet (D1)

    The Brewster-angle relationship is tan θp = n2/n1. For this glass, n1 = 1 (air) and n2 = 1.52 (Appendix A), so θp = arctan(1.52). Evaluate that inverse trig function and enter the Brewster angle in degrees.

    Worked pattern: θp = arctan(n2/n1) = arctan(1.52 / 1). Use your calculator's inverse-tangent (tan-1, atan, or arctan) function, in degree mode, on the ratio n2/n1 from Appendix A — show that ratio and the inverse-trig step in your own worksheet.
