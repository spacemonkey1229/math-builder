## Overview

The dealer's chips click against each other in a steady rhythm, a sound Emilia Carlon has learned to hear underneath everything else on the floor — the slot bells, the low roar of a table that just hit big, a cocktail tray clinking past on someone's shoulder. She's been walking this floor for two years and she still checks the pit count out of habit, out of the corner of her eye, without breaking stride.

The elevator chimes. She doesn't need to turn around to know who it is — the two security guards step out first, then the assistant, then Danny Paseronopolis, exactly on schedule, the way he always is.

"How is everything this evening?"

"Running smoothly. Tables are full, should be a good night."

"Very good." He says it the way he always says it when he's pleased, which is not often enough that she's stopped noticing. He looks out over the floor with her for a moment — the two of them just watching it hum.

"Emilia, plans are in place to convert the south lobby into an additional gaming room. I'd like your thoughts on which game to place in that room. Can you let me know within two days?"

"Yes, sir."

He takes one more look across the floor, says "Very good" again to no one in particular, and heads for his next appointment. Emilia stands there a moment longer, running the two options through her head before she's even opened a spreadsheet — a room of card tables staffed by dealers, or a room of blinking machines that need no one at all.

Two days. She pulls out her phone and opens a blank note.

## Background

**Emilia Carlon** has worked for **Danny Paseronopolis**, owner of **The Landmark Hotel And Casino**, in various roles for over ten years, including two years as the casino's manager — a position she took after a prior career as an actuarial consultant. The Landmark has operated for more than 20 years and was recently ranked among the top 3 hotels and casinos in the city. Mr. Paseronopolis has given Emilia two days to recommend which of two games to install in the newly available south lobby space.

The two options are:

- **Indigo** — a dealer-managed card game, similar in structure to Poker or Blackjack. Players buy chips with their own money and bet on the strength of their hand; the casino does not bet for or against any player, but takes a percentage of each pot — the **pot rake** — set by house policy for this game (**SEE APPENDIX A**). The south lobby can hold up to **4 Indigo tables**, each seating up to 7 players and requiring one trained dealer.
- **Video Machines** — single-player, slot-style machines. A player bets a fixed amount and presses a button; the machine's built-in odds determine, at random, whether that turn wins. A winning turn pays the player their bet back plus an equal amount in winnings. On a losing turn, house policy for these machines splits the wagered amount between immediate casino revenue and a running jackpot pool (**SEE APPENDIX B**). The south lobby can hold up to **25 video machines**, needing no dealer but requiring two security staff per room regardless of how many machines are actually in use.

The Landmark does not currently operate either game, so no historical revenue exists for this specific room. What Mr. Paseronopolis does have is two years of hour-by-hour player traffic from a comparable room at another property he owns, broken out by day of week and time of day (**SEE APPENDIX C**), plus that same room's beverage-sales data by game type (**SEE APPENDIX D**) — useful context for the pitch, though not required to answer the profit question Mr. Paseronopolis actually asked. Because neither game has ever run at The Landmark before, projecting a weekly profit also requires stating a few scenario assumptions in the open (**SEE APPENDIX E**) about which hours the new room operates and how it is staffed.

Neither game changes the cost of remodeling the south lobby itself — only the cost of the tables or machines and the staff who run them (Appendix A, Appendix B).

The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in **Appendix G** — that is where Challenge 1 sends you. Curious how the pot rake and slot-machine odds work in real casinos beyond what's given here? **SEE APPENDIX F** for further reading.

## Appendix B: Video Machine Summary

- Four fixed bet amounts are available: **$0.50, $1.00, $1.50, $2.50.** Only one player plays a machine at a time.
- Each room of video machines requires **two security employees**, regardless of how many machines are installed or in use, paid **$7.50 per hour** each.
- Each machine costs **$2,860** to purchase and install.
- A single play (one button press) takes, on average, **17.9 seconds**.
- On a **losing** play, the house keeps 65% of the amount bet as immediate revenue; the remaining 35% is added to that machine's running jackpot pool, to be paid out later to a jackpot winner — not modeled in this case (**SEE APPENDIX E, Modeling Assumptions**).
- On a **winning** play, the player's bet is returned and they are paid an additional amount equal to their bet (e.g., a winning $1.50 play returns the $1.50 bet plus an extra $1.50).
- Each machine also carries three separate jackpots — a $15.00 jackpot, a $25.00 jackpot, and a "complete" jackpot equal to the machine's full accumulated pool — each won at long, fixed odds. These jackpots are **not** part of this case's weekly profit model (Appendix E).

**Frequency of betting amount per play**

| Bet amount | Frequency of this bet |
|---|---|
| $0.50 | 48.7% |
| $1.00 | 25.6% |
| $1.50 | 15.3% |
| $2.50 | 10.4% |

**Odds of winning per play**

| Outcome | Odds |
|---|---|
| $0.50 bet wins | 8 to 1 |
| $1.00 bet wins | 15 to 1 |
| $1.50 bet wins | 25 to 1 |
| $2.50 bet wins | 50 to 1 |
| Jackpot A ($15.00) | 125 to 1 |
| Jackpot B ($25.00) | 175 to 1 |
| Total Jackpot | 500 to 1 |

*Reading "X to 1" odds: for every 1 winning outcome, there are X losing outcomes, out of X + 1 total outcomes — so odds of 8 to 1 mean a win probability of 1 divided by (8 + 1).*

## Appendix E: Modeling Assumptions

> These are stated scenario choices made explicit for this case, not figures found in Appendix A–D. A Hero may challenge them in discussion — that is a legitimate move.

| # | Assumption | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| MA1 | The new south lobby room operates only during its **peak traffic window, 16:00–04:00 daily** (the two highest-traffic time blocks in Appendix C, both weekday and weekend), not around the clock. | The room is brand-new, with no existing customer base of its own. Appendix C's own traffic numbers show the 04:00–16:00 window running at roughly a third to half the volume of the 16:00–04:00 window in every case — a new room is more plausibly opened on the schedule that already proves out, not staffed at full cost through its weakest hours from day one. |
| MA2 | The three jackpots described in Appendix B are excluded from this case's weekly profit model. | A single week is far too short a window to estimate how often a jackpot at 125-to-1, 175-to-1, or 500-to-1 odds actually pays out — modeling it honestly needs a much longer time horizon than one week. The 65%/35% split that funds the jackpots is stated in full in Appendix B; only the payout side is left out. |
| MA3 | Every table or machine installed in the room is assumed to be staffed and running throughout the hours the room is open (per MA1) — the room is modeled as built out to its stated maximum (4 Indigo tables, or 25 video machines), not a partial build-out. | Appendix A and Appendix B give the room's maximum capacity; no other capacity figure is stated, so the full build-out is the only one the given data supports without inventing a number. |

## Balance Argument (facilitator reference — do not share with students before discussion)

**Argument for A (Indigo):** Under the peak-hours-only build (MA1), the four Indigo tables' high-dollar pots produce a substantially larger weighted-average rake per game than any single video machine can produce per play, even after accounting for Indigo's slower game pace and its per-table dealer wage. The room is small (4 tables vs. up to 25 machines), but the dollars moving through each Indigo game dwarf a $0.50–$2.50 machine bet.

**Argument for B (Video Machines):** Video machines need no specialized staff to open — no dealer to hire, certify, or schedule — and their revenue scales with sheer volume: over 200 plays per hour per active machine, across up to 25 machines simultaneously, versus at most a few dozen hands per hour across 4 Indigo tables combined. Machines also carry a much lower fixed labor cost (2 security staff for the whole room, regardless of machine count, vs. one paid dealer *per table*).

**The margin:** Under MA1–MA3, Indigo's projected weekly profit is close enough to Video's that the choice is genuinely debatable — see `case.json.balance.margin_pct` and `sensitivity_model.py` for the exact figures, computed from D4. Neither argument above states that number; work it from Appendix A–C and D4's own steps.

**The trap:** A Hero who skips straight to "4 tables can't possibly out-earn 25 machines" is reasoning from *unit count* instead of *dollars per unit* — Indigo's per-table dollar rate is high enough to close most of that 25-to-4 gap. The opposite trap also exists: a Hero who assumes card tables obviously win because "the pots are huge" without ever computing a video-machine blended profit-per-play (D3) is skipping the actual math the same way. D1–D4 exist specifically to force both sides to be computed, not assumed.
