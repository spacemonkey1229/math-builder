Overview

The fabricator needs a hull profile by five o'clock, and Theo Marsh is standing over two cross-section drawings taped to the whiteboard, a grease pencil in one hand.

One drawing is a tall steel cylinder with a pointed cone welded to its top — the shape of every NOAA weather buoy Theo has ever hauled out of the water for maintenance. The other is a steel sphere, squat and round, no taller than his truck. Behind him, the fabrication shop's cutting table sits idle, waiting on his mark. The shop foreman already called twice.

The buoy has to hold a wave sensor, a radio mast (an antenna pole for transmitting data to shore), and enough battery to run both through a winter it won't see anyone until spring. It has to float high enough to keep the antenna dry and low enough to stay put in a gale. And it has to do all of that moored to the same cast-concrete sinker either way — the sinker isn't the question. The hull is.

Theo circles the cylinder-and-cone drawing, then the sphere. Somewhere in the shop's steel-scrap bin sits the offcuts from the last hull nobody double-checked before cutting.

The grease pencil hovers over the order sheet.

Background

Theo Marsh is a coastal engineer with the state ports authority, responsible for the mooring design of a new offshore data buoy that will carry a wave sensor and a radio mast (an antenna pole for transmitting data to shore) for a coastal-hazard early-warning program. Theo is accountable to the fabrication shop for one number by the end of the day: which hull shape to cut steel for.

Both hull designs under consideration must displace at least the same minimum volume of water, so that either one provides enough buoyant capacity to carry the sensor package and stay stable in the water (SEE APPENDIX A). Both designs are moored to the same style of cast-concrete pyramidal sinker — a heavy weight resting on the seafloor, connected to the buoy by chain, that anchors it in place — sized once and used for either option (SEE APPENDIX B). The sinker is not part of the decision, only the shared cost and shared holding weight it contributes.

The Spar Design is a tall steel cylinder with a conical “cutwater” nose welded to the top, not to help it move forward, but to shed waves that wash across a stationary buoy’s top the same way a moving hull’s bow sheds water — matching the general proportions of the boat-shaped spar-hull buoys NOAA's National Data Buoy Center has moored offshore for decades (SEE APPENDIX A). Its narrow profile and low center of gravity give it a naturally stable, low-rolling ride in rough seas, and its flat cylindrical sides are simple for the shop to fabricate and to bolt instrument brackets onto.

The Spherical Design is a single steel sphere. A sphere encloses more volume per unit of steel surface than any other shape, so for the same buoyant capacity it should need less steel — but a sphere has no natural “bottom,” so it needs added ballast low in the hull to keep it from rolling over (tipping side to side) in a swell, the way the Spar's shape does on its own (SEE APPENDIX C).

Steel and concrete are priced by weight and by volume, and buoyancy is a volume — so the honest way to compare the two hulls is dollars of net buoyant lift per dollar of material, not raw size (SEE APPENDIX D). He has to send the shop one hull shape.

Appendix A — Hull Dimensions, Steel, and the Real Buoys Behind Them

NOAA's National Data Buoy Center (NDBC) has moored boat-shaped, spar-hulled data buoys offshore for decades, including its 6-meter NOMAD-class hull — described by NDBC as giving “relatively high cost effectiveness and excellent long-term survivability in severe seas,” with no known capsizings of that hull class. NDBC's discus buoys are named directly for their size class (e.g. a “3-meter discus buoy”). Theo's two designs are scaled to that real size class (SEE APPENDIX E, MA1–MA3).

Both hull designs must displace at least 6.41 m³ — the minimum buoyant volume the naval architecture team (the marine engineers who design the buoy's hull and stability) specified (MA1).

Appendix C — The Spherical Design's Stability Ballast

A spar hull's long, narrow, bottom-heavy shape gives it a naturally low center of gravity and a stable, low-rolling ride without any added weight. A sphere has no “bottom” to speak of — without help, it can roll and tumble in a swell instead of riding it.

Appendix D — The Cost-and-Lift Comparison, Step by Step

Net buoyant lift = (hull volume × seawater density) − hull steel weight − sinker's submerged weight − (Spherical Design only) stability ballast weight.

A sinker’s submerged weight = (material density − seawater density) × volume — seawater pushes back on anything submerged in it.

Total material cost = every dollar of steel, concrete, and (Option B only) ballast.

The primary comparison (D5) is net buoyant lift ÷ total material cost, in kilograms of net lift per dollar.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    “One more call, before Theo signs the order.”

    The fabrication shop just got back to him. Their rolling equipment can bend flat steel plate into a cylinder — that's routine. But a sphere isn't made of flat plate bent one way. It's built from compound-curved panels, curved in two directions at once, and this shop can't roll those in-house.

    They'd have to subcontract the spherical hull's curved panels to an outside fabricator — at a real cost markup on top of the raw steel: a stated 40% surcharge on the Spherical Design's own steel cost.
