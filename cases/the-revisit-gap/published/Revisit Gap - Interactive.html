Overview

The board meets in nine days, and Marcus Oduya has one line item left to defend: the satellite bill.

Last year’s invoice sits open on one monitor — five figures, itemized by week. On the other monitor, a free download queue slowly fills with the same forest, the same tree line, a few days slower and a little blurrier at the edges. Marcus has spent three years watching both feeds catch the same clearings, eventually. The question the board actually wants answered isn’t which feed is sharper. It’s whether the sharper one is worth what it costs.

His inbox has a reminder from the field-patrol coordinator: a report of a small clearing near the reserve’s eastern boundary, confirmed on the ground, that neither feed flagged the same week it happened. Marcus still isn’t sure which feed would have caught it sooner, or whether either one would have caught it at all.

He opens a blank slide, types “Recommendation,” and stops.

Outside his window, a delivery truck idles at the gate — this month’s invoice, printed and signed, waiting for his initials before it goes to the board packet.

Background

Marcus Oduya is a deforestation-monitoring analyst for a rainforest-conservation NGO, responsible for recommending the program’s satellite-imagery budget for the coming year to the board before it locks. He is accountable for one recommendation, backed by numbers: which imagery plan to fund for the 25 km² reserve his program monitors.

Both options watch the same reserve for the same purpose — catching illegal forest clearing early enough for the field-patrol team to respond (SEE APPENDIX A). They differ in resolution (the size of ground each single pixel represents), revisit cadence, and cost.

Commercial Weekly subscribes to 3-meter-resolution satellite imagery, newly tasked over the reserve every week — instead of only capturing the reserve when its orbit happens to fly over it, the operator points the satellite’s camera at the reserve on request — from a commercial provider (SEE APPENDIX A). Finer resolution means smaller clearings are visible before they grow, but the subscription is billed by the square kilometer and by the week, and someone still has to review every scene (one satellite image covering the reserve) it delivers (SEE APPENDIX B).

Sentinel Free relies on the Sentinel-2 satellite constellation’s free, public 10-meter-resolution imagery, which automatically revisits (re-photographs, on its fixed orbit) the same ground roughly every 5 days (SEE APPENDIX A). It costs nothing to download, but a coarser pixel misses small clearings entirely, and someone still has to review every scene it delivers too (SEE APPENDIX B).

Neither option is free once both are counted the same way: the honest comparison is hectares of confirmed illegal clearing detected per dollar of annual imagery cost — not sticker price, and not resolution alone (SEE APPENDIX C). He has to recommend one plan.

Appendix A — The Two Feeds, Resolution, and Detection Lag

Sentinel-2 is a two-satellite European Space Agency constellation whose finest optical bands have a native ground resolution of 10 meters, and which revisits a given point on Earth roughly every 5 days at the equator with both satellites operating [S1]. It is downloadable free of charge from public portals including USGS EarthExplorer (a US government satellite-image search tool), the Sentinel-2 L2A (a processing stage where the raw signal is already corrected for atmosphere and ready to use) archive on AWS Open Data, and the Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem (the European Space Agency's own free data portal) [S2][S3][S4].

Commercial sub-5-meter imagery (this case uses a 3-meter-resolution product, matching Planet’s SkySat constellation (a commercial satellite operator) as resold through SkyFi’s Planet Select marketplace (an online storefront for buying satellite images)) can be newly tasked: instead of only capturing an area when its orbit happens to fly over it, the operator points the satellite’s camera at a customer-chosen spot on request, on a set cadence — weekly, in this case [S5].

Minimum detectable clearing size. A widely used rule of thumb holds that a feature must span roughly 3×3 pixels to be reliably detected — for example, 30-meter imagery from Landsat (a long-running US government Earth-observation satellite program) is generally treated as reliably resolving features no smaller than about 0.81 hectares [S6][S7].

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    “One more report, before the board packet closes.”

    An independent field audit — ground survey teams walking the reserve’s boundary by hand — just came back. It found that 75% of this year’s confirmed clearings were small enough to slip under Sentinel Free’s detection floor. Not 50%, like the original estimate assumed. The loggers doing the smallest, hardest-to-spot cuts favor the cloudiest weeks of the wet season — exactly when Sentinel Free’s own imagery is most likely to be unusable anyway.
