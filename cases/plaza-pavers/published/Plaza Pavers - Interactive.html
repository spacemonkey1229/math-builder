Overview

Priya Nakamura is alone in the small conference room at Cascadia Hardscape Design & Build, and the bid portal closes at five. Her boss handed her this one on purpose — “your name on it, your call” — and she still isn’t sure if that was a vote of confidence or a test.

Two site plans are taped to the whiteboard, one over the other, and she keeps peeling back the top sheet to look at the one underneath. The city’s arts commission wants the plaza’s original historic pavement pattern back. A rival firm has already filed a bid built on the cheapest thing that could possibly go down flat and fast, and word came back through a contact on the commission that it undercut the published target by enough to make everyone nervous.

Priya’s laptop shows two spreadsheets, side by side, neither one finished. Her phone buzzes face-down on the desk — her boss, probably, asking if it’s done. She picks up a dry-erase marker, caps it, sets it down again. Somewhere on one of these two sheets is the number that decides whether Cascadia wins this plaza or loses it to the firm that didn’t bother matching the history.

Background

Priya Nakamura is the senior designer of record on Cascadia Hardscape Design & Build’s bid for the restoration of Kessler Plaza, a public gathering space the city is repaving as part of a downtown streetscape project. She has full authority to set the technical design of the bid; the firm’s owner has final sign-off but told her he wants her recommendation, not his.

The city’s arts commission has published a target cost of $18.50 per square foot for the finished installation, weighted heavily in how bids are scored — not an automatic disqualifier, but a bid that misses it has to defend why in the narrative. A competing firm has already submitted a bid using a single-shape hexagon paver laid edge to edge, which the commission’s own pre-bid materials flagged as underpricing the historic-match criterion the commission has also stated it wants.

Kessler Plaza’s paved area measures 200 ft × 150 ft. Two paver systems are under real consideration for Cascadia’s bid:

The Hex Grid — a single regular-hexagon paver laid edge to edge across the full plaza, the same system the rival firm bid. Because a regular hexagon’s interior angles are exactly the right measure to close all the way around a point with no gap, this pattern needs almost no interior cutting — only the plaza’s straight rectangular border forces any cut pavers at all.
The Weave Pattern — a combination of one regular-octagon paver and one square paver, matching the plaza’s original 1962 pavement as documented in the city’s historic-preservation archive. This pattern uses two paver SKUs and needs a mitred cut at every straight border edge the pattern crosses, which is real labor and real material waste.

A real hexagon-paver field, laid edge to edge exactly like Option A’s Hex Grid. Photo: Pethrus, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0.

The octagon-and-square tiling behind Option B’s Weave Pattern, the same combination historically used in mid-century plaza and sidewalk pavement. Diagram: Watchduck (Tilman Piesk), Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0.

Before settling on either shape, Cascadia’s design team keeps a standing reference of every regular polygon it has considered and rejected for single-shape paving over the years, along with two tessellating quadrilaterals — a rhombus paver and a kite paver — that were rejected for reasons other than geometry (see Appendix A). Priya must justify in her bid narrative why only these two options survived to the final round.

Every dollar of the winning bid’s cost lives in three places: the material cost of the pavers themselves, the labor cost of cutting pavers to fit the border, and the waste cost of pavers ruined or discarded in cutting. The commission’s target cost applies to the total of all three, per square foot of finished plaza — not to materials alone. Curious about the real-world math and history behind these patterns? Also on file: a rhombus paver and a kite paver, both real tessellating quadrilaterals, rejected for a non-geometric reason — their acute corners (well under 90°) chip and spall under foot traffic and snowplow blades far more often than the field pavers’ obtuse or right-angle corners do.

Border condition. A custom-cut piece closes the gap where either pattern meets the straight border. Four of its five interior angles are fixed by the neighboring field pavers — 90°, 90°, 112°, and 146° — and the stonecutter needs the fifth angle to program the cut. Where this piece’s cut edge crosses the border line, it creates one pair of vertical angles and one pair of supplementary angles.

Appendix E — Historic Pavement Reference

Kessler Plaza’s original 1962 pavement, as documented in the city’s own historic-preservation archive, was an octagon-and-square paver field — a pattern common on mid-century American civic plazas and sidewalks generally. The arts commission’s pre-bid materials state: “Bidders are asked to honor the plaza’s documented historic pavement character where a firm can do so responsibly within the published target cost.”
