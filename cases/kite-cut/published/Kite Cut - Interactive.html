Overview

Priya Anand is on her knees on the workshop floor, surrounded by spooled ripstop nylon and a fan of fiberglass rods, when her phone buzzes against the cutting mat again. The city Arts Festival's event coordinator wants a final answer on the opening-day kite giveaway — tonight, before Anand Kite Works closes for the day.

Two sample frames lie side by side on the mat, both a diamond silhouette the crowd will recognize on sight, both cut to the same square-ish outline. Only the crossbar sits in a different place on each. On one, the crossbar splits the spine dead center — a shape so symmetric she can fold it on itself either way. On the other, the crossbar rides high, closer to the nose, an old proportion she found in a kite-history binder her grandfather kept, the one that supposedly lets the sail fly without a tail dragging behind it in the wind.

Both frames look, to the naked eye, like they'll eat about the same yardage off the roll. Priya isn't convinced that's actually true, and with the whole festival order riding on tonight's purchase order, "looks about the same" isn't something she can sign her name to.

She pulls the fabric roll and the spar invoices onto the mat, picks up a marker, and starts laying out cutting patterns.

Background

Priya Anand owns and runs Anand Kite Works, a small kite-making business that supplies hand-built festival and promotional kites to city arts organizations, schools, and specialty retailers. She prices and cuts every production run herself, which means every material decision — fabric, spars, trim — has to pencil out before she commits the shop's supply budget to an order.

The festival wants 250 identical diamond-shaped kites for its opening-day giveaway (see Appendix D), each built to the shop's standard festival size: a 36-inch-tall, 36-inch-wide sail — a size consistent with typical diamond-shaped kites generally, which "vary in size, though most are less than 1 metre (3 ft) in height" [S1] (see Appendix A). Every kite in the run uses the same two framing sticks — a vertical spine and a horizontal spreader, each cut from 0.125-inch fiberglass rod [S2] — crossing at a right angle to form the sail's two diagonals. Where those two sticks cross is the only real difference between the two designs on Priya's cutting mat.

The True Diamond crosses the spreader at the exact midpoint of the spine — 18 inches down from the nose, splitting both sticks evenly in half. The Eddy Flyer follows the historic Eddy kite proportion instead: the spreader crosses 19 percent of the way down the spine from the nose [S3], well above center. That single difference in crossing point is real engineering, not decoration — "typical diamond-shaped kites need a tail to help with stability" [S4], while the off-center Eddy proportion (paired with a bowed spreader) was specifically developed to fly stable without one [S3][S4] (see Appendix E for how this case treats the tail Priya must still cut for The True Diamond).

Fabric comes off a single roll of 60-inch-wide ripstop nylon priced at $7.95 per yard [S5] (see Appendix B), and every square inch that isn't turned into a finished sail or tail is fabric Priya has already paid for and cannot resell. How efficiently each frame's sail shape can actually be laid out and cut from that roll — not just how much sail area each design encloses — is the real question behind tonight's purchase order (see Appendix C).

Appendix C — Production Cutting Layouts

Computed directly from Appendix A's dimensions (not sourced): sail area for both options — diagonals 36 in × 36 in, Area = d1 × d2 ÷ 2 — 648 sq in per kite. Option A's side lengths are all 25.46 in (a Rhombus). Option B's side lengths are 19.26 in and 34.27 in (a Kite, not a Rhombus).

Option A's cutting layout: two sail panels, one rotated 180° about the midpoint of a shared edge, nest into a single Parallelogram — zero fabric waste. Option B's cutting layout (Modeling Assumption MA3, see Appendix E): the same edge-rotation pairing does not close cleanly for the off-center kite outline, so this batch is cut with each panel individually boxed inside its own 36 in × 36 in bounding square.
