Case Study: Kite Cut
MS | Math | Case Study | 2026-27 | Heroes Academy

## Overview

Priya Anand is on her knees on the workshop floor, surrounded by spooled ripstop nylon and a fan
of fiberglass rods, when her phone buzzes against the cutting mat again. The city Arts Festival's
event coordinator wants a final answer on the opening-day kite giveaway — tonight, before Anand
Kite Works closes for the day.

Two sample frames lie side by side on the mat, both a diamond silhouette the crowd will recognize
on sight, both cut to the same square-ish outline. Only the crossbar sits in a different place on
each. On one, the crossbar splits the spine dead center — a shape so symmetric she can fold it on
itself either way. On the other, the crossbar rides high, closer to the nose, an old proportion she
found in a kite-history binder her grandfather kept, the one that supposedly lets the sail fly
without a tail dragging behind it in the wind.

Both frames look, to the naked eye, like they'll eat about the same yardage off the roll. Priya
isn't convinced that's actually true, and with the whole festival order riding on tonight's
purchase order, "looks about the same" isn't something she can sign her name to.

She pulls the fabric roll and the spar invoices onto the mat, picks up a marker, and starts laying
out cutting patterns.

## Background

Priya Anand owns and runs Anand Kite Works, a small kite-making business that supplies hand-built
festival and promotional kites to city arts organizations, schools, and specialty retailers. She
prices and cuts every production run herself, which means every material decision — fabric, spars,
trim — has to pencil out before she commits the shop's supply budget to an order.

The festival wants **250 identical diamond-shaped kites** for its opening-day giveaway (SEE
APPENDIX D), each built to the shop's standard festival size: a **36-inch-tall, 36-inch-wide**
sail — a size consistent with typical diamond-shaped kites generally, which "vary in size, though
most are less than 1 metre (3 ft) in height" [S1] (SEE APPENDIX A). Every kite in the run uses the
same two framing sticks — a vertical **spine** and a horizontal **spreader**, each cut from
**0.125-inch fiberglass rod** [S2] — crossing at a right angle to form the sail's two diagonals.
Where those two sticks cross is the only real difference between the two designs on Priya's cutting
mat.

**The True Diamond** crosses the spreader at the exact midpoint of the spine — 18 inches down from
the nose, splitting both sticks evenly in half. **The Eddy Flyer** follows the historic Eddy kite
proportion instead: the spreader crosses **19 percent of the way down the spine from the nose**
[S3], well above center. That single difference in crossing point is real engineering, not
decoration — "typical diamond-shaped kites need a tail to help with stability" [S4], while the
off-center Eddy proportion (paired with a bowed spreader) was specifically developed to fly stable
without one [S3][S4] (SEE APPENDIX E for how this case treats the tail Priya must still cut for The
True Diamond).

Fabric comes off a single roll of **60-inch-wide ripstop nylon** priced at **$7.95 per yard** [S5]
(SEE APPENDIX B), and every square inch that isn't turned into a finished sail or tail is fabric
Priya has already paid for and cannot resell. How efficiently each frame's sail shape can actually
be laid out and cut from that roll — not just how much sail area each design encloses — is the real
question behind tonight's purchase order (SEE APPENDIX C). All figures in this case are sourced in
Appendix F, or stated as an explicit modeling assumption in Appendix E where no public figure
exists. The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in Appendix H —
that is where Challenge 1 sends you. (SEE APPENDIX G for further reading on Eddy kites and
diamond-kite construction.)

## Dilemma

You are Priya Anand.
Do you:
A.) Cut the whole order as The True Diamond — a symmetric rhombus frame that needs a tail on every
kite OR
B.) Cut the whole order as The Eddy Flyer — the historic off-center kite frame that flies tailless

## Appendix C — Production Cutting Layouts

This appendix's geometry is computed directly from Appendix A's dimensions (not sourced):

- Sail area, both options — diagonals 36 in × 36 in, Area = d1 × d2 ÷ 2 — **648 sq in per kite**
  (SEE D2 for the full comparison of A vs. B).
- Side lengths, Option A: all four sides = √(18² + 18²) = **25.46 in** — all four equal (a
  Rhombus).
- Side lengths, Option B: two sides = √(18² + 6.84²) = **19.26 in**; the other two sides =
  √(18² + 29.16²) = **34.27 in** — two unequal pairs of adjacent equal sides (a Kite, not a
  Rhombus).
- Option A cutting layout: two sail panels, one rotated 180° about the midpoint of a shared edge,
  nest into a single Parallelogram — zero fabric waste (SEE D3 for the full construction).
- Option B cutting layout (Modeling Assumption MA3, SEE APPENDIX E): the same edge-rotation
  pairing does not close cleanly for the off-center kite outline, so this batch is cut with each
  panel individually boxed inside its own 36 in × 36 in bounding square.

## Appendix E — Modeling Assumptions

> These figures are **not sourced**. They are stated scenario assumptions chosen by the case
> author, each with a real basis named below. A Hero may challenge them in discussion — that is a
> legitimate move, and Appendix H names the skills a Hero would need to test one.

| # | Assumption | Value used | Basis | Sensitivity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MA1 | Tail length for Option A, as a multiple of the kite's own height | **4× kite height = 144 in (12 ft)** | Kite-making guidance for diamond-shaped kites suggests starting a tail at roughly 3–4 times the kite's height and testing from there [S6]; a separate hobbyist reference corroborates "several times the height" without giving an exact number [S7]. No single fixed industry ratio exists. | Scales Option A's tail cost linearly; on its own it does not decide the case's overall material-cost winner (SEE MA3). |
| MA2 | Tail strip width | **4 in** | No public standard exists for a giveaway kite's tail width. 4 in is a common, easy-to-cut craft strip width, cut from the same fabric roll as the sail. | Same as MA1 — scales Option A's tail cost linearly. |
| MA3 | Cutting-layout efficiency for Option B's sail panels | Cut individually inside a 36 in × 36 in bounding square — no pairing efficiency assumed | The zero-waste Parallelogram pairing proven for Option A in D3 relies on the Rhombus's point symmetry (both diagonals bisect each other). The Eddy sail's off-center crossing point breaks that symmetry, so the same simple two-panel pairing does not close into a clean shape. This case assumes the shop uses the simpler, individually-boxed layout for this run rather than researching a more complex interlocking cut. | This is the case's single most sensitive assumption. Every quadrilateral can, in principle, tile a plane without any gaps at all — if a more efficient interlocking layout exists for the Eddy sail's shape, Option B's fabric cost could fall.
