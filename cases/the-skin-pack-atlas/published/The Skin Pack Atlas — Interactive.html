Overview

The fan on Alicia Tran's laptop is loud enough to hear over the Discord call, and nobody has said anything for eleven seconds. On her second monitor sit two renders of the same character, side by side, wearing the same new skin. On the left, every scale on the tail is crisp enough to count. On the right, the same scales blur slightly the moment the camera zooms in — not broken, just softer, the kind of soft a player notices without being able to say why.

Alicia is the only technical artist at Tanager Games, seven people total, and she is the one who has to explain — again — why a texture that looks perfect on her own phone can make a stranger's phone refuse to draw it at all. The community manager shares her screen too: support tickets from the last skin pack, sorted by country, most of them from India and Nigeria — Tanager's two fastest-growing markets, where a large share of new phones still ship with budget-tier silicon rather than the flagship chips Alicia's own test device runs. “Players are saying their skins are just… gray,” she says. “Some are asking for refunds.”

The build for tonight's release is sitting in two branches, one for each atlas, and Alicia has maybe twenty minutes before she has to pick one and merge it. She pulls up her spreadsheet of device data instead of picking by feel this time. Somewhere in these two columns of numbers is an answer that isn't just “which one looks better in a screenshot.”

She opens both branches side by side, uncaps a marker for the whiteboard, and starts drawing a grid.

Background

Alicia Tran is the sole technical artist at Tanager Games, a seven-person indie studio that makes Molt, a collectible-creature idle RPG (see Appendix D). Molt's revenue comes almost entirely from character skin packs, and Alicia is responsible for the technical decision of how each pack's new art gets packed into the game before it ships — and accountable to the rest of the small team for making sure this week's pack does not silently break the game for players who already paid for it. Two packaging options are on the table for tonight's release, and only one can ship:

The Crest Atlas — pack the new character art into a single 8192×8192 texture atlas. This is the sharpest possible version of the new skins, and it is what Alicia's own flagship test device renders (see Appendix D).

The Anchor Atlas — pack the same art into a single 4096×4096 texture atlas instead. This requires compressing the source art harder to fit, which visibly softens fine detail like scale edges and fur, but every device Tanager has ever tested has been able to load a 4096×4096 texture without failure (see Appendix D).

The failure mode that matters is not gradual: a mobile GPU either supports a texture at a given dimension or it does not. If a device's GPU cannot handle a texture at the atlas's dimension, that texture does not load — the character shows up untextured, or the game refuses to draw that screen at all. This is a hard pass/fail, tied to a real, published mobile-GPU texture-size capability ladder (see Appendix C), not a matter of the art simply looking a little worse on older phones.

Tanager's two fastest-growing markets, by new installs, are India and Nigeria (see Appendix A). Both markets skew toward budget-tier Android hardware: in India, MediaTek — whose chips run ARM Mali-family GPUs rather than Qualcomm's Adreno — is the single largest smartphone chipset vendor, and in Nigeria more than half of all phones sold are one of three Transsion-made brands (Tecno, Infinix, Itel) built almost entirely on the same budget MediaTek/Unisoc silicon (see Appendix C). A real, current, peer-reviewed-track study of commercial mobile games found that GPU-related problems — specifically devices too old or low-tier to render what a game asks of them — account for close to a quarter of all the compatibility bugs those games' own developers had to fix (see Appendix C). A ratio that only counts devices running budget silicon, though, is not the whole picture: not every install is worth the same to the studio, because heavier-spending players tend to cluster on newer, higher-tier devices. Tanager's own analytics dashboard reports total monthly skin-pack revenue by country, but does not break that revenue out by device tier — a real limitation many small studios operate under. Alicia has to reconstruct that missing breakdown herself, using India and Nigeria's known device-tier composition and each market's known total revenue as a system of two linear equations (see Appendices A and B), built on top of a small set of disclosed modeling assumptions and proxies where Tanager's own real business data does not exist to source (see Appendix E).

Alicia's recommendation must be judged on one number: the revenue-weighted percent of Tanager's active install base that can render the chosen atlas without a texture load failure, found by building a 2×2 system of linear equations from Appendices A and C and solving it using matrix algebra (see Appendices A, B, C and D). Every sourced and assumed figure in this case is listed in Appendix F, and the vocabulary and “how to…” skills this case will ask of you are listed in Appendix G — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

Appendix B — The Install-Base Matrix M

Alicia's model treats each of Tanager's two growth markets as a row and each of two device tiers as a column:

  • Column 1 — Flagship-tier: GPUs capable of rendering an 8192×8192 texture without failure.
  • Column 2 — Value-tier: GPUs found in budget-oriented chipsets (MediaTek in India; Transsion-brand devices — Tecno, Infinix, Itel — in Nigeria, predominantly MediaTek/Unisoc chipsets), used here as the best available current, region-specific proxy for devices at meaningful risk of a texture-size failure at 8192×8192 (see Appendix C, MA3).

Each entry equals that market's total reported active installs (Appendix A) multiplied by its Flagship-tier or Value-tier share (Appendix A, sourced from Appendix C). M is a matrix with matrix dimension 2×2 — 2 rows (markets) by 2 columns (device tiers) — once every entry is filled in. Its exact numeric entries are:

It does not flip the case's answer, and a Hero who raises this point should be credited for catching a real, current limitation of the model.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — D1: Build the install-base matrix M and state its dimensions

    Given data is in Appendix A/B. Each entry equals a market's total active installs times its Flagship- or Value-tier share. Type the matrix's four entries and its dimensions (e.g. “2x2”).

    Step 2 — D2: Identity matrix I and inverse M−1

    Write the 2×2 identity matrix, then your computed M−1. The check verifies M · M−1 = I entry by entry.

    Step 3 — D3: Determinant of M

    Use the 2×2 determinant formula: for [[m11,m12],[m21,m22]], det = m11·m22 − m12·m21.

    Step 4 — D4: Solve r = M−1v for the revenue-per-install vector

    v = [India revenue, Nigeria revenue]T (Appendix A). Solve for r = [Flagship-tier $/install, Value-tier $/install]T.

    Step 5 — D5: Final decision table

    For each atlas option: the device-count percent of the install base that renders successfully, and the revenue-weighted percent (using D4's per-tier revenue rates). The Anchor Atlas succeeds on every device tier by construction (Appendix D).

    This Case Ships Without a Twist

    As with the project's other strongest structurally-driven cases, the surprise here is mathematical, not narrative: the moment a Hero realizes that “percent of devices that fail” and “percent of revenue that fails” are not the same number, and that the gap between them is exactly what D4's matrix solve was for. A Hero who stops at Appendix C's raw device-share percentages will never notice this on their own.

    The pressure a scripted twist would normally add is still here — it's in the case's own trap and the Deep Conflict discussion your facilitator runs instead: a large, real device-count share on budget-tier GPUs in Tanager's fastest-growing markets that looks alarming on its own, and Appendix E's disclosed MA3 finding that the model's own hard cap may be over-counting failures on top of that. Rather than manufacture a scripted mid-discussion reveal, this case ships without one.
