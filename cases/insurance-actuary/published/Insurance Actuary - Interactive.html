Overview

The mahogany table is already half full. Sofia Ficonia takes the folding chair against the wall — the one reserved for staff who present nothing and say less — and sets her laptop bag between her feet. Eight years at Safetylife and she has never once been in this room while the Board was in it.

Uni Kimuni buttons his suit jacket at the head of the table. He is thirty-nine and has been CEO for eleven months, hired the week the old leadership was walked out for losing a third of the company's revenue in four years. He glances back at Sofia — not at the Board, at her — and says it quietly enough that only she catches it: "Sure this is the right call?"

She has run the numbers four times. She has run them a fifth time this morning, on the drive in, replaying the arithmetic in her head at every red light. Two spreadsheets sit open on the laptop she has not yet closed: one titled YOUNG, one titled SENIOR. A third file, the one she has not opened since Tuesday, is titled MIDDLE — and stays closed, because the team settled that question days ago. Kimuni is still watching her.

She nods.

He turns to the table, opens the folder in front of him, and begins: "Effective 2019, Safetylife will discontinue automobile insurance for one driver age class." Twelve board members lean forward. Sofia's cursor sits blinking over the export button on the spreadsheet that isn't open yet.

Background

Safetylife has sold personal and commercial automobile insurance in Carbosia — a developed nation with insurance laws similar to most U.S. states — for 79 years. In 2014 it was Carbosia's largest personal auto insurer, writing over Ĉ3.2 billion (Carbosian Caribs) in premium. Every year since, its revenue and market share fell; by 2018, revenue had dropped to Ĉ2.4 billion. The Board dismissed the old leadership and hired Uni Kimuni, who has publicly committed to growing 2019 revenue by 2% — a promise he made to the Board and to Safetylife's shareholders before he had seen a single claims file.

Kimuni's plan, built with Sofia's actuarial team, is to narrow Safetylife's personal auto book to two of its three driver-age classes: Young (14–24), Middle-aged (25–64), and Senior (65+). One class is discontinued entirely — no new policies, no renewals. The team has already ruled out cutting Middle-aged: it is Safetylife's largest and most stable customer segment by far, and no analysis the actuaries ran supported discarding the company's core book. The real decision is between the other two.

Safetylife prices and tracks risk using a 27-class code: every policy is classified by Vehicle Size (Small / Medium / Large), Driver Age (Young / Middle / Senior), and Driver Risk (Low / Average / High, based on driving record), coded by the first letter of each in that order. A large car with a middle-aged primary driver and a driving record showing a higher likelihood of future claims is class LMH. Three years of quarterly claims data — car-years of exposure, number of claims, and Ĉ amount of claims incurred, across all 27 classes and 5 required coverages (Bodily Injury, Property Damage, Comprehensive, Collision, Personal Injury) — sit in Safetylife's system for every quarter from 2016 through 2018 (see Appendix A and Appendix B).

Carbosian law requires every vehicle owner to carry all five coverages before a title transfers, so the 27-class matrix is Safetylife's complete personal-auto book — there is no discontinued-coverage carve-out to lean on. Whichever age class the Board votes to cut, every policy in it, across all 9 Vehicle Size × Risk combinations, is gone: every customer, every premium dollar, every claim.

Age-based risk classification is not a Safetylife invention — real auto insurers flag both young and senior drivers for different, well-documented reasons (see Appendix D). Safetylife's own risk-code key, and worked examples of how a class code is built, are reproduced for reference (see Appendix C). Additional background on the actuarial profession itself is available for the "how to…" research task (see Appendix E), and the full list of vocabulary and "how to…" questions this case draws on is in Appendix Z.

Sofia's task, and the room's, is not "which class costs the most." It is which single cut most helps Kimuni make good on a 2% revenue-growth promise he has already made in public (see Appendix A).

Appendix D — Real-World Context: Age and Auto-Insurance Risk

Safetylife and Carbosia are fictional, but the reason actuaries look at driver age at all is not. Two real, cited findings on why age-based risk classification is standard actuarial practice — not just a Safetylife policy:

  • Young drivers. The fatal crash rate per mile driven for drivers age 16–19 is just over 3 times the rate for drivers age 20 and older, based on National Household Travel Survey data (Insurance Institute for Highway Safety, "Fatality Facts: Teenagers"). Inexperience, not recklessness alone, is the standard actuarial explanation cited for this gap. [S1]
  • Senior drivers. Passenger-vehicle drivers age 85+ have a fatal crash involvement rate of 7.9 per 100 million miles traveled, compared with 1.6 for drivers age 60–64; the rate begins climbing measurably around age 70–74 (2.1 per 100 million miles). Critically, the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety attributes this rise "largely to increased susceptibility to injuries, particularly to the chest, and medical complications, rather than an increased tendency to get into crashes" — i.e., older drivers are not shown to crash more often, but crashes involving them are costlier and more often fatal (Insurance Institute for Highway Safety, "Fatality Facts: Older People," citing Cicchino, 2015). [S2]

Both patterns are exactly what actuaries mean when they say a risk class is "flagged": Young drivers by claim frequency and inexperience, Senior drivers by claim severity and injury risk — two different real-world reasons an insurer's own data (see Appendix A and Appendix B) can show two different age classes both running above the company average.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — 2019 forecast: Ĉ per vehicle (D1)

    Fit a least-squares line to each class's 12 quarters of exposure, and to its 12 quarters of claims, from Appendix A. Extend each line through 2019 Q1–Q4, sum the four projected quarters, then divide forecast claims by forecast exposure to get a Ĉ/vehicle rate. Do this once per age class.

    Tolerance is ±1% — small rounding differences in your regression are fine.

    Step 2 — Blended remaining-book rate (D2)

    Using your Step 1 forecasts (or the notebook's, if you haven't finished Step 1), compute the exposure-weighted blended Ĉ/vehicle for Safetylife's remaining book under each option: Option A keeps Middle + Senior; Option B keeps Young + Middle.

    Step 3 — The margin, as a percent (D2)

    Which option's remaining book has the lower blended rate, and by what percent lower is the better rate than the worse one? (Percent difference relative to the lower/better rate.)

    Step 4 — One risk class's own rate (D3)

    Pick any risk class from Appendix B and compute its own Ĉ/vehicle rate: Total Claims ÷ Car-Years Exposure. Do this for several classes within Young and Senior to build your D3 ranking.

    D4's chart: use the “Download CSV” buttons on Appendix A's two tables to pull the raw quarterly numbers into a spreadsheet and chart Ĉ/vehicle (claims ÷ exposure) per quarter, per class — that's your original visual.
