## Overview

Kwame Osei stands at the edge of Vantage Mesa at first light, wind snapping the survey stakes' pink flagging tape sideways so hard it hums. Behind him, a rental truck's tires are still ticking as they cool from the climb up the switchback access road. Ahead, the mesa drops away into a haze-free horizon in every direction — the kind of sky a site-selection engineer spends a career chasing. His phone has no signal up here, which is either a very good sign or a very bad one, depending on how badly he needs to call the mechanical engineer back today.

Down in the valley behind him, tucked into the lee of the ridge, sits the other option: a gentler plot at Sable Hollow, wrapped in still air and a scatter of low cloud that never quite burns off before mid-morning most days. He walked that site yesterday in a windbreaker he didn't need.

He is the site-selection engineer for a seven-university consortium building its first shared robotic survey telescope, and the lease options on both parcels close Friday. The consortium's engineering subcommittee wants one recommendation, not two shortlisted options, not a follow-up study. Kwame has spent three weeks with a wind gauge, two astroclimate databases (records of a site's historical sky and weather conditions — how often it's clear, cloudy, or windy), and a manufacturer's dome catalog spread across his kitchen table, and he still isn't sure the mesa's extra clear nights are worth what its enclosure will cost to keep standing against the wind.

His laptop is open on the truck's tailgate, two folders on the seat behind him — one labeled VANTAGE, one labeled SABLE. His phone buzzes, finally finding a bar of signal. It's the subcommittee chair.

## Background

Kwame Osei is the site-selection engineer for a seven-university astronomy consortium's first shared robotic survey telescope. He is responsible for recommending exactly one of two leased parcels to the consortium's engineering subcommittee — and for defending that recommendation with real astroclimate and construction data, not a general impression of which site "feels" clearer.

Two sites are under lease option: **Vantage Mesa**, a high desert plateau with an outstanding historical clear-sky record but persistent high wind that forces the telescope's dome enclosure to be built to a higher wind-design standard; and **Sable Hollow**, a lower, sheltered valley site with a noticeably lower clear-sky fraction but calm enough conditions that the enclosure can be built to a standard wind rating. Both leases were secured on identical terms, and the telescope itself — its optics, mount, and required dome diameter — does not change depending on which site it goes into (SEE APPENDIX C). The decision therefore turns entirely on which site delivers more usable observing time for every dollar spent enclosing and anchoring the telescope, not on which site is simply "clearer" or simply "cheaper" in isolation.

The dome enclosure at both sites is the same basic composite structure (see the labeled diagram in Appendix J): a cylindrical drum wall, a hemispherical dome cap, a conical wind-deflector screen over the shutter slit (a narrow shutter panel, like a garage door built to follow the dome's curve, that retracts backward along a track cut into the dome so the telescope can see out through the gap it leaves behind), and a pyramidal concrete pier footing beneath the telescope mount. Only the drum wall's, dome cap's, and windscreen's steel thickness differ by site — Vantage Mesa's higher design wind speed (a construction-code rating for how strong a structure must be built to survive a region's worst expected windstorm) requires thicker steel throughout the shell. No dome manufacturer publishes its own wind rating for a small enclosure like this, so this case borrows a real, well-known U.S. housing wind-speed standard as a stand-in (SEE APPENDIX D). The pier footing itself is sized identically at both sites: real observatory pier design conventionally prioritizes extra mass for vibration damping — keeping the telescope's optical axis (the straight line its mirror and camera look along) from shaking during a long exposure — over wind resistance alone (SEE APPENDIX E).

Each site's historical astroclimate record — the fraction of nights clear enough to observe, and the length of usable darkness per night — comes from a long-running, real site-monitoring program (an automated weather station and camera system that logs sky conditions every night for years — SEE APPENDIX A, APPENDIX B). Converting that historical record into a fair projection of next year's usable nights requires treating each night as an independent clear-or-not event, since neither site's monitoring program publishes a formula for "how many clear nights next year" — only a historical rate.

The lease-option window on both parcels closes in five days. After that, the consortium must begin permitting on whichever site Kwame has recommended, or lose both options and restart its site search next season.

The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in **APPENDIX H** — that is where Challenge 1 sends you. Every sourced figure and stated assumption in this case is documented in **APPENDIX F**, **APPENDIX G**, and **APPENDIX I**.

## Appendix A — Vantage Mesa: Astroclimate Record

**Modeled on ESO's Paranal Observatory** (Atacama Desert, Chile, 2,635 m elevation), a real, high-altitude, high-desert astronomical site whose sky-condition statistics are published by the European Southern Observatory's own Astronomical Site Monitor. Kwame's fictional "Vantage Mesa" is explicitly modeled on this real site's real published clear-sky statistics [S1], not presented as itself real.

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Photometric + Clear nights fraction, long-term average (2014–2024) | 77% [S1] |
| Usable-time fraction (Photometric + Clear + Thin cirrus), long-term average | 90% [S1] |
| Seasonal clear-sky fraction, April–September (winter semester) | 75% [S1] |
| Seasonal clear-sky fraction, October–March (summer semester) | 80% [S1] |
| Average usable hours of darkness per clear night (both sites) | 9.0 [MA4] |

*Plain-language notes: **Photometric** is the strictest sky-quality category — no visible clouds at all and very stable atmospheric transparency — while **Clear** is a slightly looser category (no visible clouds, but somewhat more transparency variation allowed). **Thin cirrus** is a faint, high-altitude cloud layer thin enough that many observations can still proceed through it. The **Astronomical Site Monitor** is ESO's own automated weather and sky-quality instrument package that continuously records these categories at Paranal.*

*Note: this site's monitoring program reports more than one real "how clear" figure — a 77% Photometric+Clear fraction and a 90% usable-time fraction that also counts thin-cirrus nights. This case's primary metric (Challenge D6) uses the 77% figure. Appendix B's comparison site does not report an identical set of categories — deciding which pair of numbers is actually comparable across the two sites is part of the work (see also Appendix G, MA5).*

> *Source: [S1] European Southern Observatory, "Sky Conditions" (Paranal Astroclimate / Astronomical Site Monitor), eso.org/sci/facilities/paranal/astroclimate/Obsconditions.html.*

## Appendix C — Enclosure Geometry (identical at both sites)

**Modeled on a real Ash-Dome Model R steel observatory dome** (Deep Space Products / Ash-Dome), a real manufacturer's real installed dome, 5.0 m (16'6") diameter [S4]. Both sites use a dome of this same diameter — the telescope's optics and mount do not change by site.

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Drum / dome / windscreen base radius (m) | 2.5 [S4] |
| Cylindrical drum wall height (m) | 2.65 [S4] |
| Conical windscreen height (m) | 1.2 [MA1] |
| Standard-spec drum wall steel mass, as installed (kg) | 755 [S4] |
| Standard-spec dome cap steel mass, as installed (kg) | 1,193 [S4] |

*The dome cap is a hemisphere — geometrically half of a full sphere — sitting atop the cylindrical drum at the same radius. The windscreen is modeled as a simplified cone mounted on a small flat bracket bolted to the drum/dome seam, its own flat circular base meeting the bracket's flat face — the same way a real cone-shaped accessory would actually attach to a curved wall, since a cone can't sit flush against a curve directly. This is a deliberate geometric simplification of a real accessory whose actual shape is closer to a curved or faceted panel, chosen so its material volume can be found with the same method used for the drum and dome. Wall thickness throughout this case is treated as a radial offset (outer radius minus inner radius) rather than a true surface-normal offset — a standard simplification for a thin shell whose thickness is much smaller than its radius.*

|---|---|---|
| Design wind speed (mph) | 70 [S5] | 100 [S5] |

*Derivation note: the standard-site thicknesses are back-calculated from Appendix C's real published component weights, assuming a uniform-thickness steel shell at a standard steel density of 7,850 kg/m³ (Appendix F). ## Appendix E — Pier Footing Specifications (identical at both sites)

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Footing shape | Square pyramid |
| Base side length (m) | 3.0 [MA3] |
| Height (m) | 1.0 [MA3] |

*A telescope pier's footing is conventionally sized well beyond what bearing capacity (how much weight a given soil can support per square foot before it compresses or fails) alone requires, because extra mass and stiffness damp vibration at the optical axis — a real, widely cited design principle in both professional and amateur observatory construction. This case holds the footing's dimensions constant at both sites for that reason: unlike the drum, dome, and windscreen, footing size in this case is not modeled as wind-driven.*

*A real poured-concrete footing needs formwork (temporary panels that hold wet concrete in shape while it cures) only on the faces that are not already contained by the surrounding excavation — the base sits directly against undisturbed soil and needs no form. This case's formwork cost (Challenge D4, Appendix F) therefore uses only the four triangular lateral faces' area, not the pyramid's full total surface area. Both figures are still worth computing: the full total surface area is the complete, standard application of the surface-area-of-a-pyramid formula, and the lateral-only figure is what a real construction estimate would actually price.*
