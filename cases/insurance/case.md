## Overview

The mailbox is cold enough to sting. Tiffany Sanderson pulls her coat tighter — still in the fuzzy slippers Ava picked out for her at Christmas — and jogs the gravel path back toward the house, the freezing Eagle, Idaho air biting at her ears. Junk mail, a seed catalog, and one envelope with a logo she still half-recognizes from three years of paycheck stubs.

Inside, warm again, she reads it twice standing up.

*"We are writing to inform you that your current policy will not be renewed at your next renewal date... because of your ending employment with Micron Technology, Inc."*

She already knew this was coming — she's the one who quit, a month ago, to finally build the consulting practice she's been sketching on napkins for two years. Greg knew too. But a letter makes it real in a way that a calendar reminder doesn't.

From the next room, she can hear Neri negotiating with Briggs over a puzzle piece, and Ava's tablet playing something with a laugh track. Three kids. A urologist who has to sign off on Neri every few months. A chiropractor Greg swears by. A phone somewhere on the counter, still open to a tax-credit calculator from last night.

Greg leans in the doorway, coffee in hand. "So which one is it?"

Tiffany doesn't answer yet. She sets the letter down next to two folders — one thick with three printed marketplace plans, one thin, just a single text thread from a friend about something called an HSA.

Someone has to open one of them first.

## Background

Tiffany (b. 1984) and Greg (b. 1987) Sanderson live in Eagle, Idaho, with their three children: Ava (11), Briggs (8), and Neri (3). Greg has been a stay-at-home dad for years; Tiffany spent three years as a production strategist at Micron before leaving in January 2021 to start her own consulting company. Leaving Micron also meant losing the family's employer health coverage, effective March 12, 2021.

Now self-employed, Tiffany and Greg applied through Idaho's health insurance marketplace, yourhealthidaho.org, and were approved. Their household qualifies for a real **Advance Premium Tax Credit (APTC) of $1,394.00 per month** — a subsidy that lowers whatever plan premium they owe (SEE APPENDIX A). After comparing every Gold-tier plan on the exchange, Tiffany and Greg narrowed the field to one marketplace plan their current family practice doctor, Neri's urologist, and Tiffany's women's clinic all accept: **PacificSource Navigator Gold 2000** (SEE APPENDIX A and APPENDIX C). Then, the night before this case opens, Greg's friend sent over a different idea entirely: skip the marketplace Gold plan, and instead buy a **High-Deductible Health Plan (HDHP)** paired with a **Health Savings Account (HSA)** — a different insurer, with a much lower monthly bill but a much bigger bill if something goes wrong (SEE APPENDIX A). Nothing in what the friend sent says whether this HDHP's own provider network includes any of the Sandersons' current doctors. The friend's plan is checked against the real, federal 2021 legal rules for an HSA-qualified HDHP in APPENDIX F.

The family is all non-tobacco users, and are not currently pregnant, though they've talked about trying for a fourth child this year. Neri has a diagnosed pre-existing condition — a duplicated kidney — that means quarterly visits to both the family practice doctor and a pediatric urologist, a biannual imaging test called a VCUG, and a daily generic antibiotic refilled monthly (SEE APPENDIX D). Greg sees a chiropractor every month. In 2020, the family's real medical record included three primary-care visits each for Tiffany and Greg, one urgent care visit (Greg cut his hand on a fence), one urgent care visit for Briggs (a fractured hand from the trampoline), and one emergency-room visit for Ava (24 stitches after a slip-n-slide accident) (SEE APPENDIX D) — real data this case uses to estimate what an ordinary year of unplanned care costs.

Tiffany and Greg have $74,500 in savings, and are committed to living within a budget until the new consulting business has predictable income. Every dollar figure in this case that isn't a direct citation is a disclosed, stated Modeling Assumption (SEE APPENDIX G) — none is invented quietly; every citation this case relies on is listed in full (SEE APPENDIX H). Whichever plan they pick has to survive both an ordinary year and a bad one — the vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in Appendix I, and two optional real-world articles on choosing a plan are listed in Appendix B.

---

## Dilemma

You are Greg and Tiffany Sanderson.

Do you:

A.) Buy the HDHP and open an HSA, per Greg's friend's plan OR

B.) Purchase the PacificSource Navigator Gold 2000 plan

The family qualifies for the $1,394.00/month APTC regardless of which Gold-tier marketplace plan they pick, since that credit is set by household income, not plan choice [S2].

### Appendix D — The Sanderson Family's Real 2021 Medical Care

**Neri (age 3), pre-existing duplicated kidney:**
- Quarterly (4×/yr) family-practice doctor visits, and quarterly (4×/yr) urologist visits.
- Biannual (2×/yr) VCUG imaging test.
- Monthly (12×/yr) refill of a generic antibiotic, Sulfamethoxazole-Trimethoprim 212 mg, priced at **$23.42 per bottle without insurance** — the family's own real, stated cash price.

**Greg:** monthly (12×/yr) chiropractor visit, **$134.00 per visit without insurance** — the family's own real, stated cash price.

**Tiffany and Greg's real 2020 record** (the most recent full year on file): 3 routine primary-care visits each (6 total) — real, ordinary care this case expects to recur again in 2021, the same as Neri's and Greg's other recurring appointments above — plus one urgent-care visit when Greg cut his hand on a fence (7 stitches), which is *not* expected to recur on its own and is instead one data point folded into the family-wide unplanned-incident count below.

**Ava and Briggs's real 2020 record:** Briggs fractured his hand jumping off the trampoline (1 urgent-care visit); Ava ran into a sprinkler on a wet slip-n-slide and needed 24 stitches (1 emergency-room visit).

Across the whole family in 2020, that is **3 unplanned urgent-care/ER incidents among 5 people in one year** — real data, not invented, and the basis for D3's probability-and-mean estimate of 2021's unplanned visits.

### Appendix F — 2021 HSA and HDHP Federal Rules

Real, current-for-2021 IRS rules, from Revenue Procedure 2020-32 [S8]:

| Rule | 2021 family-coverage figure |
|---|---|
| Minimum annual deductible to legally qualify as an HDHP | $2,800 |
| Maximum annual out-of-pocket to legally qualify as an HDHP | $14,000 |
| Maximum annual HSA contribution | $7,200 |

Checked against Appendix A: the friend's plan's $6,300 deductible clears the real $2,800 legal floor, and its $12,800 family out-of-pocket maximum stays under the real $14,000 legal ceiling — so it is a real, rules-compliant HSA-qualified HDHP, even though this case cannot independently verify the specific insurer or plan name (SEE Appendix G, MA1).
