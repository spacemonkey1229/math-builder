Overview

Marcus Feld has the two prototypes side by side on his bench, and for the third time this morning he reaches across each of them in turn, the way an operator would reach after setting an infusion rate — thumb landing on the Confirm control, then across to the panel's other required controls in the same motion. On his monitor, the motion-capture software still has a small red dot frozen over yesterday's test session, a tester's fingertip traced frame by frame across two control panels that look almost the same and measure completely differently. Both panels are freshly milled prototypes, and the room still smells faintly of warm plastic from the model shop three doors down.

His inbox has the tooling vendor's email open in the background tab, subject line unread twice already: time to lock the panel drawing. Once that drawing goes out, the injection-mold cavity gets cut, and there is no cheap way back — a hardware revision this far along does not get a second layout without a very uncomfortable conversation, and whichever panel he signs off on this afternoon is the one with his name on the sign-off line if a miss-rate problem ever turns up in the field.

Marcus picks up the Close Reach panel first. Bigger Confirm control, right where his thumb already wants to be. Then the Long Reach panel — smaller Confirm control, farther out, but the panel's other required controls sit closer together than they do on the first prototype, easier to reach in the same breath.

He sets both panels down, side by side, and opens the sign-off form.

Background

Marcus Feld is a Human Factors Engineer at Meridian Medical, responsible for signing off on the control-panel layout of the company's bedside infusion-therapy console before its next hardware revision goes to tooling. Once his sign-off is submitted, the panel's injection-molding cavity is cut and the layout is locked for the life of that hardware revision — any layout problem found afterward becomes a design-history-file finding with Marcus's own sign-off attached to it.

The console's post-setup confirmation sequence requires an operator to reach the Confirm control — the single most frequently used control in the sequence — and then, in the same reach cycle, the panel's other required controls (see Appendix A). Panel space near the operator's resting hand position is limited: the near-zone closest to that resting position can comfortably fit either a larger Confirm control or more generous spacing for the other required controls, but not both at once (see Appendix A).

Two fully tooled prototype panels have already been built and tested (see Appendices A and C):

Close Reach claims most of the near-zone for the Confirm control — a larger target, placed close to the resting hand position — which lowers the Confirm control's own predicted movement time and tested miss rate, but pushes the panel's other required controls farther from the resting hand position, raising their predicted movement time and tested miss rate.

Long Reach gives most of the near-zone to the other required controls instead, placing them closer to the resting hand position, but shrinks the Confirm control and moves it farther out, raising its own predicted movement time and tested miss rate.

Every control's predicted movement time is modeled with Fitts's Law, the standard human-factors model for target-acquisition movement time, using coefficients from a published ISO 9241-9 evaluation (see Appendix B). Marcus's recommendation has to be judged on one figure: the whole panel's predicted seconds of task-completion time per successful actuation, across the full control sequence — not the Confirm control in isolation, and not either option's raw movement-time total without accounting for how often an operator misses and has to try again (see Appendices A, B and C).

Every sourced and assumed figure in this case is listed in Appendices D and E, and the vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in Appendix F — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

Appendix B — Fitts's Law Model & ISO 9241-9 Coefficients

Fitts's Law predicts the movement time required to acquire a target of a given width at a given distance (Paul Fitts, 1954), and is the model international usability-testing standard ISO 9241-9 uses to evaluate pointing-device and control performance. This case uses the common Shannon-formulation index of difficulty:

ID = log2(D/W + 1), in bits  ·  MT = a + b · ID, in seconds

where D is the movement from the resting hand position to a control's center, W is that control's target width, and a and b are regression coefficients fit to real human performance data.

Value
Fitts's Law intercept coefficient, a, seconds0.6992 [S1]
Fitts's Law slope coefficient, b, seconds per bit0.1714 [S1]

These coefficients are drawn from the "touchpad + tap" condition of a published ISO 9241-9 conformant Fitts's Law evaluation (see Appendix E, S1) — a real, dated, peer-reviewed measurement of human target-acquisition performance for a hand-operated tap-to-select task, used here as the best available public stand-in for this panel's hand-reach-and-press task. The whole-panel model built in Challenge D1 applies this formula once for the Confirm control and once for each of the other required controls (Appendix A), summed into a single predicted completion time T(x) for one pass through the full control sequence. Referenced from Background and Challenge D1.

    Reveal the Twist

    Someone in the room points out that your own Step 1–3 work already found something interesting: the layout that actually minimizes raw predicted movement time isn't either prototype on Marcus's bench. It's the interior critical point x* — some allocation strictly between Long Reach and Close Reach — and on raw movement time alone (before any miss rate is folded in), it beats both named options. If a third prototype were milled at that exact allocation, would Marcus recommend it instead of either panel actually sitting on his bench?

    Before answering, remember what Appendix C actually tested: the usability lab has only ever run operators through the two named endpoints. No layout at x* has ever been built, let alone tested for miss rate. What miss rate would that third panel actually have in the field — and can Marcus's sign-off form responsibly answer D4 for a panel nobody has built?
