Case Study: The Bandstand Angle
MS | Math | Case Study | 2026-27 | Heroes Academy

## Overview

Tomas Reyes has the plat map spread across his kitchen table at eleven at night, a mechanical
pencil behind his ear and a cold cup of coffee doing nothing for him anymore. He isn't paid for
this. He's a licensed structural engineer by day, and for the last stretch of evenings he's been
the volunteer the park committee found who could actually stamp a frame drawing himself.

The matching grant is real money, and it disappears the moment the filing window closes. His phone
buzzes on the table, face-down, and he already knows who it is before he flips it over — the
committee chair, asking if the drawing is ready. It isn't.

He'd assumed the surveyor would have the corner lot's boundary lines finished by now, a clean,
current plat he could just trace a frame onto. Instead he's working from the parks department's
decade-old plat, most of its corners freshly re-flagged by the crew that came out last month —
except one, a corner near where the city shifted the curb line, that nobody has gotten back out to
re-stake yet.

Two frames are sketched on his legal pad. One is symmetric and forgiving — the kind of shape he
could hand a crew and never get a call back about a bad cut. The other hugs the lot's actual
boundary the way the committee's rendering promised the neighbors it would, and no two of its
corners are the same.

He looks at the blank signature line on the permit application that needs a stamped drawing by
morning, uncaps his pencil, and starts measuring angles.

## Background

Tomas Reyes is a licensed structural engineer and a volunteer member of the Elm Corner Park
Committee, which raised the first half of the funding for a new open-air park bandstand — a small
covered platform for concerts, meetings, and shade — on a corner lot the city donated. A matching
grant covers the rest, but only if a permit application, with a stamped frame drawing, is filed by
the end of the month. Tomas volunteered to draw the frame himself rather than pay a firm; his
stamp is what makes the drawing permit-ready.

The lot sits on a corner where two streets meet at something other than a clean right angle, and
the buildable area left after the required setbacks is smaller and less regular than the lot
itself (SEE APPENDIX B). The parks department's plat map is a decade old; a survey crew re-flagged
five of the buildable envelope's six corners last month, but the sixth, along the side where the
city recently shifted the curb line, has not been re-staked. Tomas has the other five corners'
angles in hand and must work out the sixth himself before he can finish either drawing (SEE
APPENDIX B).

Two frame options are on the table for the same roof, held to the same pitch and the same
dimension lumber so the comparison isolates the frame's shape, not its slope or lumber size (SEE
APPENDIX A). Framing lumber is priced by the board-foot (SEE APPENDIX C), and every hip rafter and
band board costs real material and, once cut, real crew time.

**The Even Eight** frames the roof as a regular octagon: eight equal sides, eight equal interior
angles, eight identical hip rafters, because every vertex sits the same plan-view distance from
the frame's center. A crew marks one hip rafter as the pattern and batch-cuts the rest — but a
regular octagon must fit inside the *tightest* part of the irregular buildable envelope, so it
covers less of the lot than the envelope could actually hold.

**The Site Six** frames the roof as an irregular hexagon fit to the envelope's own six corners — no
two sides equal, no two interior angles equal, no two hip rafters the same run, rise, or length,
since each vertex sits its own distance from the center. It captures more of the envelope than a
regular shape can, but every hip rafter needs its own template and test-fit — there is no pattern
to batch from.

Both roofs rise at the same pitch to a small flat deck at the peak, not a single point, so each
roof panel between two neighboring hip rafters is a four-sided shape (one polygon side at the
bottom, the matching deck edge at the top, two hip-rafter segments as the slanted sides), not a
triangle. The deck is identical hardware on both options and isn't part of the comparison; what
differs is only whether each panel's two hip-rafter sides match in length.

The committee's own rendering, shown to the neighbors at the last public meeting, follows the
lot's actual boundary — The Site Six. Whether that is worth what its non-identical rafters cost to
fabricate, against The Even Eight's simpler build and smaller footprint, is the number Tomas has
to defend before tomorrow's deadline. All figures here are sourced in Appendix F, or stated as a
modeling assumption in Appendix E where no public figure applies to this constructed site. The
vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case asks of you are listed in Appendix H, where Challenge 1
sends you. (SEE APPENDIX G for further reading.)

## Dilemma

You are Tomas Reyes.
Do you:
A.) Draft The Even Eight — a regular octagon frame, eight identical hip rafters, a smaller
footprint OR
B.) Draft The Site Six — an irregular hexagon frame fit to the lot's real boundary, six
non-identical hip rafters, a larger footprint
