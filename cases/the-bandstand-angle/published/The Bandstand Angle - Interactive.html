Overview

Tomas Reyes has the plat map spread across his kitchen table at eleven at night, a mechanical pencil behind his ear and a cold cup of coffee doing nothing for him anymore.

He isn't paid for this. He's a licensed structural engineer by day, and for the last stretch of evenings he's been the volunteer the park committee found who could actually stamp a frame drawing himself.

The matching grant is real money, and it disappears the moment the filing window closes. His phone buzzes on the table, face-down, and he already knows who it is before he flips it over — the committee chair, asking if the drawing is ready. It isn't.

He'd assumed the surveyor would have the corner lot's boundary lines finished by now, a clean, current plat he could just trace a frame onto. Instead he's working from the parks department's decade-old plat, most of its corners freshly re-flagged by the crew that came out last month — except one, a corner near where the city shifted the curb line, that nobody has gotten back out to re-stake yet.

Two frames are sketched on his legal pad. One is symmetric and forgiving — the kind of shape he could hand a crew and never get a call back about a bad cut. The other hugs the lot's actual boundary the way the committee's rendering promised the neighbors it would, and no two of its corners are the same.

He looks at the blank signature line on the permit application that needs a stamped drawing by morning, uncaps his pencil, and starts measuring angles.

Background

Tomas Reyes is a licensed structural engineer and a volunteer member of the Elm Corner Park Committee, which raised the first half of the funding for a new open-air park bandstand — a small covered platform for concerts, meetings, and shade — on a corner lot the city donated. A matching grant covers the rest, but only if a permit application, with a stamped frame drawing, is filed by the end of the month. Tomas volunteered to draw the frame himself rather than pay a firm; his stamp is what makes the drawing permit-ready.

The lot sits on a corner where two streets meet at something other than a clean right angle, and the buildable area left after the required setbacks is smaller and less regular than the lot itself (see Appendix B). The parks department's plat map is a decade old; a survey crew re-flagged five of the buildable envelope's six corners last month, but the sixth, along the side where the city recently shifted the curb line, has not been re-staked. Tomas has the other five corners' angles in hand and must work out the sixth himself before he can finish either drawing (see Appendix B).

Two frame options are on the table for the same roof, held to the same pitch and the same dimension lumber so the comparison isolates the frame's shape, not its slope or lumber size (see Appendix A). Framing lumber is priced by the board-foot (see Appendix C), and every hip rafter and band board costs real material and, once cut, real crew time.

The Even Eight frames the roof as a regular octagon: eight equal sides, eight equal interior angles, eight identical hip rafters, because every vertex sits the same plan-view distance from the frame's center. A crew marks one hip rafter as the pattern and batch-cuts the rest — but a regular octagon must fit inside the tightest part of the irregular buildable envelope, so it covers less of the lot than the envelope could actually hold.

The Site Six frames the roof as an irregular hexagon fit to the envelope's own six corners — no two sides equal, no two interior angles equal, no two hip rafters the same run, rise, or length, since each vertex sits its own distance from the center. It captures more of the envelope than a regular shape can, but every hip rafter needs its own template and test-fit — there is no pattern to batch from.

Both roofs rise at the same pitch to a small flat deck at the peak, not a single point, so each roof panel between two neighboring hip rafters is a four-sided shape (one polygon side at the bottom, the matching deck edge at the top, two hip-rafter segments as the slanted sides), not a triangle. The deck is identical hardware on both options and isn't part of the comparison; what differs is only whether each panel's two hip-rafter sides match in length.

The committee's own rendering, shown to the neighbors at the last public meeting, follows the lot's actual boundary — The Site Six. Whether that is worth what its non-identical rafters cost to fabricate, against The Even Eight's simpler build and smaller footprint, is the number Tomas has to defend before tomorrow's deadline.     Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Interior Angles (D2)

    Use the sum-of-the-interior-angles-of-a-polygon theorem to find Option A's angle sum and each (congruent) angle, then Option B's angle sum and its sixth, not-yet-resurveyed corner.

    D2 also asks you to state, with justification, whether each frame is convex — do that on your own worked page; it isn't something a calculator can grade for you.

    Step 2 — Hip Rafter Run / Rise / Length (D3)

    Using the roof pitch (Appendix A) and the Pythagorean theorem, compute one of Option A's identical hip rafters, then Option B's corner-1 hip rafter.

    D3 also asks you to repeat this for every remaining vertex and explain why Option A's eight hip rafters come out identical while Option B's six do not — this checker verifies one vertex from each option as a spot check.

    Step 3 — Full Framing Takeoff (D5)

    Enter your own total board-feet, total frame+site-prep cost, and covered square feet per dollar for each option (check your work against Appendix D), then hit Check.

    Reveal the Twist

    Tomas emails both drawings to the truss shop. The shop calls back: they have exactly one open slot before the permit deadline, and it's sized for a single half-day batch-cut run. The Even Eight's eight identical hips fit that slot easily. The Site Six's six unique hips — no shared pattern, each one needing its own template and test-fit — don't.
