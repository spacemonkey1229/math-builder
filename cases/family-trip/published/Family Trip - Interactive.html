Overview

The phone buzzes face-down on the kitchen counter, next to a coffee mug that went cold an hour ago. Patricia Jackson doesn't reach for it — Suzanna has just, finally, fallen asleep against her shoulder, and some sounds aren't worth the tradeoff.

Across the kitchen, Tom is hunched over his laptop, a browser tab refreshed four times in the last ten minutes. A countdown banner blinks in the corner of the page: SALE ENDS TONIGHT. His thumb hovers near the trackpad.

Down the hall, Ronaldo and Cedric are arguing about a Nerf dart — the kind of noise that used to mean nothing and now means everything, because by the time this house empties out for the holidays, it'll either be loaded into a minivan for a stretch of highway that doesn't end, or squeezed into a strip of airport seats with a diaper bag apiece.

"Hey babe," Tom says, not looking up. "Allegiant's got a sale. Ends tonight."

Patricia doesn't answer right away. She already said her piece over dinner — the money, the drive, the baby, all of it circling the table twice.

Tom glances at her. She looks back and says nothing new.

Somewhere in his back pocket, the calculator app is still open from an hour ago, its last line unfinished. The cursor blinks over the browser tab.

Someone has to close it.

Background

Tom and Patricia Jackson live in Boise, Idaho, with their four children: Ronaldo (7), Cedric (5), Ethan (20 months), and baby Suzanna (2 months). Every winter break, the Jacksons travel to Gilbert, Arizona, to spend the holidays with the Youngs — close friends since college, and the closest thing the kids have to a second set of grandparents. For as long as they've been making the trip, it has followed the same pattern: two long days behind the wheel each way, with an overnight stop in Las Vegas to break it up, in the family's own 2014 Honda Odyssey (see Appendix A and Appendix B).

This year, for the first time, an Allegiant Air sale on the nonstop route between Boise (BOI) and Phoenix-Mesa Gateway (AZA) — the closest commercial airport to Gilbert — has put flying on the table (see Appendix C). Allegiant doesn't fly this route every day, so whichever dates the family picks have to land on one of the airline's actual scheduled days.

Tom and Patricia have set a total holiday budget of $1,300 — one number that has to cover both getting the family to Arizona and back, in either direction, and their Christmas gifts for each other and all four kids. Two of the four kids, Ethan and Suzanna, are young enough to fly as free lap children (one per row, not one per adult) under Allegiant's own policy — a detail that changes the flying math more than it first appears to (see Appendix D). Tom and Patricia would also need somewhere to sleep in Las Vegas if they drive (see Appendix E).

Tom's employer gives him five business days (Monday–Friday) of paid vacation to use however he likes, on top of Christmas Eve, Christmas Day, and New Year's Day, which he already has off regardless of vacation days (see Appendix G). The Jacksons want to spend as much of that time as possible actually in Gilbert with the Youngs — especially Christmas Day itself — rather than in transit.

The Odyssey seats eight and already belongs to the family outright. Driving it doesn't cost anything extra to own — but it isn't free to run, and how you count that cost turns out to matter (see Appendix F).

Whichever option the Jacksons pick, they need a real plan: a dated itinerary, a real total cost, and a real accounting of how much of Tom's limited time off it actually costs them.

Appendix D — Allegiant Air: Fees and the Infant/Lap-Child Policy

Federal September 11th Security Fee (a government fee, applies on every U.S. airline): $5.60 per one-way flight, capped at $11.20 per round trip, per passenger [S6].

Baggage. One free personal item per passenger. Carry-on and checked bags cost extra, priced dynamically by route/date — Allegiant publishes no fixed number [S7].

Traveling with an infant. A child age 14 days–2 years flies free as a "lap child" held by a fare-paying passenger 12+ — but only one lap child per row, due to the number of oxygen masks per row — not one per adult [S8].

The Jacksons have two children under 2 (Ethan, 20 months, and Suzanna, 2 months) and two adults — exactly enough lap-child slots to fly both infants free. Only 4 of the 6 family members need a paid ticket.

These are two different, both-real ways to price a mile of driving. Gas price alone only covers what leaves your wallet at the pump. The IRS's own standard mileage rate bundles in fuel, maintenance, tires, and depreciation — the government's own estimate of a vehicle's full operating cost. Both appear in the Working Notebook for a reason.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Option A: What does flying actually cost?

    Only 4 of the 6 family members need a paid ticket (Appendix D). Compute fares + the federal fee + bags.

    Step 2 — Option B: two ways to price a mile

    Compute Option B's driving cost twice: once with gas price alone, once with the IRS's standard mileage rate. Both use the same hotel cost.

    Step 3 — Elapsed travel time

    Add both driving legs, both directions (Appendix A), to find total time behind the wheel for a round trip.

    Step 4 — Dollars remaining for Christmas gifts

    Pick your option above, then compute $1,300 minus that option's real cash cost (Option A: fares+fees+bags. Option B: gas-only+hotel — the cash a family with a minivan they already own actually spends).

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    "Everyone's cost-per-mile so far has used the price of gas. But the Odyssey isn't free to run — the IRS publishes a standard mileage rate every year: the number the federal government itself uses to estimate what a mile in your own car actually costs, once you count not just gas but maintenance, tires, and the fact that a car with 1,888 more miles on it is worth measurably less than one without them. Recompute Option B's cost using that rate instead of the pump price."

    Use Appendix F's IRS rate in Step 2's "fully-loaded" field above instead of the gas-only price, and recheck. Then ask yourself: did the gap between your two options get wider, or narrower — and does that change which one you'd defend?
