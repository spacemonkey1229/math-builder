Overview

The alert map is due in eleven minutes, and Priya Anand still has the cursor parked over two different buttons.

On her second monitor, last month’s satellite pass sits stacked under this morning’s download, and between them, right along the tree line above the Kutai concession, a thin bright ribbon of “new clearing” pixels blinks red. She has seen this ribbon before. It is never quite in the same place twice.

Her phone buzzes on the desk — Bilal, the field-enforcement lead, already up and reading her draft alerts before his coffee. Team’s fueling the trucks. Confirm the Kutai coordinates?

Two months ago she sent his rangers four hours down a logging road to a “clearing” that turned out to be the same stand of trees the satellite had always seen — just shifted half a pixel to the left. They came back at dusk, angry, having walked past three real clearings her map never flagged, because she’d told them where the software said to look, not where the forest actually was.

She has two ways to line up today’s pixels against last month’s. One takes a few minutes and a button click. The other takes the rest of the morning, a stack of surveyed ground points, and Bilal’s trucks sitting idle in the yard while she works.

The cursor doesn’t move. Behind her, an engine turns over in the lot.

Background

Priya Anand is the remote-sensing analyst for a rainforest-conservation NGO monitoring the Kutai concession and several neighboring blocks. Every month she downloads a fresh satellite scene, lines it up against last month’s scene — a step called image registration — and differences the two to produce the forest-loss alert map that Bilal’s field-enforcement team drives out on. She is accountable for two numbers that follow her every month: how many real clearings her map catches, and how many “clearings” turn out to be nothing but a shifted pixel grid.

Both this month’s and last month’s scenes come from the Sentinel-2 satellite constellation, whose finest visible-light bands (Blue, Green, Red, and Near-Infrared) have a native ground resolution of 10 meters [S1] — meaning every pixel in the scene represents a square patch of forest floor 10 m on a side. Priya’s NGO pulls these scenes free of charge from three live public portals: the USGS EarthExplorer archive, the Sentinel-2 Level-2A Cloud-Optimized GeoTIFF archive on AWS Open Data, and the Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem (see Appendix A). Because the two scenes covering Kutai in a given month are sometimes captured on Sentinel-2’s ascending orbital pass and sometimes on its descending pass, the pixel-column order can run in opposite directions between them — a fact Appendix A explains and Appendix B’s polygon coordinates depend on.

Priya’s two options

Fast Lock shifts the entire new scene by a whole number of pixels — left/right, up/down — until it lines up with last month’s scene as closely as a whole-pixel shift allows. It runs in about 20 minutes of Priya’s time, mostly spent launching the automated tool and checking its output (MA5, Appendix D) — the computation itself takes only seconds — but because real ground features rarely sit on an exact pixel boundary, it can leave true features misaligned by as much as one full pixel width (Appendix C).

True Lock resamples the new scene to sub-pixel precision using a set of surveyed ground control points (GCPs) — real, fixed features on the ground whose coordinates are known independently of the satellite (Appendix B). It removes almost all of that pixel-width smear, but the tie-point work — visually locating and marking each control point by hand in both scenes — takes about 4 hours of Priya’s time (MA6, Appendix D) — time that also has to cover every other concession she monitors this month (Appendix D). Every hour Bilal’s trucks sit fueled and idle in the yard costs the program money it does not have twice (Appendix D).

Why a shift can create a “clearing” that isn’t there

Schematic only — grid spacing and offsets above are exaggerated for visibility, not drawn to Appendix B’s actual pixel coordinates. The idea it illustrates is literal, though: a whole-pixel shift (left) leaves the two scenes’ grids offset by up to a full pixel width wherever a real edge doesn’t happen to sit on a pixel boundary, and that offset band is exactly what a naive differencing step reads as “new clearing.” Sub-pixel registration (right) shrinks that offset well below one pixel, which is why D1 and D2 ask you to work out how much of the gold sliver each method actually leaves behind.

Appendix A — Satellite Scenes & the Three Free Portals

Sentinel-2 is a two-satellite European Space Agency constellation. Its finest-resolution optical bands (visible and near-infrared) have a native ground sample distance of 10 meters [S1]; its other bands are coarser.

Sentinel-2 band groupGround resolutionBands
Visible + NIR (finest)10 m [S1]B2 (Blue), B3 (Green), B4 (Red), B8 (NIR)
Red-edge / SWIR (mid)20 m [S1]B5, B6, B7, B8A, B11, B12
Atmospheric (coarsest)60 m [S1]B1 (Coastal aerosol), B9 (Water vapor), B10 (Cirrus)

Free portals. USGS EarthExplorer (free browsing, free-account download) [S2]; Sentinel-2 L2A Cloud-Optimized GeoTIFFs on AWS Open Data (free, no account, --no-sign-request public access) [S3]; Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem (free instant access) [S4].

Ascending vs. descending passes. Sentinel-2 flies a sun-synchronous orbit (786 km altitude, 98.6° inclination) crossing the equator at 10:30 local solar time on its descending node [S5]. A scene captured on one pass and a scene captured on the other can have their pixel-column order running in opposite directions relative to true north — why Challenge D3 asks you to reflect one polygon before comparing it to the other.

This case does not require you to download or process any actual satellite raster. The portals above are the real places this analysis would start; the specific scene pair, coordinates, and accuracy figures Priya works with are given directly in Appendices B and C.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — D1: Fast Lock’s translation vector

    Reference vertex (400, 600) moves to Fast Lock’s pre-registration vertex (403.4, 597.7). What translation vector (dx, dy) was applied?

    Then decide, in writing on your deliverable: is the shifted polygon congruent to the original, or only similar? (A pure translation never changes size or angles — think about what that implies.)

    Step 2 — D2: True Lock’s resampling scale factor

    Compute the scale factor between the reference polygon’s width (720−400 = 320 px) and True Lock’s resampled polygon’s width (727.2−407.6 px).

    A scale factor this close to 1 still counts — state, with your numbers, whether the resampled polygon is congruent or merely similar, and how you can tell the difference.

    Step 3 — D3: Reflect across the y-axis

    Reflect the reference polygon’s first vertex, (400, 600), across the y-axis of the shared pixel grid.

    Step 4 — D4: Ground control point quadrant

    The GCP sits at (+1,500, −2,200) pixels from the scene center (the origin). Which quadrant is that?

    Step 5 — D5: Hectares of false positives per analyst-hour

    Using Appendix D’s per-scene analyst-hours and Appendix C’s residual figures, compute each option’s ratio for this one Kutai scene: misregistration ribbon area (ha) ÷ analyst-hours spent.

    Before you recommend an option from this number alone, reread Appendix D and Appendix E’s sensitivity note: does this single-scene ratio account for what happens to Priya’s other four concessions this month? If not, what would a whole-month version of this same ratio look like — and does it change your recommendation?

    Twist

    Reveal this only after you've finished working through the case — the Twist is meant to land after your analysis, not before.

    Reveal the Twist

    This month’s Kutai scene has a cloud bank sitting over three of the tile’s four quadrants — usable ground control points survive in only one quadrant. True Lock can only sub-pixel-correct the quadrant with surviving GCPs; the other three quadrants can only ever get Fast Lock’s whole-pixel treatment this month, regardless of how many hours Priya spends.

    If this month’s flagged ribbon sits in one of the cloud-covered quadrants, True Lock’s advertised accuracy simply is not available there.
