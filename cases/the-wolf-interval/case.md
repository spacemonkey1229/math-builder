Case Study: The Wolf Interval
MS | Math | Case Study | 2026 | Adventure


## Overview

Iris Callahan leans over the open belly of the harpsichord -- the exposed strings and tuning pins
inside the case, lid lifted -- a Baroque-era instrument that plucks its strings instead of striking
them, piano-style -- one ear an inch from the soundboard (the wooden panel that turns a plucked
string's vibration into audible sound), and plucks the D above middle C
against its dominant, A (a perfect fifth above it), twice in a row. The first time, the two strings
lock into a single glassy tone with no flutter at all -- the sound early-music players call "pure."
She holds her breath and plucks the same interval one octave higher (musicians call this move "one
register over"), in a different key, and the room fills with something else: a low, grinding beat
-- the pulsing you hear when two pitches are almost, but not quite, in tune -- like two voices
arguing instead of singing. Her apprentice, Marcus, winces from across the shop. "That's the wolf,"
he says. "You can hear it howl."

Iris built this harpsichord -- the shop calls it the Fenice -- for a period-performance ensemble
that wants their home key to sound the way it did centuries ago: pure and beatless, no compromise.
She can give them exactly that. But somewhere across the twelve keys the Fenice can be asked to
play, the purity has to come out of somebody else's pocket -- stacking perfect intervals never
quite closes the loop back to where it started. She can hide that debt in one ugly, unplayable
interval, or spread it, in slivers too small to notice, over every key at once.

The delivery truck is due Thursday. Iris opens her tuning log and stares at the blank column where
her decision goes.


## Background

Iris Callahan is the sole tuner and finishing luthier at Callahan Historical Keyboards, a small
shop that builds and restores harpsichords for professional and academic customers. Tuning means
turning each string's own small metal tuning pin, by hand, tiny fractions of a turn at a time,
until that one string's pitch matches the target exactly -- and a harpsichord this size has dozens
of strings, so retuning the whole instrument mid-concert is not something any working keyboardist
does. Every instrument that leaves Iris's bench is tuned this way, string by string, before its
first rehearsal, and the customer never gets a second visit before opening night -- whatever
temperament (the specific system a tuner picks for deciding exactly how in-tune, or how slightly
out-of-tune, each of the twelve possible notes will be relative to each other) she chooses is the
temperament they perform in.

Her current commission is a two-manual harpsichord (one built with two separate keyboards, stacked
one above the other, each connected to its own set of strings), the Fenice, built for the Cantilena
Consort, a period-performance ensemble that plays almost exclusively in the instrument's home key
(the one "home base" pitch, and the set of notes built around it, that a piece of music is written
in) of D major and its closest relatives (SEE APPENDIX C). The consort's music director was
explicit in the build contract: the home key must sound "pure," with no audible beating in its
fifths and thirds -- a fifth and a third are both intervals, meaning the distance between two
pitches; a fifth spans five letter-names of the musical alphabet (D up to A, for example) and a
third spans three (D up to F#) -- this is the sound a Baroque audience would actually have heard.

A harpsichord's strings hold only one temperament at a time. There is no re-tuning between pieces
at a concert, and no blending two systems on the same instrument -- Iris has to pick exactly one
before the Fenice leaves her shop.

Her first option is Just Intonation: tune the Fenice's fifths and thirds pure in the home key of D
major, exactly as the consort requested. Every interval in D major beats to nothing. But stacking
twelve pure fifths to reach every other key never closes back to a whole number of octaves (SEE
APPENDIX A) -- the small leftover gap has to land somewhere, and the traditional answer is to dump
it all into one deliberately sacrificed interval, the "wolf" (named for the harsh, uneven howl its
beating produces), which the consort would then have to avoid playing in entirely.

Her second option is 12-Tone Equal Temperament: divide that same unavoidable gap evenly across all
twelve fifths (SEE APPENDIX A and APPENDIX B), so every key -- including D major -- is equally
playable and no interval is ever catastrophically out of tune. The cost is that no key, not even
the consort's own home key, is ever perfectly pure. Every ratio and constant this case relies on is
sourced in APPENDIX D, and the full math vocabulary and "how to..." research list is in APPENDIX E.

The Fenice ships Thursday. Iris tunes once.


## Dilemma

You are Iris Callahan.
Do you:
A.) Tune the Fenice's fifths and thirds pure in Just Intonation for the home key of D major OR
B.) Tune the Fenice in 12-Tone Equal Temperament, evenly usable across all twelve keys

## Appendix A - Interval Ratios and Definitions

All harpsichord tuning math starts from two agreed-on physical facts about pitch, plus one
man-made convention for spreading error out evenly. All three are cited in Appendix D.

| Quantity | Definition | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Octave | frequency doubles (the "same note again," one register up) | ratio 2/1 |
| Pure ("just") perfect fifth | the acoustically beat-free fifth | ratio 3/2 [S1] |
| Equal-tempered semitone | 1/12 of an octave, by convention (a semitone is the smallest standard step in Western music -- e.g. from one piano key to the very next one, black or white) | ratio 2^(1/12) [S2] |
| Equal-tempered perfect fifth | seven equal-tempered semitones | ratio 2^(7/12) [S2] |
| Cents (the standard unit of mistuning) | 1200 x log base 2 of the frequency ratio | 1200 cents per octave [S1] |

**D1/D4 worked step.** A pure fifth (3/2) is smaller than a full octave (2/1). Dividing the octave
by the fifth shows exactly how much more is needed to complete an octave starting from one fifth:

    2 / (3/2)  =  2 x (2/3)  =  4/3

So one pure fifth covers 3/4 of an octave's frequency ratio (since 3/2 x 4/3 = 2), and the
remaining factor of 4/3 is what a second interval has to supply. This is a whole number (2)
divided by a fraction (3/2).

**D1 worked step -- why the stack never closes.** Stacking twelve pure fifths means multiplying
3/2 by itself twelve times, which equals 3^12 / 2^12. Landing on a whole number of octaves would
require 3^12 / 2^12 to equal some whole power of 2, i.e. 3^12 = 2^k for a whole number k. Factor
both sides completely:

- 3^12 = 3 x 3 x 3 x 3 x 3 x 3 x 3 x 3 x 3 x 3 x 3 x 3 -- every prime factor is 3, a prime number.
- 2^k = 2 x 2 x ... x 2 -- every prime factor is 2, a different prime number.

Because 3 and 2 are both prime and are different primes, a product built entirely of 3s can never
equal a product built entirely of 2s -- their prime factorizations can never match, for any whole
number of fifths or octaves. (3^12 itself, 531,441, is composite -- it factors as twelve 3s -- but
none of those factors is a 2, which is the whole proof.) Twelve fifths stacked can therefore never
land exactly on a whole number of octaves. The size of that permanent mismatch is calculated in
Appendix B.


## Appendix B - The Circle-of-Fifths Ledger

Musicians call the chain of pure fifths used below the "circle of fifths": start on D, go up by a
pure fifth twelve times in a row (D-A-E-B-F#-C#-G#-D#-A#-F-C-G), and the twelfth step lands back
near D again, having passed through all twelve keys exactly once. The listing order below (D first,
G last) is only bookkeeping for adding up the total -- it is NOT a claim about which physical fifth
becomes the wolf. Any one of the twelve fifths in the loop could be designated "last" and absorb
the comma instead; which one Iris actually picks is a real tuner's choice, not fixed by the
arithmetic. Historically, builders picked the fifth that sits farthest around the loop from the
key(s) they most needed pure -- for a chain built outward from D in both directions, that is the
fifth near G#/A-flat, the point diametrically opposite D, not the fifth adjacent to D itself.

**D2 -- the Pythagorean comma.** Twelve pure fifths, multiplied out:

    (3/2)^12 = 3^12 / 2^12 = 531,441 / 4,096  =  129.746337890625

Seven octaves:

    2^7 = 128

The leftover ratio (the "Pythagorean comma") is twelve fifths divided by seven octaves:

    (531,441 / 4,096) / 128  =  531,441 / 524,288  ~  1.0136433

In lowest terms this is already reduced: 531,441 = 3^12 (all factors of 3) and 524,288 = 2^19 (all
factors of 2), so by the same reasoning as Appendix A they share no common factor to cancel. In
cents, this comma measures **~23.46 cents** [S1] -- a little under a quarter of a semitone.

**D3 -- spreading the comma (Option B).** The comma's excess over a perfect 1:1 match is:

    531,441/524,288 - 1  =  7,153/524,288  ~  0.0136433

Equal temperament divides this excess evenly across the twelve fifths -- a fraction divided by a
whole number:

    (7,153/524,288) / 12  =  7,153/6,291,456  ~  0.0011369  per fifth

which is **~1.955 cents** of mistuning built into every single fifth, all twelve of them [S2].

**D5 -- the two distributions, side by side.** Which single fifth in the loop gets designated the
wolf is a real tuner's choice, not fixed by the arithmetic (any one of the twelve could carry the
comma instead, and the total/average below comes out the same regardless of which). Historically,
builders placed it at the point in the loop farthest around from the key(s) they most needed
pure -- for a chain built outward from D, that is the fifth near G#/A-flat, diametrically opposite D
on the circle. That is the convention this case adopts under Option A: the wolf sits near G#, not
adjacent to D.

| Fifth in the loop | Option A -- Just Intonation, mistuning of that fifth | Option B -- Equal Temperament, mistuning of that fifth |
|---|---|---|
| The other eleven fifths (pure, regardless of which one is designated the wolf) | 0 cents (pure, by construction) | ~1.955 cents, each |
| The fifth designated the wolf (by convention, near G#/A-flat -- diametrically opposite D) | ~23.46 cents (absorbs the entire comma) | ~1.955 cents |
| **Total mistuning, all twelve fifths** | **~23.46 cents** | **~23.46 cents** |
| **Average mistuning per key (total / 12)** | **~1.955 cents = ~0.0196 of a semitone** | **~1.955 cents = ~0.0196 of a semitone** |
| **Worst single key's mistuning** | **~23.46 cents = ~0.235 of a semitone (the wolf)** | **~1.955 cents = ~0.0196 of a semitone (every key, equally)** |

The two totals are identical because both options are distributing the exact same unavoidable
comma from Appendix A -- Option A concentrates it in one interval the consort must never play near;
Option B spreads it so thin no single key ever sounds obviously wrong, but no key, including D
major, is ever perfectly pure either. (SEE APPENDIX C for which keys the consort actually plays.)

The consort's music director sent this repertoire list with the build contract, so Iris knows

| Prelude in G major | house arrangement, after Bach | G major | 1 | 3 |
| Toccata in E major | house arrangement, after Froberger | E major | 2 | 2 |

"Steps from D on the circle of fifths" counts the fewest fifth-jumps needed to reach that key from
D, in either direction around the loop from Appendix B -- e.g. A major is one pure fifth up from D,
so it is 1 step; A-flat major is 6 steps either way around the 12-key loop, the maximum possible,
which is exactly why Appendix B's convention places the wolf there.

All six pieces are constructed, in-house arrangements for this case and are not claims about any
real recorded performance -- the key list and how often each is programmed are the scenario's own
stated facts, not sourced data (SEE APPENDIX D for everything in this case that IS sourced to a
