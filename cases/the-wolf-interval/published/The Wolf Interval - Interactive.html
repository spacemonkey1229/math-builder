Overview

Iris Callahan leans over the open belly of the harpsichordA keyboard instrument that produces sound by plucking its strings when a key is pressed -- unlike a piano, which strikes its strings with a hammer. -- the exposed strings and tuning pins inside the case, lid lifted -- a Baroque-era instrument that plucks its strings instead of striking them, piano-style -- one ear an inch from the soundboardThe vibrating wooden panel inside a stringed instrument that turns a plucked string's small vibration into sound loud enough to hear across a room., and plucks the D above middle C against its dominantThe fifth note of a musical scale, counted up from the "home" note -- the second most important note in a key, and the note a perfect fifth measures to., A (a perfect fifth above it), twice in a row. The first time, the two strings lock into a single glassy tone with no flutter at all -- the sound early-music players call "pure." She holds her breath and plucks the same interval one octave higher (musicians call this move "one register over"), in a different key, and the room fills with something else: a low, grinding beatA wobbling, pulsing loudness you hear when two pitches that are almost -- but not quite -- in tune sound together, caused by their sound waves drifting in and out of alignment. -- the pulsing you hear when two pitches are almost, but not quite, in tune -- like two voices arguing instead of singing. Her apprentice, Marcus, winces from across the shop. "That's the wolfA musical interval so far out of tune it produces a harsh, uneven beating that early musicians said sounded like a wolf's howl -- the deliberately sacrificed interval in a tuning system that can't make every key sound pure.," he says. "You can hear it howl."

This case describes a sound, not just a number. Before reading further, open Wikipedia's "Wolf interval" article and press play on its embedded audio examples ("Wolf fifth on C" and a mean fifth compared directly against a wolf fifth) -- thirty seconds of actually hearing the difference will do more than any paragraph can to make "a low, grinding beat... like two voices arguing instead of singing" concrete.

Iris built this harpsichord -- the shop calls it the Fenice -- for a period-performance ensemble that wants their home key to sound the way it did centuries ago: pure and beatless, no compromise. She can give them exactly that. But somewhere across the twelve keys the Fenice can be asked to play, the purity has to come out of somebody else's pocket -- stacking perfect intervals never quite closes the loop back to where it started. She can hide that debt in one ugly, unplayable interval, or spread it, in slivers too small to notice, over every key at once.

The delivery truck is due Thursday. Iris opens her tuning log and stares at the blank column where her decision goes.

Background

Iris Callahan is the sole tuner and finishing luthier at Callahan Historical Keyboards, a small shop that builds and restores harpsichords for professional and academic customers. Tuning means turning each string's own small metal tuning pinA small peg a string is wound around. Turning it with a special tool (a "tuning lever" or "tuning hammer," not a screwdriver) tightens or loosens the string, which changes its tension -- and a string's tension is what sets its pitch., by hand, tiny fractions of a turn at a time, until that one string's pitch matches the target exactly -- and a harpsichord this size has dozens of strings, so retuning the whole instrument mid-concert is not something any working keyboardist does. Every instrument that leaves Iris's bench is tuned this way, string by string, before its first rehearsal, and the customer never gets a second visit before opening night -- whatever temperamentThe specific system a tuner picks for deciding exactly how in-tune, or how slightly out-of-tune, each of the twelve possible notes will be relative to each other. she chooses is the temperament they perform in.

Her current commission is a two-manual harpsichord (one built with two separate keyboards, stacked one above the other, each connected to its own set of strings -- so a player can switch between two different tone colors mid-piece, or link the two keyboards together for a fuller, louder sound, the way an organist uses multiple manuals), the Fenice, built for the Cantilena Consort, a period-performance ensemble that plays almost exclusively in the instrument's home keyThe one "home base" note, and the set of notes built around it, that a piece of music is written in -- also just called the "key." Everything else in the piece is heard as a distance from this note. of D major and its closest relatives -- the handful of other keys nearest to D on the circle of fifths, sharing most of the same notes (SEE APPENDIX C). The consort's music director was explicit in the build contract: the home key must sound "pure," with no audible beating in its fifths and thirds -- a fifth and a third are both intervals, meaning the distance between two pitches; a fifth spans five letter-names of the musical alphabet (D up to A, for example) and a third spans three (D up to F#) -- this is the sound a Baroque audience would actually have heard.

A harpsichord's strings hold only one temperament at a time. There is no re-tuning between pieces at a concert, and no blending two systems on the same instrument -- Iris has to pick exactly one before the Fenice leaves her shop.

Her first option is Just Intonation: tune the Fenice's fifths and thirds pure in the home key of D major, exactly as the consort requested. Every interval in D major beats to nothing. But stacking twelve pure fifths to reach every other key never closes back to a whole number of octaves (SEE APPENDIX A) -- the small leftover gap has to land somewhere, and the traditional answer is to dump it all into one deliberately sacrificed interval, the "wolf" (named for the harsh, uneven howl its beating produces), which the consort would then have to avoid playing in entirely.

Her second option is 12-Tone Equal Temperament: divide that same unavoidable gap evenly across all twelve fifths (SEE APPENDIX A and APPENDIX B), so every key -- including D major -- is equally playable and no interval is ever catastrophically out of tune. The cost is that no key, not even the consort's own home key, is ever perfectly pure. Every ratio and constant this case relies on is sourced in APPENDIX D, and the full math vocabulary and "how to..." research list is in APPENDIX E. A separate glossary of the real-world, non-math vocabulary this case uses -- harpsichord parts, tuning terms, the acoustic cause of the wolf's "howl" -- is in APPENDIX F.

Appendix A — Interval Ratios and Definitions

All harpsichord tuning math starts from two agreed-on physical facts about pitch, plus one man-made convention for spreading error out evenly. All three are cited in Appendix D.

D1/D4 worked step. A pure fifth (3/2) is smaller than a full octave (2/1). Dividing the octave by the fifth shows exactly how much more is needed to complete an octave starting from one fifth:

2 ÷ (3/2)  =  2 × (2/3)  =  4/3

So one pure fifth covers 3/4 of an octave's frequency ratio (since 3/2 × 4/3 = 2), and the remaining factor of 4/3 is what a second interval has to supply. This is a whole number (2) divided by a fraction (3/2) -- see Appendix E for how to do that division yourself.

D1 worked step -- why the stack never closes. Stacking twelve pure fifths means multiplying 3/2 by itself twelve times, which equals 312 / 212. Landing on a whole number of octaves would require 312 / 212 to equal some whole power of 2, i.e. 312 = 2k for a whole number k. Factor both sides completely:

  • 312 = 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 -- every prime factor is 3, a prime number.
  • 2k = 2 × 2 × ... × 2 -- every prime factor is 2, a different prime number.

Because 3 and 2 are both prime and are different primes, a product built entirely of 3s can never equal a product built entirely of 2s -- their prime factorizations can never match, for any whole number of fifths or octaves. (312 itself, 531,441, is composite -- it factors as twelve 3s -- but none of those factors is a 2, which is the whole proof.) Twelve fifths stacked can therefore never land exactly on a whole number of octaves. The size of that permanent mismatch is calculated in Appendix B.

Appendix B — The Circle-of-Fifths Ledger

Musicians call the chain of pure fifths used below the "": start on D, go up by a pure fifth twelve times in a row (D-A-E-B-F#-C#-G#-D#-A#-F-C-G), and the twelfth step lands back near D again, having passed through all twelve keys exactly once. The listing order below (D first, G last) is only bookkeeping for adding up the total -- it is NOT a claim about which physical fifth becomes the wolf. Any one of the twelve fifths in the loop could be designated "last" and absorb the comma instead; which one Iris actually picks is a real tuner's choice, not fixed by the arithmetic. Historically, builders picked the fifth that sits farthest around the loop from the key(s) they most needed pure -- for a chain built outward from D in both directions, that is the fifth near G#/A-flat, the point diametrically opposite D, not the fifth adjacent to D itself.

The twelve positions, numbered. Number the chain in order: 1 D, 2 A, 3 E, 4 B, 5 F#, 6 C#, 7 G#, 8 D#, 9 A#, 10 F, 11 C, 12 G, and then back to 1 D again -- twelve evenly spaced positions arranged in a ring, each one fifth up from the position before it. G#, position 7, sits six positions away from D, position 1, in either direction around the ring (six steps going 1→7 forward, six steps going 1→7 the other way around too, since 12 − 6 = 6) -- the maximum possible separation on a twelve-position ring, which is what "diametrically opposite" means here. That fixed six-step distance is exactly why tuners traditionally place the wolf at G#: farther around the ring from D means a more distantly related key, sharing fewer notes with D major -- and a piece written in a distantly related key is used less often by an ensemble built around D major, exactly the pattern Appendix C's own program list shows directly (the keys closest to D on the ring -- 0 and 1 steps away -- are programmed 6, 4, and 3 times this season; the single piece 6 steps away, at G#/A-flat, is programmed only once). Placing the wolf at the position least likely to come up in the actual repertoire is not a guess about geometry -- it is a bet on which key gets played the least, and Appendix C is exactly the evidence a real tuner would use to make that bet.

D2 — the Pythagorean comma. Twelve pure fifths, multiplied out:

(3/2)12 = 312 / 212 = 531,441 / 4,096  =  129.746337890625

Seven octaves:

27 = 128

The leftover ratio (the "Pythagorean comma") is twelve fifths divided by seven octaves:

(531,441 / 4,096) ÷ 128  =  531,441 / 524,288  ≈  1.0136433

In lowest terms this is already reduced: 531,441 = 312 (all factors of 3) and 524,288 = 219 (all factors of 2), so by the same reasoning as Appendix A they share no common factor to cancel. In cents, this comma measures ≈ 23.46 cents [S1] -- a little under a quarter of a semitone.

D3 — spreading the comma (Option B). The comma's excess over a perfect 1:1 match is:

531,441/524,288 − 1  =  7,153/524,288  ≈  0.0136433

Equal temperament divides this excess evenly across the twelve fifths -- a fraction divided by a whole number:

(7,153/524,288) ÷ 12  =  7,153/6,291,456  ≈  0.0011369  per fifth

which is ≈ 1.955 cents of mistuning built into every single fifth, all twelve of them [S2].

D5 — the two distributions, side by side. Which single fifth in the loop gets designated the wolf is a real tuner's choice, not fixed by the arithmetic (any one of the twelve could carry the comma instead, and the total/average below comes out the same regardless of which). Historically, builders placed it at the point in the loop farthest around from the key(s) they most needed pure -- for a chain built outward from D, that is the fifth near G#/A-flat, diametrically opposite D on the circle. That is the convention this case adopts under Option A: the wolf sits near G#, not adjacent to D.

    Step 1 — D1: the prime-factorization proof

    Twelve stacked pure fifths give 312 over some power of 2. Enter the exponent on 3 and the exponent on 2 in that comparison (Appendix A).

    Step 2 — D2: the Pythagorean comma

    Using Appendix A's ratios, compute twelve pure fifths as a fraction, seven octaves as a whole number, and the comma (fifths ÷ octaves) as a ratio and in cents.

    Step 3 — D3: spreading the comma (fraction ÷ whole number)

    Divide the comma's excess fraction (531,441 − 524,288, over 524,288) by 12, the whole number of fifths, WITHOUT converting to a decimal first.

    Step 4 — D4: whole number ÷ fraction

    Divide the octave ratio (2, a whole number) by the pure-fifth ratio (3/2, a fraction) to find what's left over after one fifth.

    Step 5 — D5: the two averages, side by side

    Using your Step 2 comma-in-cents value, compute the AVERAGE mistuning per key (total ÷ 12 keys) under Option A and under Option B separately, then compare them.
