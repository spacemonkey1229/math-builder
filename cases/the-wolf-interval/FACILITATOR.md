# FACILITATOR GUIDE — The Wolf Interval

## Intro

Frame the format for the room: this is a case study, not a worksheet. Heroes read the case, do the
math themselves, and defend a firm stance to peers. There is no "the teacher will tell us the
answer" moment. Name the opener before anything else: "Iris has to tune this harpsichord exactly
once, before Thursday. Which way does she go?"

## Launch

Play or read aloud a short (thirty-to-sixty-second) audio or video clip contrasting a pure perfect fifth
with an equal-tempered one, if available (a tuning demonstration or a synthesized comparison is
easy to find or build with any tone generator). If no audio is available, read the Overview aloud,
slowly, emphasizing the sensory beat description ("a low, grinding beat... like two voices arguing
instead of singing").

## Anchor 1 — TAKE A STAND AND VOTE

No reasoning yet, just commit. "Is anyone courageous enough to stand with Just Intonation? With
Equal Temperament?" Cold-call two or three Heroes from each side to give a one-sentence gut
reaction only — no numbers allowed yet. Record the vote split; you will return to it at Close.

## Anchor 2 — WHAT IS THE PROBLEM WE'RE SOLVING?

"Is this the right question? What do you mean by 'pure'? What do you mean by 'usable'?" Let
confusion grow deliberately — a Hero will likely say "just make it sound good everywhere," and the
guide's job is to press: "Can you define 'good' with a number? The case gives you one — find it."
Do not let anyone open Appendix B yet. If the room lands on "we don't actually know what we're
optimizing," say so out loud: "Wow, this is a genuinely hard problem to even state." That stall is
the win, not a delay.

## Anchor 3 — WHAT IS THE PROCESS?

"If we go through Appendix A and B and find the numbers, how will we decide?" Push for a specific
decision rule before anyone computes anything: "average mistuning," "worst-case mistuning," "cost
to the actual repertoire"? Re-ask: "Is our question still the right question, now that we've named
a decision rule?" Most rooms will propose "lowest average mistuning wins" — let them. The Data Deep
Dive is about to make that rule collapse in an interesting way.

## Anchor 4 — WHAT DON'T WE KNOW?

"Do we need to make assumptions? Which ones are critical?" Steer toward: the case never says
exactly which key the wolf lands on (Appendix B says so explicitly) — is that a problem? Do we need
to assume anything about how the consort's season might change? (This sets up the Twist.)

## Data Deep Dive

Now, and only now, open Appendix A and B. Walk the room through the prime-factorization proof
(D1) together at the board — 3^12 has only 3s, 2^k has only 2s, they can never match — before
letting anyone touch the comma arithmetic. Then compute the comma (D2), split it (D3), and build
the per-key table (D5). The room will discover, usually with an audible reaction, that the AVERAGE
mistuning is mathematically identical for both options (~1.955 cents/key either way) — this is the
single most important moment in the discussion. If nobody notices, ask directly: "Compare your two
'total mistuning' rows. What do you see?"

## Twist

At roughly the 15-minute mark: the consort's manager calls with news that wasn't in the original
packet. Appendix C's Fantasia in A-flat major — the one piece already sitting exactly in the wolf
key under Option A's convention (Appendix B: the wolf sits at A-flat/G#, diametrically opposite D,
precisely because real builders place it as far as possible from the keys they need pure) — was
easy to write off as "just one performance, we'll manage." Not anymore: the guest violinist the
consort just booked for next month's high-profile benefit concert has built her entire feature
spot around that Fantasia. It is no longer a piece the consort can quietly drop or reschedule.
Heroes who voted A on the theory of "it's only one performance, in a piece we could always cut"
must now reckon with a piece that has become the centerpiece of the season's biggest booking.
"You have 60 seconds. Are you changing your plan — or not?"

*Guide note:* this only works because Appendix B commits to a specific, disclosed convention for
where the wolf sits (at A-flat/G#) and Appendix C already, honestly, shows one programmed piece
sitting exactly there — the Twist does not introduce a new fact about WHERE the wolf is, only a
new fact about how expendable that one piece actually turns out to be. If a Hero pushes back with
"the case never said the wolf HAD to be there," the honest answer is "correct, Appendix B calls it
a tuner's choice — and Iris chose the convention real builders use for exactly this reason: to keep
the wolf as far as possible from her most-used keys, which is what Appendix C already shows her
having done, one edge case and all."

The follow-up is the point: "What just broke in your original model? Were you wrong about the
math — or just missing information the case never promised you'd always have?" (The math was never
wrong; the assumption that the repertoire would hold still was.)

## Close

VOTE AGAIN. "Did we ask the right question?" "What math was new to you today, and how did you
attack it?" Compare the new vote split to Anchor 1's. Ask directly whether anyone changed sides
because of the AVERAGE-is-identical finding versus the Twist — those are two different reasons to
move, and naming which one moved a Hero is itself useful data for next time.

## Lessons Learned

- The Pythagorean comma cannot be eliminated, only relocated — a genuine, provable fact from prime
  factorization (2 and 3 share no prime factor), not an approximation or a design choice.
- "Average" and "worst case" can tell completely different stories about the exact same data. A
  case can be a mathematical tie on one metric and a landslide on another.
- A plan that depends on "we'll just avoid X" is fragile to any new information that removes the
  option to avoid X. Check whether your plan has that shape before committing to it.

## Standards Touched On

Prime; Composite; How to factor a number; How to divide a fraction by a whole number; How to
divide a whole number by a fraction. (Also exercises, without formally claiming credit toward
`new_coverage[]`: Exponent (also Power) and How to evaluate an exponential expression, both already
well-covered in the spine, used throughout Appendix B's (3/2)^12 and 2^7 calculations.)

## Argument for A (Just Intonation)

Option A gives eleven of the Fenice's twelve keys, including the consort's own contractually-named
home key of D major, an exact, mathematically perfect 0 cents of mistuning — literally what the
build contract specified. Option B never achieves that in any key, not even D major. Weighed
against the consort's actual programmed season (Appendix C), the D-major, A-major and G-major
pieces alone are 13 of the season's 17 programmed performances — all inside the pure zone under
Option A — and only the single Fantasia in A-flat major sits exactly where the wolf lands. A Hero defending A is betting that "avoid one bad key" is a manageable, plannable constraint
for a working ensemble that controls its own programming.

## Argument for B (Equal Temperament)

Averaged across all twelve keys, Option B carries exactly the same total mistuning as Option A —
both distribute the identical ~23.46-cent Pythagorean comma (Appendix B), an algebraic identity
forced by Appendix A's prime-factorization proof, not a coincidence of this case's specific
numbers. The purity Option A buys in eleven keys is therefore not free: it is paid for by
concentrating the entire comma into one catastrophically mistuned key (~0.235 of a semitone, about
12x worse than Option B's worst key), which the consort must permanently plan around and can never
safely use. Option B's worst key is only ~1.955 cents off — small enough that the case's own
Appendix B computation shows it as under 2% of a semitone — and every key stays usable without
restriction, including any future repertoire (like the Twist's benefit concert) the consort has not
yet programmed.

## The margin

**margin_pct = 0.0%** on the primary metric (average fraction-of-a-semitone mistuning per key).
This is not a rounding coincidence or a retuned constraint — it is a provable algebraic identity:
both options distribute the exact same total Pythagorean comma (Appendix A's prime-factorization
proof: the comma can be relocated among the twelve fifths, never eliminated or unevenly erased on
average) across the same twelve keys, so `total / 12` is identical regardless of the distribution
chosen. `sensitivity_model.py`'s `evaluate()` reproduces this identity directly rather than
re-deriving two separately-computed numbers that happen to match. The real, defensible tension in
this case is NOT on the primary average metric at all — it is the tradeoff between Option A's
perfect-in-eleven/never-usable-in-one shape and Option B's uniformly-mediocre-everywhere shape, which
Appendix B's "worst single key's mistuning" row makes visible and which the primary average
deliberately does not capture. Guides should say this out loud in the Close, not let the room
believe the tie means "it doesn't matter."

## Five Socratic questions

1. Both options divide up exactly the same ~23.46-cent Pythagorean comma (Appendix B) — so if the average mistuning per key is mathematically identical either way, what is this discussion actually about?
2. Option A gives eleven keys a perfect 0 cents of mistuning — what has to be true about the Cantilena Consort's actual programmed repertoire (Appendix C) for that to matter more than Option B's uniformly-small mistuning in all twelve keys?
3. If a single new booking forced the Fenice into the wolf key, how many cents of mistuning would Option A suddenly owe that one piece, compared to what Option B was already quietly charging every piece on the program?
4. Iris can never make the Pythagorean comma disappear, only move it (Appendix A's prime-factorization proof) — if a future customer asked for a temperament with literally zero mistuning in every key, what would you tell them, mathematically, and why?
5. Which Hero's plan is more fragile: the one banking on "we'll simply never use the wolf key," or the one banking on "a little bit of mistuning everywhere is unnoticeable" — what evidence would change your mind about either one?
6. The trap here is treating "average mistuning" as the whole answer — what does the average hide that the per-key table in Appendix B (D5) reveals?

## The trap

Heroes will notice — correctly — that Appendix B's two "total mistuning" rows are numerically
identical, and conclude that Options A and B are therefore equally good, so the choice "doesn't
matter." That conclusion is exactly the trap: it treats a tied AVERAGE as proof of tied OUTCOMES,
when the real difference is in the shape of the distribution — eleven perfect keys and one
catastrophic one, versus twelve uniformly slightly-imperfect keys. A Hero who stops at "the
averages match" has done the arithmetic correctly and still reached an indefensible position,
because they never checked Appendix C to see whether the consort's actual repertoire ever visits
the one key that Option A sacrifices. (Independently confirmed, 2026-09-17: a fresh cold-solve
subagent given only case.md reached this exact same trap unprompted and named it correctly on its
own — see QA_REPORT.md's P1/P3 findings.)

## Sensitivity note

No stated Modeling Assumption exists in this case to flex — every quantity feeding the primary
metric is either a cited mathematical definition (Appendix D, S1/S2) or a fixed structural fact of
12-tone tuning (12 fifths, 7 octaves), never an authorial estimate. `sensitivity_model.py` declares
`FLEX = []` for exactly this reason, and Tier 2 probe 5 correctly reports PASS with an empty sweep:
there is nothing this case asks a Hero to assume, so there is nothing for a guide to worry will
turn out to be wrong. This is a real, meaningful finding, not an evasion — the margin is stable not
because it survived a stress test, but because it is a mathematical identity that could not have
come out any other way.

| 15:00–20:00 | Immediate recommit: who reverses? "What just broke?" |
| 20:00–30:00 | Threaded deep conflict, 2 named threads: (1) purity-in-most-keys vs. catastrophic-risk-in-one; (2) average-tie vs. worst-case-gap — is "average" ever the right way to grade a tuning system? |
| 30:00–45:00 | Close, re-vote, reflection, Lessons Learned |
