## Overview

The generator dies at 4:40 in the morning, and in the sudden quiet Adaeze Okonkwo can hear the rain starting on the storeroom's tin roof.

She does not move for a moment. Behind her, in the dark, the walk-in refrigerator holds every dose of measles vaccine the district will get this quarter, already three weeks late. The backup generator will hold. The backup generator has always held.

Her phone lights the room green as she checks it. Twenty-two minutes until the drivers arrive. Somewhere out past the district line, in six villages strung along a week of bad road that turns to soup when it rains like this, mothers have already started walking toward clinics that have had nothing to offer them since March.

On the desk in front of her are two purchase orders, both approved, both stamped, and she is allowed to submit exactly one. One buys four small cold boxes, light enough that a single health worker can strap one to the back of a motorcycle and take it up the footpaths to Bulera and Nyamirama, where no vehicle has ever gone. The other buys two large ones — far cheaper for every litre they carry, and far too heavy for anything but the truck.

The truck cannot reach Bulera. The truck has never reached Nyamirama.

Adaeze picks up both forms, one in each hand, and outside she hears the first engine turning over in the yard.

## Background

Adaeze Okonkwo is the District Vaccine Logistics Officer for Gomba District. She is responsible for getting vaccine from the district cold store to six rural health facilities and for every dose that spoils on the way. She reports quarterly on doses administered and doses wasted, and both numbers follow her.

This quarter's allocation is **9,000 doses of measles vaccine**. It must reach the six facilities on a single outreach circuit. Once the vaccine leaves the district cold store, it is travelling in **passive containers** — insulated boxes chilled with frozen water-packs. There is no refrigeration on the vehicles. The cold is packed in at the start and it runs out.

The World Health Organization certifies these containers by a measurement called **cold life**: the number of hours from the moment the lid closes until the warmest point inside first reaches +10 °C, tested at a constant outside temperature of +43 °C (SEE APPENDIX A). Measles vaccine must be held between **+2 °C and +8 °C**; the target is the midpoint of that range. Any reading outside the range is a *temperature excursion*, and excursed doses cannot be administered (SEE APPENDIX B).

Her container budget is **$420**. The two approved purchase orders are:

**Order A — The Light Fleet:**
Four **AOV ACB-264SL** cold boxes, $102.21 each — $408.84 total. 6 litres of vaccine capacity per box, 25.4 kg fully loaded. Adaeze's team plans to send **three boxes on the truck route** (the four facilities in Appendix C reachable by road) and **one box on the motorcycle route** (Bulera and Nyamirama). At 25.4 kg, one worker can strap a box to a motorcycle — compare that against the WHO lifting guidance below before you decide how comfortable you are with it.

**Order B — The Heavy Pair:**
Two **Blowkings CB-20-CF** cold boxes, $128.00 each — $256.00 total. 20 litres of vaccine capacity per box, 49.5 kg fully loaded — far cheaper per litre than Order A, but at 49.5 kg each box needs two people and a vehicle. Both boxes go on the truck route. **Bulera and Nyamirama receive nothing.**

WHO guidance sets 25 kg as the recommended maximum a single worker should lift, and 50 kg as the design ceiling for a cold box (SEE APPENDIX A).

The circuit and the six facilities' demand are in **APPENDIX C**. Container specifications are in **APPENDIX A**. Vaccine packing volumes and prices are in **APPENDIX D**. The insulation data in **APPENDIX E** explains *why* the boxes perform differently, and **APPENDIX F** states the assumptions Adaeze's team uses when they plan a route — including exactly how the boxes in Order A are assigned to routes, so that a fresh reader of this case and Adaeze's own team will always compute the same delivery schedule.

The vocabulary and the "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in **APPENDIX G** — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

She has to submit one form.

## Appendix B: Temperature Standard

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Refrigerated vaccine storage range | **+2 °C to +8 °C** (36–46 °F) |
| Target setpoint | the **midpoint** of that range |
| Temperature excursion | *"Any temperature reading outside ranges recommended in the vaccine manufacturers' package inserts is considered a temperature excursion and requires immediate action."* |
| Required data-logger accuracy | ±0.5 °C |
| Data-logger recording interval | at least every 30 minutes |
| Maximum time vaccine may be out of the cold store during a *clinic session* | 8 hours |

> *Source: CDC, Epidemiology and Prevention of Vaccine-Preventable Diseases (Pink Book), Ch. 5, "Vaccine Storage and Handling," updated 3 April 2024. cdc.gov/pinkbook/hcp/table-of-contents/chapter-5-vaccine-storage-and-handling.html*

*Note: the 8-hour figure is a limit on a single clinic working session — how long a health worker may keep vaccine out during a vaccination day. It is not a limit on transport time. A multi-day outreach circuit is governed by the container's cold life, not by this number.*

**A note on writing the rule:** if you call the target temperature *T*, an excursion is any moment when the reading differs from the target by more than 3 °C in either direction. There is a compact way to write that with a single expression. Appendix G lists it.

## Appendix F: Modeling Assumptions

**Assumption 0 — Box assignment (this is a plan, not measured data, but it is fixed for everyone).** Order A's four boxes are assigned by vehicle, not by facility: **three boxes travel on the truck route** and serve all four road-accessible facilities between them, and **one box travels on the motorcycle route** and serves both Bulera and Nyamirama. Order B's two boxes both travel on the truck route; neither box ever leaves the truck. Use this assignment for every calculation — it is what makes your delivery schedule (D4) match everyone else's.

*How the three truck boxes split the four truck stops among themselves does not matter — you don't need to solve that sub-problem.* Even in the worst case, where one box alone absorbs all four stops' lid openings (132.3 − 4×4 = 116.3 h effective), it still clears the last stop, Kitovu, at hour 96 with 20.3 hours to spare. Divide the four stops across the three boxes any way you like; the truck leg is never the tight one.

The two items below **are not measured data.** No published source gives them. Adaeze's team uses them for planning, and this case adopts them so that everyone works from the same basis. If you disagree with either, say so in your pitch and show what changes — Appendix G's "how to" list includes the skill you'd need to test that.

**Assumption 1 — Opening cost.** WHO cold life is certified with the lid closed for the entire period. In the field, every stop means opening the box. Assume **each lid opening costs 4 hours of cold life**, deducted **up front** for every stop a box is planned to make before it ever leaves the depot: a box planned to open at two facilities starts the trip with `rated cold life − (2 × 4) = rated − 8` hours. This is how a logistics officer plans — you commit to a route before you drive it, so you budget for every stop you intend to make. Do **not** deduct openings one at a time as they happen; everyone must use the same convention or the answers will not be comparable. *(This case's headline numbers are stable for any opening cost from 2 to 6 hours — see the sensitivity note in the Guide.)*

**Assumption 2 — Holding, then failing.** A container's ice keeps the compartment inside **+2 °C to +8 °C** for its entire cold-life rating; the warming to +10 °C happens quickly once the ice is exhausted. So for this case, treat **cold life as the in-spec time**: vaccine arriving at or before the box's remaining cold life is in spec, and vaccine arriving after it is an excursion.

> This is a simplification, and it is the *optimistic* direction — a real box drifts upward through the +8 °C line somewhat before it reaches +10 °C, so true in-spec time is shorter than rated cold life. If you think that matters, model it and show what changes. A student who argues that both orders are worse than they look, and shows the arithmetic, is making a stronger case than one who doesn't notice.
