Overview

Marcus Feld is standing at the gate-scan terminal at 9:40 at night, watching the flagged-container count keep climbing on the screen, when the yard supervisor leans over his shoulder and says the sentence he was hoping to avoid: "Cutoff's in four hours, and the lane's already backing up."

Every container on tonight's sailing that got flagged during the gate scan — because a hazmat placardA diamond-shaped warning sign required on a container carrying hazardous cargo, showing which IMDG hazard class it holds. triggered the sensor, or because a reefer plug request hit the system — has to clear inspection before it can be loaded. Marcus doesn't decide how many containers got flagged. He decides how they get processed. Right now, every flagged container funnels into one lane, one crew, checking for both hazmat compliance and reefer temperature control on whatever rolls up next. It's simple. It's also slow, and getting slower by the minute.

There's a second option sitting in his terminal-ops binder, dog-eared from the last planning meeting: split the lane in two. One crew does nothing but hazmat placard and documentation checks. Another does nothing but reefer plug and setpoint checks. Faster, specialist crews — except for the containers that are both hazmat and reefer, which would have to get pulled off one lane, hauled across the yard, and re-queued on the other.

The yard supervisor is still standing there. The gate-scan screen is still climbing.

Marcus opens the manifestThe official list of every container (and its contents) carried on a specific sailing, used by customs and terminal staff alike. count on his tablet and does not press submit on either lane assignment yet.

Background

Marcus Feld is the customs and logistics coordinator on the night shift at a mid-sized container terminalThe dockside facility where shipping containers are loaded onto and off of vessels, and where cargo gets inspected before it ships.. He is personally accountable for making sure every container flagged for hazardous-materials or refrigerated-cargo inspection clears before the ship's loading cutoffThe fixed deadline before a sailing when all cargo must be loaded and documented — miss it, and the container waits for the next ship. — a container that misses cutoff gets bumped to the next sailing, which costs the terminal a contractual penalty and costs the shipper a customer relationship. Containerized cargo moves the overwhelming majority of world trade by volume — maritime transport alone carries more than 80% of goods traded worldwide [S4] — and a terminal's inspection lane is one of the few places in that whole chain where a single bottleneck can hold up an entire vessel.

Two categories of flagged cargo drive tonight's bottleneck. Hazmat-flagged containers carry cargo regulated under one of the International Maritime Organization's nine hazard classesThe IMDG Code's nine official categories of dangerous goods — see Appendix A — from explosives to corrosive substances. — from flammable liquids to the lithium-ion batteries now common in consumer electronics and e-bike shipments — and require a placard and documentation check before loading. Reefer-flagged containers carry temperature-controlled cargo and require a plug and setpoint verification before loading. (A reeferShipping-industry short for a refrigerated shipping container — one with its own built-in cooling unit, plugged into the terminal's power grid while it waits to load. is a refrigerated shipping container.) Some containers are flagged for both reasons at once: a temperature-sensitive hazardous cargo, or — increasingly, per a supplemental compliance bulletin the terminal follows — a refrigerated shipment of lithium-ion cells that also needs hazmat placarding.

Marcus has exactly two lane configurations available for tonight's flagged pool, and the terminal's operations manual requires he commit to one before the crew shift locks in. The Combined Lane runs every flagged container through one generalist crew certified to check both hazmat and reefer requirements — no re-handling, but every check the crew performs runs slower because the crew has to stay ready to switch procedures container to container. The Split Lanes run two specialist crews, each faster at its own single job — but any container flagged for both categories has to be physically pulled from one lane, re-handled across the yard, and re-queued on the other, which costs real time neither specialist check requires on its own.

Appendix A — Hazmat Classification: The IMDG Code

The International Maritime Organization's International Maritime Dangerous Goods (IMDG CodeInternational Maritime Dangerous Goods Code — the IMO's official rulebook classifying which cargo counts as hazardous and how it must be packed, labeled, and stowed on a ship.) defines nine hazard classes that govern which containers get hazmat-flagged at the gate scan [S1]:

ClassCategory
1Explosives
2Gases (compressed, liquefied, or dissolved under pressure)
3Flammable liquids
4Flammable solids
5Oxidizing substances and organic peroxides
6Toxic and infectious substances
7Radioactive materials
8Corrosive substances
9Miscellaneous dangerous goods (includes lithium-ion batteries and dry ice)

Class 9 is the category driving tonight's overlap: lithium-ion battery shipments — increasingly shipped inside refrigerated containers to protect battery performance and safety in transit — are frequently both Class 9 hazmat-flagged and reefer-flagged at once.

Appendix C — Inspection and Re-Handling Times
ConfigurationCheckTime per container
The Combined LaneHazmat check (generalist crew)8 minutes
The Combined LaneReefer check (generalist crew)7 minutes
The Split LanesHazmat check (specialist crew)6 minutes
The Split LanesReefer check (specialist crew)5 minutes
The Split LanesRe-handle (crane pull, yard move, re-queue) — dual-flagged containers only11 minutes

In The Combined Lane, a container flagged for only one category gets only that one check; a container flagged for both gets both checks back-to-back, in the same lane, with no re-handling. In The Split Lanes, a container flagged for only one category goes to that one specialist lane; a container flagged for both must complete a check in one lane, then be re-handled to the other lane — paying the specialist lane's faster check times, plus the 11-minute re-handle cost. All five times are disclosed Modeling Assumptions — see Appendix D, MA2–MA6.
