Overview

Priya Chandrasekaran kneels in the wet grass at the edge of a fence line outside Gardiner, pulls her jacket tighter against the mountain air, and looks at two iron pins sitting a few inches apart in the dirt. One is old, rust-streaked, driven decades ago. The other is bright and new, its cut edges still sharp. Only one of them can be the real corner of the parcel behind her — the other is either a careless mistake or somebody's quiet attempt to move a fence line.

The contractor is due early this morning to start digging post holes, and he is not going to wait for a philosophical debate about which pin looks more official. Priya's job is not to guess. It is to read the old recorded plat, read the angles the ground itself is actually giving her, and let the geometry — not the rust, not the shine — decide which pin the original surveyor really meant.

She sets up the total station on the tripod, levels it, and sights back along the boundary she already trusts. Two intersecting lines, two candidate corners, and a plat that quietly encodes the answer in a single recorded angle at the corner nobody disputes. Priya has done this dozens of times. She has also been wrong before, which is exactly why she does the math instead of trusting her gut.

Background

Priya Chandrasekaran is a deputy county surveyor for Park County, Montana, called out to resolve a boundary dispute on a parcel just outside Gardiner before a fencing contractor breaks ground on the wrong line. Two candidate iron pins — an older, weathered Pin 1 and a newer Pin 2 — sit a few inches apart near where the parcel's recorded plat says its southwest corner should be. Only one of them is the corner the original 1962 plat actually describes.

Every professional land survey in the US ultimately ties back to the same federal control network (see Appendix A for a real, publicly published example monument near this parcel). The recorded plat is genuinely useful here, because rural boundary plats are drawn with a real geometric convention: this parcel's north fence line and south fence line are recorded as parallel to each other, and the parcel's west boundary — the line connecting its northwest and southwest corners — is the transversal line that crosses both of them (see Appendix B). The northwest corner's monument is intact and undisputed, with a plat-recorded interior angle. Because two parallel lines cut by a transversal create a fixed, provable relationship between the angles at each crossing, that undisputed northwest angle is enough to calculate the expected interior angle at the disputed southwest corner — before Priya ever measures a single candidate pin.

Each candidate pin gets its own total-station shot, tying it back to the same boundary line Priya already trusts (see Appendix C). That shot and the boundary line are two intersecting lines meeting at the corner stake, and the actual interior angle at each candidate pin has to be worked out from the raw field reading using the real relationships between angles formed at an intersection — not just read off the instrument directly.

As a second, independent check, Priya also ties each candidate pin and the undisputed northwest corner to a nearby witness point with a separate pair of shots, forming a triangle for each candidate (see Appendix D). Classifying each triangle — by its angles and by whether any of its sides could possibly match — is a second, independent piece of evidence a good surveyor checks before recommending which pin to trust.

The total station gives Priya a distance along with every angle shot, and the 1962 plat itself records an approximate boundary length as a check figure — so Priya also has a straight-line distance from the undisputed northwest corner to each candidate pin, to compare against what the plat recorded (see Appendix C). This distance check and the angle check do not agree on which pin is the closer match, which is exactly the kind of real conflict a surveyor has to weigh: a rough, overgrown fence line makes a distance shot more exposed to real-world measuring error (brush, slope, an uneven tape or laser path) than a single clean angle shot from a leveled instrument, which is why Priya treats the angle check as the deciding evidence rather than the distance check — but a good pitch should still address why, not just report both numbers.

The pin whose actual angle comes closest to the plat's expected angle is the real corner. At each candidate pin, the total station's backsight ray (aimed back along the trusted boundary line) and the foresight shot to the candidate pin form two intersecting lines. The instrument records the angle on the north side of that intersection; the parcel's own interior corner angle is on the south (interior) side — the two sides of a straight backsight line are supplementary angles that always add to 180° (not the vertical angles formed across the same intersection, which sit opposite the recorded angle instead of alongside it). See Challenge D2, D3.

The distance rows are a genuine, real conflict: Pin 1's measured distance is only a few feet off the plat's recorded boundary length, while Pin 2's is off by more — the distance check favors Pin 1, even though the angle check favors Pin 2. This is not a mistake in the data; it is the actual disagreement Priya has to weigh.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 · The Plat's Expected Corner Angle (D1)

    Appendix B records the northwest corner's interior angle, and the north/south fence lines are parallel, cut by the west-boundary transversal. Use the co-interior (same-side interior) angle relationship to find the interior angle the plat expects at the disputed southwest corner, in degrees.

    Step 2 · Pin 1's Actual Corner Angle (D2)

    Appendix C gives Pin 1's total-station backsight-to-shot angle. The backsight ray and the interior corner angle are supplementary angles across the same straight line. Find Pin 1's actual interior corner angle, in degrees.

    Step 3 · Pin 2's Actual Corner Angle (D3)

    Do the same for Pin 2's total-station backsight-to-shot angle (Appendix C). Find Pin 2's actual interior corner angle, in degrees.

    Step 4 · The Pin 1 Witness Triangle (D4)

    Appendix D gives two of the Pin 1 witness triangle's three angles. Find the third, then classify the triangle by its angles and by whether any of its sides could match.

    Step 5 · The Pin 2 Witness Triangle (D5)

    Do the same for the Pin 2 witness triangle (Appendix D): find its third angle, then classify it by angle and by side.

    Step 6 · The Angular Deviation (D6)

    Using D1's expected angle and D2/D3's actual angles, find how far each pin's actual corner angle is from the plat's expected angle — an absolute value expression, so the result is never negative. If you've revealed the Twist below and checked the box there, this re-checks against the corrected reading.

    Step 7 · The Verdict (D7)

    Using D6's two deviations, find the percentage margin between them — how much smaller the winning deviation is than the losing one, as a percent — and recommend which pin is the true corner. If you've applied the Twist, this checks against the corrected margin instead.
    Explore MA1's sensitivity. Appendix F's MA1 says the northwest angle's 3° excess past square carries real recording uncertainty. Drag the slider to flex that excess ±50% and watch the plat's expected angle and the margin recompute live — nothing here is fixed, it's calculated fresh every time you move it.
