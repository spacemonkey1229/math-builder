Case Study: Survey Corner
MS | Math | Case Study | 2026-27 | Heroes Academy

## Overview

Priya Chandrasekaran kneels in the wet grass at the edge of a fence line outside Gardiner, pulls
her jacket tighter against the mountain air, and looks at two iron pins sitting eleven inches
apart in the dirt. One is old, rust-streaked, driven decades ago. The other is bright and new,
its cut edges still sharp. Only one of them can be the real corner of the parcel behind her — the
other is either a careless mistake or somebody's quiet attempt to move a fence line.

The contractor is due at seven this morning to start digging post holes, and he is not going to
wait for a philosophical debate about which pin looks more official. Priya's job is not to guess.
It is to read the old recorded plat, read the angles the ground itself is actually giving her, and
let the geometry — not the rust, not the shine — decide which pin the original surveyor really
meant.

She sets up the total station on the tripod, levels it, and sights back along the boundary she
already trusts. Two intersecting lines, two candidate corners, and a plat that quietly encodes
the answer in a single recorded angle at the corner nobody disputes. Priya has done this dozens of
times. She has also been wrong before, which is exactly why she does the math instead of trusting
her gut.

## Background

Priya Chandrasekaran is a deputy county surveyor for Park County, Montana, called out to resolve a
boundary dispute on a parcel just outside Gardiner before a fencing contractor breaks ground on the
wrong line. Two candidate iron pins — an older, weathered **Pin 1** and a newer **Pin 2** — sit a
few inches apart near where the parcel's recorded plat says its southwest corner should be. Only
one of them is the corner the original 1962 plat actually describes.

Every professional land survey in the US ultimately ties back to the same federal control network
(SEE APPENDIX A for a real, publicly published example monument near this parcel). The recorded
plat is genuinely useful here, because rural boundary plats are drawn with a real
geometric convention: this parcel's north fence line and south fence line are recorded as
**parallel** to each other, and the parcel's west boundary — the line connecting its northwest and
southwest corners — is the **transversal** that crosses both of them (SEE APPENDIX B). The
northwest corner's monument is intact and undisputed, with a plat-recorded interior angle. Because
two parallel lines cut by a transversal create a fixed, provable relationship between the angles at
each crossing, that undisputed northwest angle is enough to calculate the *expected* interior angle
at the disputed southwest corner — before Priya ever measures a single candidate pin.

Each candidate pin gets its own total-station shot, tying it back to the same boundary line Priya
already trusts (SEE APPENDIX C). That shot and the boundary line are two intersecting lines meeting
at the corner stake, and the *actual* interior angle at each candidate pin has to be worked out from
the raw field reading using the real relationships between angles formed at an intersection — not
just read off the instrument directly.

As a second, independent check, Priya also ties each candidate pin and the undisputed northwest
corner to a nearby witness point with a separate pair of shots, forming a triangle for each
candidate (SEE APPENDIX D). Classifying each triangle — by its angles and by whether any of its
sides could possibly match — is a second, independent piece of evidence a good surveyor checks
before recommending which pin to trust.

The total station gives Priya a distance along with every angle shot, and the 1962 plat itself
records an approximate boundary length as a check figure — so Priya also has a straight-line
distance from the undisputed northwest corner to each candidate pin, to compare against what the
plat recorded (SEE APPENDIX C). This distance check and the angle check do not agree on which pin
is the closer match, which is exactly the kind of real conflict a surveyor has to weigh: a rough,
overgrown fence line makes a distance shot more exposed to real-world measuring error (brush,
slope, an uneven tape or laser path) than a single clean angle shot from a leveled instrument, which
is why Priya treats the angle check as the deciding evidence rather than the distance check — but a
good pitch should still address why, not just report both numbers.

The pin whose actual angle comes closest to the plat's expected angle is the real corner. All
figures in this case are sourced in APPENDIX E, or stated as explicit modeling assumptions in
APPENDIX F where no public figure exists. Curious about how real total stations, geodetic control
networks, and boundary resurveys actually work beyond this case's math (SEE APPENDIX G for further
reading). The vocabulary and the "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in
APPENDIX H — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

## Appendix B — The Recorded Plat

| | Value |
|---|---|
| North fence line and south fence line | Recorded as parallel |
| West boundary (NW corner to SW corner) | The transversal crossing both fence lines |
| Northwest corner interior angle (undisputed, plat-recorded) | 93° |

Because two parallel lines cut by a transversal create same-side (co-interior) angles that always
add up to 180°, the northwest corner's angle and the disputed southwest corner's expected angle are
directly related — SEE CHALLENGE D1.

## Appendix C — Corner Intersection Field Notes

| | Value |
|---|---|
| Total-station backsight-to-shot angle, Pin 1 | 88.5° |
| Total-station backsight-to-shot angle, Pin 2 | 89.0° |
| Recorded plat boundary length (NW corner to SW corner, check figure) | 245.0 ft |
| Measured distance, NW corner to Pin 1 | 247.8 ft |
| Measured distance, NW corner to Pin 2 | 251.5 ft |

At each candidate pin, the total station's backsight ray (aimed back along the trusted boundary
line) and the foresight shot to the candidate pin form two intersecting lines. The instrument
records the angle on the north side of that intersection; the parcel's own interior corner angle is
on the south (interior) side — the two sides of a straight backsight line always add to 180°.
SEE CHALLENGE D2, D3.

The distance rows are a genuine, real conflict: Pin 1's measured distance (247.8 ft) is only 2.8 ft
off the plat's recorded 245.0 ft, while Pin 2's (251.5 ft) is 6.5 ft off — the *distance* check
favors Pin 1, even though the *angle* check favors Pin 2. This is not a mistake in the data; it is
the actual disagreement Priya has to weigh.

## Appendix G — Learn More

Real, currently-live resources for a Hero who wants to go beyond this case's required math:

- ["Total station" — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_station). Explains, in more
  technical depth than this case needs, how a real total station combines a theodolite and
  electronic distance measurement to compute precise angles and distances in the field.
- ["Land surveying" — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_surveying). Covers how real
  land surveyors establish and re-establish property boundaries from recorded deeds/plats and
  physical monuments — the same kind of boundary-resolution work Priya is doing in this case.
- ["Parallel Lines" — Math Is Fun](https://www.mathsisfun.com/geometry/parallel-lines.html). A
  clear walkthrough of the angle relationships (co-interior, alternate interior, corresponding)
  created when a transversal crosses two parallel lines — directly useful for Challenge D1.
- [NGS Datasheets — National Geodetic Survey](https://www.ngs.noaa.gov/datasheets/). The real,
  public lookup tool for the same kind of federal survey-monument datasheet cited in Appendix A —
  a Hero can search for real benchmarks near their own town.
