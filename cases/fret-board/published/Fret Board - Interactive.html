Overview

Yuki Tanaka runs a strip of masking tape down the guitar neck clamped in the workshop vise, marking where the first fret line has to land. Sawdust hangs in the light from the shop's one window, and somewhere behind her a radio is playing too quietly to make out the words. This is her first solo fret job — no one checking her marks before the saw touches wood.

The client is due back for the instrument before the week is out. Yuki's mentor left two sticky notes on the bench this morning before heading to a supplier meeting across town: one with a string of decimals under the header “exact,” the other with a short column of simple fractions under “shop method.” Both get you a playable neck. Only one of them is what she was actually taught to check her work against.

She sets her calculator down next to the fret rule and the calipers, glances at the clock, and picks up the pencil.

Background

Yuki Tanaka is an apprentice at a small custom-guitar workshop that builds and repairs fretted instruments to order. She has assisted on fret jobs for two years but has never marked and cut a fingerboard on her own for a paying client. The shop's owner has told her plainly: a fret placed wrong enough to throw the instrument out of tune is not a small fix. Pulling a fret, filling the slot, and re-cutting it costs roughly a week of unpaid rework on top of whatever else is on her bench (see Appendix F).

Every fret on a guitar neck marks the point where the string, stopped short by a metal bar, produces one semitone higher in pitch than the fret before it. Modern instruments are built to equal temperament: twelve equally-spaced semitones per octave, where doubling the vibrating string length drops the pitch by exactly one octave (see Appendix A). Two methods exist for finding where those twelve fret lines actually go on the neck:

Option A — The Precision Method. Compute each fret's distance from the nut using the exact twelfth-root-of-two ratio that defines equal temperament, carried to four decimal places at every fret (see Appendix B).

Option B — The Rule of Eighteen. Use the traditional luthier's shortcut passed down in the shop: divide the remaining string length by a fixed whole-number divisor at each fret, a method generations of luthiers used before calculators were sitting on every bench (see Appendix C).

The shop's scale length, fret count, and Yuki's client deadline are fixed constraints, not choices (see Appendix F). Both methods finish comfortably inside that same five-day deadline, but they do not cost the shop the same number of minutes — computing each fret's Precision Method position by hand takes a little longer than the Rule of Eighteen's simpler running division, though that gap is small next to the time every job spends, regardless of method, cutting, installing, and dressing the frets once they're marked (see Appendix F). What also differs is how far each method's fret positions drift from true equal temperament, and whether that drift stays inside what a professional's ear — and a client's tuner — will accept (see Appendix D). Fret-position drift can also be read as a string-length ratio problem: reducing the open-to-fretted length ratio at each fret shows which frets, if any, land on the kind of simple whole-number ratio a musician's ear recognizes as a clean interval (see Appendix E). Every sourced or derived figure in this case is documented, with its source and confidence, in Appendix G; the handful of figures with no public source are stated openly as Modeling Assumptions rather than invented.

The vocabulary and the “how to…” skills this case will ask of you are listed in Appendix H — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

Appendix A — Equal Temperament, Defined

The modern Western fretted instrument is built on equal temperament: one octave (a 2:1 ratio of vibrating string length) is divided into twelve equal semitone steps, geometrically rather than in equal-sized pieces. Moving up one fret always multiplies the remaining vibrating string length by the same constant ratio.

Appendix D — Cents of Deviation and Intonation Tolerance

Musicians measure pitch difference in cents: 1200 cents to an octave, 100 cents to one equal-tempered semitone. cents = 3986.3 × log10(ratio), where ratio is the true equal-tempered string length (or frequency) divided by the approximated one.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Precision Method fret positions (D1)

    Compute distance(n) = L × [1 − (1/2)^(n/12)] for frets 1–12, to four decimal places. L = 25.5000 in.

    Step 2 — Rule of Eighteen fret positions (D2)

    At each fret, spacing = (L − previous distance) ÷ 18, applied iteratively from the nut. Same frets 1–12.

    Step 3 — Cents of deviation at fret 12 (D3)

    Use your own fret-12 answers from Steps 1 & 2 (or fill them in below) to find the remaining string length each method leaves at fret 12, then convert the ratio of those two lengths to cents: cents = 3986.3 × log10(ratio).

    Step 4 — GCF string-length-ratio table, frets 1–5 (D4)

    Reduce each fret's open : fretted length ratio (ten-thousandths of an inch) to lowest terms using the greatest common factor.

    Step 5 — Total shop time and recommendation (D5)

    Reveal the Twist

    It's day three. Yuki has now marked and cut two other necks using the Precision Method while she waited on glue to cure on this one, and something has changed: she's not fumbling the fractional-exponent keystrokes anymore. What used to take her a slow, careful 20 seconds a fret now takes her about half that — she's found a rhythm on the calculator, punching in n/12 without hunting for the right button.

    Nothing about the shop's build spec changed. The scale length is still 25.5000 in, the neck still needs 22 frets, and the shared cutting/installing/dressing time is still whatever it is. What changed is Yuki herself — the "cost" of the Precision Method's extra arithmetic was never a fixed law of nature, it was how fast this apprentice, on this day could work through it.
