Overview

Renata Koh has forty minutes before the capital review board wants her recommendation memo, and the two spec binders in front of her are still open to the same page: rotation time, thirty minutes, both of them, printed in identical bold type like the manufacturers copied off each other's homework. She hasn't touched her coffee. Somewhere down the hall a printer is jamming on someone else's agenda packet, and every few minutes a staffer walks past her open door a little too slowly, trying to read her face for which way she's leaning.

She isn't leaning anywhere yet. Both binders sit under a single yellow legal pad on which she has drawn, twice now, the same lopsided circle with an arrow through it — a wheel, a center line, a horizon. Somewhere in that circle is the answer to a question the board has been asking for three months: which observation wheelthe modern engineering term for a large Ferris wheel built for scenic viewing design Bellwether Point builds.

Background

Renata Koh is the attractions engineer for the Bellwether Point Pier Redevelopment Authority, a public agency rebuilding a storm-damaged pier into the anchor of a new waterfront district. The board has asked her to score two competing design proposals and bring a single recommendation to Thursday's vote — she does not choose the winner herself, but the board has never overridden her recommendation memo in the agency's history, so in every way that matters, this call is hers.

Before she was an engineer, Renata spent six years running the ride panel at a regional amusement park — loading capsules, watching the sky for lightning, learning the difference between a wheel that feels smooth to a rider and one that merely tests smooth on paper — before going back to school for her engineering degree. She still trusts what her hands remember over what a spec sheet promises. It's a running joke among her colleagues that she has now designed the sightlines for three different sunset-view wheels and, across eleven years of working actual evening shifts, has caught the view from the top of exactly zero of them — somebody has to watch the loading gate while the tourists watch the horizon. That ground-level instinct is why she reads a spec sheet the way she does: not for which wheel sounds more impressive, but for which one actually delivers what it promises to the rider standing in the capsule, not just to the board approving the drawing.

Both proposals come from real, already-built reference installationsthe real, already-built wheel whose published specs a manufacturer uses to describe a licensed copy, licensed to Bellwether Point by the manufacturers who engineered them. Option A is built to the reference specifications of the Thames-side observation wheel design (Appendix A). Option B is built to the reference specifications of the Las Vegas Strip observation wheel design (Appendix B).

The centerpiece of the pier's marketing plan is a sunset-view guarantee: every ticket sold for the evening session promises riders a continuous, unobstructed view above Bellwether Point's own waterfront skyline during golden hourthe period shortly before sunset when light is warm and low-angled, timed to the wheel's own rotation. The authority's engineering charter states the qualifying sightline height a rider must clear for that guarantee to hold (Appendix C).

Because the two designs differ enormously in scale, and because a taller, wider wheel is well-established engineering knowledge to cost far more than a proportional multiple of a smaller one — steel tonnage and foundation loadthe total weight and force a structure transfers into the ground both grow faster than the wheel's own diameter — the authority's own capital-reviewthe public body that approves how an agency spends money on a large project policy does not score a design on raw output alone. It scores each design on how much qualifying sunset-view time it delivers per meter of wheel diameter built. Renata's memo has to report that score for both options, honestly, alongside the raw totals.

  3. Your pitch must include:
    • D1. A height-versus-time function h(t) for each option, in the form h(t) = D − R·cos(ωt), with the amplitude, midline, and period each labeled and justified from that option's real diameter, boarding clearance, and rotation time (Appendices A, B and C).
    • D2. A degrees-to-radians conversion table showing each option's angular rotation rate ω in both degrees per minute and radians per minute, with the conversion shown step by step (Appendix D).

    • D5. A final scoring table reporting, for each option: total qualifying sunset-view minutes delivered per operating hour, and that same total divided by the option's wheel diameter (the authority's own per-meter scoring rule from Background) — with your recommendation stated and defended using that score.

Twist

Twist: N/A, justified

Pier Wheel doesn't carry a scripted mid-discussion twist (SPEC.md's Twist section allows "Twist: N/A," explicitly justified). This case's tension already turns on reading Background's scoring rule correctly — the authority scores each design on qualifying sunset-view minutes per meter of diameter, not on raw minutes delivered — rather than on new information withheld until mid-discussion. A late reveal would compete with, not sharpen, that structural read: the whole pedagogical weight already rests on whether the room notices the per-meter rule, not on a fact nobody had yet. No twist is dropped anywhere in the 45-minute facilitator plan for this reason.

A future revision could add a twist where the board reveals a hard construction-footprint or foundation-load limit that structurally rules out Option B regardless of score — deliberately not added here so the case's whole weight sits on the scoring-rule read (see FACILITATOR.md's Common Trap and Twist notes).
