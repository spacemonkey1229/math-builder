## Overview

Renata Koh has forty minutes before the capital review board wants her recommendation memo, and
the two spec binders in front of her are still open to the same page: rotation time, thirty
minutes, both of them, printed in identical bold type like the manufacturers copied off each
other's homework. She hasn't touched her coffee. Somewhere down the hall a printer is jamming on
someone else's agenda packet, and every few minutes a staffer walks past her open door a little
too slowly, trying to read her face for which way she's leaning.

She isn't leaning anywhere yet. Both binders sit under a single yellow legal pad on which she has
drawn, twice now, the same lopsided circle with an arrow through it — a wheel, a center line, a
horizon. Somewhere in that circle is the answer to a question the board has been asking for three
months: which observation-wheel design does Bellwether Point build.

Renata pulls the legal pad toward her, uncaps a pen, and writes a variable at the top of a blank
page: *h(t) =*.

She stops there, pen still on the paper, and looks up at the clock.

## Background

Renata Koh is the attractions engineer for the Bellwether Point Pier Redevelopment Authority, a
public agency rebuilding a storm-damaged pier into the anchor of a new waterfront district. The
board has asked her to score two competing design proposals and bring a single recommendation to
Thursday's vote — she does not choose the winner herself, but the board has never overridden her
recommendation memo in the agency's history, so in every way that matters, this call is hers.

Before she was an engineer, Renata spent six years running the ride panel at a regional amusement
park — loading capsules, watching the sky for lightning, learning the difference between a wheel
that feels smooth to a rider and one that merely tests smooth on paper — before going back to
school for her engineering degree. She still trusts what her hands remember over what a spec sheet
promises. It's a running joke among her colleagues that she has now designed the sightlines for
three different sunset-view wheels and, across eleven years of working actual evening shifts, has
caught the view from the top of exactly zero of them — somebody has to watch the loading gate
while the tourists watch the horizon. That ground-level instinct is why she reads a spec sheet the
way she does: not for which wheel sounds more impressive, but for which one actually delivers what
it promises to the rider standing in the capsule, not just to the board approving the drawing.

Both proposals come from real, already-built reference installations, licensed to Bellwether
Point by the manufacturers who engineered them — the authority is not inventing a wheel from
scratch, it is choosing which proven design family to build a version of (SEE APPENDICES A AND
B). **Option A** is built to the reference specifications of the Thames-side observation wheel
design (SEE APPENDIX A). **Option B** is built to the reference specifications of the Las
Vegas Strip observation wheel design (SEE APPENDIX B). Every rider-height and rotation-timing
figure in this case comes directly from each design's real, documented reference installation.

The centerpiece of the pier's marketing plan is a **sunset-view guarantee**: every ticket sold for
the evening session promises riders a continuous, unobstructed view above Bellwether Point's own
waterfront skyline during golden hour, timed to the wheel's own rotation. The authority's
engineering charter states the qualifying sightline height a rider must clear for that guarantee
to hold (SEE APPENDIX C) — the height needed to see clean over the boardwalk roofline and the
dune-top tree line beyond it, not just glimpse between buildings.

Because the two designs differ enormously in scale, and because a taller, wider wheel is
well-established engineering knowledge to cost far more than a proportional multiple of a smaller
one — steel tonnage and foundation load both grow faster than the wheel's own diameter — the
authority's own capital-review policy does not score a design on raw output alone. It scores each
design on how much qualifying sunset-view time it delivers **per meter of wheel diameter built**,
treating diameter as the policy's stand-in for the capital commitment a bigger structure actually
requires. Renata's memo has to report that score for both options, honestly, alongside the raw
totals so the board can see the whole picture (SEE APPENDICES A, B, C AND D).

Every figure in this case is either cited to a real, public source or stated openly as a scenario
assumption (SEE APPENDIX E); every source is listed in **APPENDIX F**. The vocabulary and "how
to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in **APPENDIX H** — that is where Challenge 1
sends you. (SEE APPENDIX G for the ride-engineering vocabulary that makes the decision itself make
sense.)

## Dilemma

You are Renata Koh.
Do you:
A.) Recommend Option A — the Thames-side reference design, built to a smaller diameter OR
B.) Recommend Option B — the Las Vegas Strip reference design, built to a much larger diameter

## Appendix C — The Sunset-View Requirement

Bellwether Point's engineering charter requires every evening-session rider to clear a stated
qualifying sightline height, measured from ground level, before the sunset-view guarantee applies
to that portion of the ride (SEE APPENDIX E, MA1). Both options' height functions in D1 measure
rider height from the same ground datum, so both can be checked against the same qualifying
height directly.

Boarding for both options happens at a small fixed platform above ground level, not at true
ground datum zero (SEE APPENDIX E, MA2) — this sets the minimum value each option's height
function can reach.

## Appendix G — Learn More

This case also uses real ride-engineering and attractions-industry terms that are not math
vocabulary but that a reader who has never worked in attractions design may not know. None of
these change any number in this case; they are here so the engineering itself makes sense.

- **Observation Wheel** — the modern engineering term for a large Ferris wheel built primarily
  for scenic viewing rather than a traditional fairground ride; usually taller, slower, and with
  enclosed or larger passenger capsules than a traveling carnival wheel. See [Wikipedia,
  "Observation wheel"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observation_wheel).
- **Capsule / Cabin** — the enclosed passenger compartment mounted on the rim of an observation
  wheel; the two options in this case use different manufacturer terms for the same kind of part.
  See [Wikipedia, "Observation wheel"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observation_wheel).
- **Continuous Loading** — a boarding method where the wheel never fully stops; it slows to a
  crawl so riders can step on and off a moving capsule at ground level. Both options in this case
  load this way. See [Wikipedia, "Ferris wheel"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferris_wheel).
- **Reference Installation** — the real, already-built wheel whose published specifications a
  manufacturer uses to describe what a new, licensed copy of that design can do.
- **Capital Review Board** — the public body that approves how a government agency spends money
  on a large construction project before work can begin. See [Investopedia, "Capital
  Budgeting"](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/c/capitalbudgeting.asp).
- **Rated Capacity** — the maximum number of riders a ride is designed and certified to carry at
  once; a safety and engineering figure, not a marketing estimate. See [Wikipedia, "Amusement
  ride"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amusement_ride).
- **Golden Hour** — the period shortly before sunset (and after sunrise) when sunlight is warm,
  low-angled, and considered the most visually striking time of day; the marketing basis for the
  "sunset-view guarantee" in this case. See [Wikipedia, "Golden hour
  (photography)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_hour_(photography)).
- **Foundation Load** — the total weight and force a structure transfers into the ground it sits
  on; a taller, heavier wheel requires a foundation engineered to carry more of it. See
  [Wikipedia, "Foundation (engineering)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundation_(engineering)).
