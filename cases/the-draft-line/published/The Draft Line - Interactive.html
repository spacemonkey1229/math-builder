Overview

Aisha Rahman has the stowage plan open on one monitor and the canal transit form open on the other, and the two are not agreeing with each other tonight.

She is the route planner for a container line's Asia–Europe strings, and the vessel loading at the quay outside her office window is supposed to sail in six hours. The plan has always been the Canal Passage — it's shorter, it's the network's advertised transit time, and every customer contract downstream assumes it. But the draft survey just came back from the loading superintendent, and the number is close enough to the canal's charted limit that Aisha has read it twice.

If she keeps loading to capacity, the ship draws too much water for the canal transit authority to clear her. Some containers come off at the load port — sold, but not delivered this voyage — or she reroutes the whole string around the Cape, keeps every box on board, and eats the extra sailing days it costs to get there the long way.

Her terminal ops manager is waiting on a phone call. The stowage planning software has both options open in two tabs, and Aisha has not clicked "confirm" on either one.

Appendices

Appendix A — Suez Canal Transit Rules: The Draft Restriction

The Suez Canal Authority's charted maximum permitted transit draft for a laden vessel is 20.1 meters (66 feet) [S1]. "Charted" means this is the Authority's own published, official limit — the number every vessel's paperwork is checked against before it is cleared, not an estimate. The limit is set by how deep and wide the channel itself has been dug (dredged); a wider ship (measured by its beamA ship's own width at its widest point., no wider than 50.0 m here) is held to a slightly different limit than a narrower one, but 20.1 m is the figure this case uses. The Authority reviews and adjusts the limit as the channel is deepened over time. A vessel whose laden draft exceeds the charted limit is not cleared for transit until its draft is brought under that limit.

Appendix B — Vessel Specifications

This case uses a stated, disclosed scenario vessel — the M/V Coral Meridian, an 18,000-TEU-class Ultra Large Container Vessel (ULCV) — rather than a real named ship's voyage-specific draft, which is commercial data not publicly available for any individual sailing. 18,000 TEU is consistent with real, publicly documented ULCV classes of this scale (e.g., the Maersk Triple E-class) [S2].

The draft-change-per-TEU rate is a simplified, uniform stand-in for the real relationship between cargo weight and how deep the ship sits in the water: a ship floats by displacing (pushing aside) a volume of water equal to its own weight, so every TEU removed lightens the ship by the same amount and this case treats each one as reducing draft by the same fixed number of centimeters. This case does not model which specific containers come off or from where on the ship — a real stowage plan would work out those placement details once the total offload count is set; this case, like a route planner's first-pass estimate, only needs that total count.

Both routes burn fuel and accrue this daily operating cost for every day the vessel is at sea, for the full length of whichever route is chosen — this is not a cost unique to the longer route. Only The Canal Passage additionally pays the Suez Canal toll, a one-time transit fee. Unit note: ocean freight rates are commonly quoted per 40-foot container (FEUForty-foot Equivalent Unit — the standard "big container" unit; two 20-foot TEU slots side by side equal one FEU.). By standard industry convention, 1 FEU = 2 TEU. Prices are quoted per FEU because a single 40-foot box is the more common size shippers actually book, while ship capacity is counted in TEU because that was the original, smaller standard container size the whole industry's counting system is still built around. The $1,813/TEU figure above is $3,626/FEU [S4] ÷ 2 TEU per FEU.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    "Aisha's phone buzzes. It's the customer service desk. The 200 containers coming off at the load port — three of the shippers are invoking the missed-sailing clause in their service contract. Under the standard agreement, the line owes an expedited-alternate-carriage penalty for any booked cargo that misses its sailing. That penalty is $1,800 per TEU. All 200 containers qualify."

    A "missed-sailing clause" is a common real-world shipping-contract term: if a shipper's booked cargo doesn't make it onto the ship it was promised, the line owes them a penalty for the cost and delay of re-booking it elsewhere — "expedited-alternate-carriage" just means paying to get that cargo moving some other way, fast.
