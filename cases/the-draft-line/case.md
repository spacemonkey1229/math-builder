## Overview

Aisha Rahman has the stowage plan open on one monitor and the canal transit form open on the other, and the two are not agreeing with each other tonight.

She is the route planner for a container line's Asia–Europe strings, and the vessel loading at the quay outside her office window is supposed to sail in six hours. The plan has always been the Canal Passage — it's shorter, it's the network's advertised transit time, and every customer contract downstream assumes it. But the draft survey just came back from the loading superintendent, and the number is close enough to the canal's charted limit that Aisha has read it twice.

If she keeps loading to capacity, the ship draws too much water for the canal transit authority to clear her. Some containers come off at the load port — sold, but not delivered this voyage — or she reroutes the whole string around the Cape, keeps every box on board, and eats the extra sailing days it costs to get there the long way.

Her terminal ops manager is waiting on a phone call. The stowage planning software has both options open in two tabs, and Aisha has not clicked "confirm" on either one.

## Background

Aisha Rahman is a route planner for a mid-sized container shipping line, responsible for committing each **laden** (fully loaded) vessel's routing before the voyage's final stowage plan — the map of which container goes in which slot on the ship — locks in. She is accountable for the voyage's profitability once the ship sails — a routing decision cannot be undone once cargo is loaded and the vessel clears the load port. Container capacity on a ship this size is counted in **TEU** (Twenty-foot Equivalent Units — the standard unit the industry uses, based on a 20-foot shipping container; a 40-foot container counts as 2 TEU).

Container ships transiting the Suez Canal must stay under the canal's charted maximum permitted transit **draft** — the vertical distance from the waterline down to the bottom of the hull, i.e. how deep the ship sits in the water — or the Suez Canal Authority will not clear them for passage (SEE APPENDIX A). A ship's draft increases as more cargo weight is loaded, because a floating vessel always displaces (pushes aside) a volume of water equal to its own weight: more weight means more displaced water, which means the ship rides lower and its draft increases; removing cargo weight does the reverse. Aisha's vessel, loaded to its full contracted cargo count, has a laden draft that exceeds this season's charted limit (SEE APPENDIX B for the vessel's stated specifications, including the specific rate this case uses to relate cargo removed to draft reduced). She has exactly two routing options available for this voyage, and the terminal's loading schedule requires her to commit to one before the ship's **berth window** — the fixed block of time the terminal has reserved for this ship at the dock — closes; the terminal has other ships booked into that same dock space right after hers, so missing the window doesn't just delay Aisha's ship, it forces a costly re-booking behind every vessel scheduled after it.

**The Canal Passage** transits the Suez Canal, the shorter and faster of the two routes (SEE APPENDIX C for route distances and the vessel's service speed) — but only if enough cargo comes off at the load port first to bring the vessel's draft under the canal's charted limit. Every container left behind is cargo this voyage does not get paid to carry. **The Cape Diversion** sails around the Cape of Good Hope instead, carrying the vessel's full contracted cargo count with no offload required — but it adds real sailing days at sea, and every extra day at sea means more days burning **bunker fuel** (the heavy fuel oil large ships buy by the metric ton) and accruing daily operating cost, on top of whichever route's own baseline fuel and operating cost every voyage already carries (SEE APPENDIX D for the toll, fuel, and revenue figures each option requires).

Every figure in this case is either cited to a real source or disclosed as a stated Modeling Assumption (SEE APPENDIX E for the assumptions and APPENDIX F for the sources). The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in Appendix G — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

## Appendix A — Suez Canal Transit Rules: The Draft Restriction

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D1).

The Suez Canal Authority's charted maximum permitted transit draft for a laden vessel is **20.1 meters (66 feet)** [S1]. "Charted" means this is the Authority's own published, official limit — the number every vessel's paperwork is checked against before it is cleared, not an estimate. The limit is set by how deep and wide the channel itself has been dug (dredged); a wider ship (measured by its **beam**, the ship's own width at its widest point) is held to a slightly different limit than a narrower one, but 20.1 m is the figure this case uses. The Authority reviews and adjusts the limit as the channel is deepened over time. A vessel whose laden draft exceeds the charted limit is not cleared for transit until its draft is brought under that limit.

This case does not model *which specific containers* come off or from where on the ship — a real stowage plan (see Background) would work out those placement details once the *total* offload count is set; this case, like a route planner's first-pass estimate, only needs that total count.

All figures in this table are disclosed Modeling Assumptions unless cited otherwise — see Appendix E. Both routes burn fuel and accrue this daily operating cost for every day the vessel is at sea, for the full length of whichever route is chosen (see Challenge D2 for each route's total transit time) — this is not a cost unique to the longer route. Only The Canal Passage additionally pays the Suez Canal toll, a one-time transit fee.

**Unit note:** ocean freight rates are commonly quoted per 40-foot container (FEU — Forty-foot Equivalent Unit). By standard industry convention, 1 FEU = 2 TEU. Prices are quoted per FEU because a single 40-foot box is the more common size shippers actually book, while ship capacity is counted in TEU because that was the original, smaller standard container size the whole industry's counting system is still built around. The $1,813/TEU figure above is $3,626/FEU [S4] ÷ 2 TEU per FEU.
