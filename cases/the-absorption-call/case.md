## Overview

Marcus Oyelaran stands at the back of the house, phone flashlight aimed at the rear wall, and claps once, hard. The sound doesn't die — it rolls forward over the empty seats, catches the ceiling, and comes back at him a half-second later, blurred and metallic. Somewhere behind the light board, the theater's production manager, Dana Ibok, is pacing. "The mixer sounded like it was recorded in a parking garage," she says. "Opening night is a rock trio doing a full PA set. Then next month we've got the songwriter series — just her, a guitar, and no mics at all." Marcus lets the echo fade before he answers. He's spent two decades tuning rooms that only ever needed to do one thing: carry a sermon clearly, or hold a chord. This one has to do both, on alternating Fridays, for the rest of the season.

He runs his hand along the cinderblock at the back of the house — bare, hard, exactly the kind of surface that throws sound back as one sharp echo, the way a flat mirror throws back one sharp beam of light. Two binders sit open on the sound desk. One is stamped with a swatch of charcoal-gray absorptive panel, dense and dead to the touch. The other holds a diffusor panel, its face molded into a grid of pockets of different depths, like an oversized ice-cube tray — nothing flat for sound to bounce straight off of. Dana taps her watch. The panel order has to go out today if it's going to arrive before the amplified run opens.

Marcus picks up both binders at once and doesn't set either one down.

## Background

Marcus Oyelaran is an independent acoustic consultant hired by the **Hollow Oak Community Theater**, a converted-warehouse black-box space, to solve a persistent complaint from its sound engineers and directors: the room is "boomy," swallowing spoken dialogue and turning amplified music into mush. The theater's board has authorized a single, fixed acoustic-treatment project — applied only to the flat rear wall of the house, the largest untreated reflective surface in the room — to be installed before the venue's amplified show run begins. Marcus reports directly to the board and is accountable for both the acoustic outcome and the installed cost, which must be quoted against the board's approved treatment budget (SEE APPENDIX D).

The theater's calendar is genuinely mixed. Most of the season is amplified: a rock trio, a hip-hop revue, and a musical with a mixed PA all depend on clear, controlled speech and vocals reproduced through loudspeakers — that is the room's dominant use for the coming season. But the theater also hosts an unamplified acoustic songwriter series later in the season, where a single performer and instrument must fill the room naturally, without a PA — a use that benefits from the room keeping some of its natural liveness rather than going acoustically "dead."

Marcus has narrowed the fix to two named options, both applied to the same rear-wall footprint and both quoted to fit inside the board's treatment budget (SEE APPENDICES B, C and D):

**The Dead Wall** — cover the rear wall in broadband absorptive panels, which convert most of the sound energy that strikes them into heat instead of reflecting it. This sharply shortens the room's reverberation time and favors speech and amplified-show clarity.

**The Scatter Wall** — cover the same rear wall in geometric diffusive panels, which redirect and break up reflections instead of removing most of that energy. This keeps more of the room's natural liveness intact for the unamplified acoustic series, while still breaking up the harshest, most disruptive echo off the flat rear wall.

Every other surface in the house — floor, ceiling, both side walls, and the wall behind the stage — stays exactly as built; Marcus is not treating them (SEE APPENDIX A). The room's volume and dimensions are fixed by the building; he cannot change them (SEE APPENDIX A). Whichever option he recommends, the panels must cover the full rear wall — the board rejected partial coverage as visually inconsistent for a public house.

Several figures in this case are stated scenario assumptions rather than sourced facts, each disclosed with its basis and range (SEE APPENDIX E). Every sourced figure is cited with its publisher, date, and confidence grade (SEE APPENDIX F). A final appendix lists the exact vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case is built to exercise (SEE APPENDIX G) — that is what Challenge 1 asks you to research.

### A Few Terms, Plainly

- **"Boomy"** — Dana and Marcus's shorthand for an excessively long, muddying echo: spoken words blur into each other and separate musical notes seem to blend together, because the room takes too long to let each sound fade out before the next one arrives.
- **PA (public address system)** — the loudspeakers, microphones, and amplifiers used to reinforce sound electronically. "Amplified" shows use one; the unamplified acoustic series does not.
- **Mixer / light board** — the audio console (mixer) and lighting console (light board) sound and lighting technicians operate from during a show; mentioned only to set the scene, not needed for any calculation.
- **NRC (Noise Reduction Coefficient)** — a rating that summarizes how much sound a material absorbs: 0 means almost none (a hard, bare wall), 1 means almost all (an open window). A very high-performing panel, like Appendix B's Option A at 1.05, can test a little above 1 on the published rating scale — that is just how this particular rating is reported for top-tier products, not a different kind of number. This case uses NRC exactly the same way as "absorption coefficient α" — Appendix B's NRC figure is the α value plugged directly into Sabine's equation.
- **Mid-band** — how absorbing a material is can differ a little by pitch; "mid-band" just means this case uses one representative middle-of-the-range number for each material, rather than a full pitch-by-pitch table.
- **Sabin** — the unit total absorption is measured in, the same way length is measured in feet: an open window lets essentially all the sound through, so one square foot of open window equals one sabin, and every material in this case is measured against that same yardstick. A room's total absorption is the sum, over every surface, of that surface's area times its own absorption coefficient.
- **"Broadband"** (as in "broadband absorptive panel") — means the panel absorbs sound well across the whole range of pitches, not just bass or just treble.
- **Confidence grade (HIGH/MEDIUM/LOW, Appendix F)** — how directly a source backs its figure: HIGH means the number comes straight from the maker or publisher of the fact itself; MEDIUM means it comes from a reputable page reporting on that same fact, once removed.
- **GIK Acoustics 242 / RPG QRD-Skyline** — real, commercially available acoustic-panel and diffusor product lines (the model names/codes themselves don't matter for any calculation), named here only as concrete, real-world reference points for the coefficients and panel sizes this case uses (SEE APPENDIX F).
- **What a diffusor panel actually looks like.** Picture a large plastic ice-cube tray, mounted flat against the wall with its open cells facing out into the room. Now imagine every cell was molded to a different depth — some barely dented in, some carved in deep — so that looking at the tray face-on, it's not one flat surface, it's a grid of shallow and deep pockets side by side. That is what a diffusor panel's face is: not flat, but pocked with cells of different depths, front-on to the room. This case does not ask you to work out *why* that shape behaves the way it does acoustically — the same way it does not ask you to derive *why* Sabine's constant is 0.161, or *why* a given material's absorption coefficient is what it is. Like those, "a panel shaped this way measures as α = 0.48" (Appendix B) is simply a lab-measured fact you are handed and use, not a mechanism to reconstruct.

### Sabine's equation

Reverberation time (RT60, in seconds) is:

**RT60 = 0.161 × V / A**

where V is the room's volume in cubic meters, and A is the room's total absorption in metric sabins — the sum, over every surface, of that surface's area in square meters multiplied by its absorption coefficient [S1] [S2].
