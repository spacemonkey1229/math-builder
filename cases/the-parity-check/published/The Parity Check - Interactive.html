Overview

Renata Kuo has forty minutes before the firmware freeze meeting, and the two functions on her second monitor are eleven lines apart.

One line calls add_parity_bit(). The other calls add_crc32(). Whichever one she leaves in the build tonight ships, unchanged, to thousands of sensor nodes already bolted to fence posts, buoys, and irrigation risers across three states — devices nobody is going to climb a pole to re-flash before next spring's maintenance window.

Her phone buzzes, face-down, next to the empty coffee cup. It's the hardware lead. He has asked her twice this week, in front of increasingly more people, how many extra milliamp-hours her error-checking scheme is going to cost his battery budget — and both times she gave him a number she wasn't fully sure would survive a real bad-weather week.

She pulls up the packet trace from the test rig: tiny frames, some barely a dozen bytes long, the sensor's whole reading crammed into less space than this sentence. She knows the trade in her gut — parity is nearly free and misses things; CRC catches almost everything and costs more, every single packet, whether it needs it or not. What she doesn't have yet is the number that turns “catches more” into “worth it, at this packet size.”

The meeting invite is already open in the other window. Renata sets down the coffee cup, glances at both function calls one more time, and reaches for the keyboard.

Background

Renata Kuo is the network reliability engineer at a small satellite-IoT operator. She owns the link-layer error-detection scheme for the company's new low-power sensor uplink, and she is the one who has to defend whatever she chooses — both to the firmware review board this week and, later, to the hardware team, whose battery budget her scheme eats into every time a packet goes out.

The uplink firmware freezes for the season in one week. After that, nothing about the error-checking scheme can change without a truck roll to every deployed sensor (see Appendix F). Renata is choosing between exactly two schemes, both already implemented and tested on the bench. All figures in this case are sourced in Appendix I; where no public figure exists, the case says so and states its assumption in Appendix G.

Renata's two options

The Featherweight Check: a single even-parity bit appended to every byte of sensor data. The cost is fixed and tiny — one extra bit for every eight data bits, roughly 12.5% of the data stream (1 bit ÷ 8 data bits). Its limitation is structural, not a matter of bad luck: a single even-parity bit can only ever detect an odd number of flipped bits in that byte. If exactly two bits flip — or four, or six — the parity check comes back clean and the corrupted byte is delivered as if nothing happened (see Appendix B).

The Full-Frame Guard: a 4-byte CRC-32 frame check sequence appended once per whole packet — the same scheme IEEE 802.11 uses to guard every Wi-Fi frame (see Appendix A). It catches essentially every realistic error pattern, including the entire class of even-bit-flip errors the Featherweight Check is blind to. Its cost is fixed per packet, not per byte: 4 bytes (32 bits) added once, however small the packet.

Same mechanism as Appendix B, drawn out bit-by-bit: an odd number of flipped bits always changes the 1-bit count's even/odd status, so parity catches it. An even number of flipped bits always leaves that status unchanged, so parity misses it — every time, not just sometimes. This is what D1 and D2 ask you to work out numerically for the actual Appendix B bytes.

That per-packet fixed cost is the whole tension. Renata's sensor packets are not one fixed size — the uplink firmware supports a range of payload sizes depending on which sensors are attached and how much telemetry a node is reporting that cycle (see Appendix C). On the company's largest packets, a 4-byte tax barely registers. On its smallest — some payloads run as small as 11 bytes — that same 4 bytes can be a third or more of everything transmitted.

Every extra bit transmitted also costs battery: the radio spends measurable energy per bit sent, and the hardware team's battery budget for this deployment is fixed for the season (see Appendix D). Renata cannot evaluate either scheme by overhead alone or by catch-rate alone — the question is which scheme catches more corrupted frames per bit spent getting there, and that number depends on packet size, which is exactly the variable she can't pin to one value (see Appendix C). A stated per-bit error rate for this class of link, and what it implies about how often a byte suffers an even vs. an odd number of flips, is in Appendix E.

Working Notebook

This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

Step 1 — D1: Byte-level parity table (Appendix B)

For each sample byte, count the 1-bits, classify EVEN or ODD, and give the even-parity bit the Featherweight Check would append (0 if the count is already even, 1 if it needs one more 1 to become even).

Step 2 — D2: The "silent miss" (Appendix B, Byte 1)

Byte 1 (01101001) is corrupted to 11101101 (2 bits flipped). Count the 1-bits in the corrupted byte, then say whether the Featherweight Check would catch this corruption.

Remember: a single even-parity bit only ever flags an ODD number of flipped bits. Whatever you find here, would the Full-Frame Guard have caught it too?

Step 3 — D3: Overhead vs. packet size (Appendix C)

For each packet size, compute each scheme's overhead as a percent of the total transmitted frame (data bits + overhead bits). Featherweight Check overhead = 1 bit per byte; Full-Frame Guard overhead is fixed at 32 bits.

Step 4 — D4: Bits to decimal digits

What is the largest possible check value each scheme can produce, and how many decimal digits does it take to write that value out? (Featherweight Check: 1 bit. Full-Frame Guard: 32 bits.)

Step 5 — D5: Break-even packet size and recommendation

Using Appendix E's stated per-bit error rate (6×10⁻⁵), estimate each scheme's efficiency — P(catches the frame's corruption) ÷ overhead bits spent — at n = 35 bytes (Appendix C row 2). Then state the packet size, in bytes, where the two schemes' raw overhead exactly ties (1 bit/byte × n bytes = 32 bits).

Before you recommend one scheme for every packet Renata ships, reread Appendix C: does the winner at 35 bytes also win at 11 bytes? At 222? What does that tell you about "one scheme for every packet size"?

Twist

Reveal this only after you've finished working through the case — the Twist is meant to land after your analysis, not before.

Reveal the Twist

During the Data Deep Dive, the hardware team confirms the sensor radio's real per-bit error rate is dominated by burst noise from a nearby transmitter during specific weather conditions — not the flat, uniform bit-error rate Appendix E states as a baseline assumption. Burst errors tend to flip clusters of adjacent bits, which changes the even/odd split in ways a uniform-rate model does not predict.

You have sixty seconds. Does your D5 break-even number still hold? What would you need to re-check?
