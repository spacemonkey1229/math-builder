Background

Devon Achebe is the gameplay camera engineer on Launchpad Games' boss-fight cutsceneA non-interactive, scripted sequence in a video game — often used to stage a dramatic story or combat beat the player watches rather than controls. team, responsible for authoring the camera path that runs during the game's climactic boss encounter. Devon does not choose how the boss animates or attacks; Devon chooses how the camera tracks it, and that choice has to survive both the animation team's blockingA rough first pass of staging and camera positions in an animated scene, locked in before final polish begins. review tomorrow and the engine's own frame-time budgetThe maximum time (in milliseconds) every system in the game has to finish its work before the next frame must be shown, based on the target frame rate. once it ships. Camera engineers commonly build orbit and "always face the target" behavior by describing an orbiting camera's position with a radius and an angle relative to the thing it's tracking — a polar description — rather than hand-authoring raw on-screen coordinates frame by frame. Unity's own Cinemachine camera system, one of the most widely used camera toolkits in commercial game engines, implements exactly this convention (see Appendix A) — because that really is the natural way to reason about "how far from the boss, and at what point around it" as the camera moves.

Devon has narrowed the boss-fight camera to two rigs, both authored around the same boss position at the arena's center. The Fixed-Radius Orbit holds the camera at one constant distance from the boss for the entire encounter, computed each frame from a simple radius-and-angle rule — cheap, predictable, and comfortably clear of the arena's stone collision wall, but it frames the boss the same way whether or not anything dramatic is happening. The Variable-Radius Push-In instead drives the camera along a polar curve whose distance from the boss actually changes with the orbit angle, tightening in specifically during the boss's telegraphed attack for a bigger, more dramatic frame — at the cost of evaluating one more term of math every single frame of the fight, not only during the dramatic beat, and at the cost of coming closer to the arena's collision wall than the fixed orbit ever does (see Appendix B for both rigs' governing equations and Option A's authored waypoints, Appendix C for the arena's clipping limit, and Appendix D for the compute-cost and framing figures each rig actually requires).

Every figure in this case is either cited to real, published camera-engineering documentation (Appendices A and F) or disclosed as a stated production Modeling Assumption (Appendix E). The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in Appendix G — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

Appendices

Appendix A — Real Camera-Engineering Technique: Polar Camera Rigs

Both of Devon's candidate rigs use a real, documented technique: describing an orbiting camera's position by a radius and an angle relative to the thing it's tracking (a polar coordinate description), and keeping the camera aimed at that target with a dedicated "look-at"/aim behavior. A componentA self-contained, reusable piece of a larger piece of software -- like a single settings panel that plugs into a bigger tool and handles one job (here, one part of the camera's behavior)., in this context, just means one self-contained, swappable piece of the camera's behavior that a developer attaches, the way you'd snap one Lego piece onto a model rather than build the whole thing from scratch. Unity's Cinemachine camera system — one of the most widely used camera toolkits in commercial game engines — implements exactly this: its Orbital Follow component positions a camera using "3 axis values: horizontal, vertical, and radius," where radius "controls the scale, allowing the camera to move towards or away from the target" [S1], and its Rotation Composer component continuously "rotates the camera to face the Look At target" [S2]. Unity's own default camera field of view is also a real, standard convention — a new Camera component defaults to 60 degrees, which is roughly the same angular slice of the world a human eye comfortably takes in without turning the head, wide enough to see a full room but not a full 360° panorama [S3]. (This case's own framing formula in Appendix D is a separate, simpler stated simplification and does not use this specific 60-degree figure in its arithmetic; it is included here only to show that "how much of the screen the camera can see at all" is itself a real, standard production number, not invented from nothing.)

Appendix D — Framing Formula and Per-Frame Compute Cost

Framing formula. Over the camera distances both rigs use, the percent of the vertical frame the boss's model occupies is modeled as inversely proportional to the camera's current polar radius:

percent of frame ≈ 300 ÷ r   (r = camera's polar radius, in game-units)

This is a stated simplification of real perspective-camera framing (a closer camera makes a fixed-size subject fill a larger fraction of the frame), scaled so that Rig A's constant orbit gives a stated baseline frame percentage — see Appendix E, MA2.

Per-frame compute cost. Every frame, the camera system has to evaluate its rig's governing equation to find the camera's next position.

RigWhat it evaluates each framePer-frame compute cost
A — The Fixed-Radius Orbitone sine and one cosine (radius is constant)0.020 ms/frame
B — The Variable-Radius Push-Inone sine, one cosine of the orbit angle, and a subtraction (radius changes with θ)0.022 ms/frame

Both figures are disclosed Modeling Assumptions — see Appendix E, MA3 and MA4. For scale: a cutscene running at a 60-frames-per-second target gives the whole engine roughly 16.67 ms per frame total, shared across rendering, animation, AI, and everything else the game does at once — see Appendix E, MA5. Neither rig's own cost looks large next to that 16.67 ms total on its own, but the camera system is only one of dozens of systems all competing for the same shared budget every single frame, on every player's hardware, for the whole length of the fight -- which is exactly why a camera engineer still has to account for it precisely rather than rounding it to zero.
