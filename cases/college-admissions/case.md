## Overview

"Alright, one more spot to go. We're down to these last two applicants," says Betsy Peterson, one of three Admissions Officers at Stanford University's Office of Undergraduate Admission. Zach Laursen, another Admissions Officer, leans back in his chair. "Both of them would be great additions to our incoming freshman class."

Betsy turns to Jack Ygarret, who is standing near the large glass window looking out at the foggy Palo Alto campus. "Who do we admit, Jack?"

Jack sets two folders side by side on the conference table, squaring their edges like it might help. He's read each of these files four times now — the essays, the transcripts, the numbers scrawled in the margins by whichever officer read them first. He thinks about the moment an envelope gets opened: a family jumping up from the kitchen table, or a family going quiet. He remembers which one his own family was, the year he applied here.

The room hums with the fluorescent lights and the faint smell of coffee gone cold. Betsy taps her pen against her folder. Zach checks the clock.

"Who's it going to be, Jack?"

## Background

Jack Ygarret has worked as an Admissions Officer for Stanford University's Office of Undergraduate Admission for eight years, reading and deciding on thousands of applications. Like every officer on the committee, he does not get to admit everyone he likes — Stanford sets a target class size each year, and once that target is filled, every remaining "yes" has to come with a corresponding "no" (**SEE APPENDIX A**).

The committee is down to its final open spot, and down to two finalists: **Tisha Ackerman** and **Harrison Carter** (**SEE APPENDIX C AND APPENDIX D**). Both have strong unweighted GPAs, competitive standardized test scores, a full slate of AP coursework, and personal essays the committee has already read and discussed. On paper, each is the kind of student Stanford's own published admissions data says it looks for (**SEE APPENDIX A**).

Every year, Stanford — like nearly every college in the United States — publishes a **Common Data Set**: a standardized report of enrollment, admissions, and test-score statistics for its incoming class (**SEE APPENDIX A**). It is the most recent, publicly available snapshot Jack has of what the rest of this year's admitted class looks like, and it is the yardstick the committee uses to sanity-check every close call. Appendix A also does not, by itself, tell Jack how many classmates rank above a given applicant at that applicant's own high school — that takes combining a rank *percentile* with a school's actual size, and one of the two files in front of him does not state that number at all (**SEE APPENDIX B, C, AND D**).

Jack has to weigh Tisha's and Harrison's files against each other, and against Stanford's own published class profile, before the committee's next meeting. The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in **Appendix F** — that is where Challenge 1 sends you. Curious about how holistic admissions and the Common Data Set actually work? **SEE APPENDIX E** for further reading.

## Modeling Assumptions

- **MA1.** This case's committee scene (Jack Ygarret, Betsy Peterson, Zach Laursen) and the specific pairing of Tisha Ackerman vs. Harrison Carter as finalists for one open spot are fiction, kept from the original case materials. Stanford's institutional Common Data Set figures in Appendix A are real and independently sourced.
- **MA2.** D5 converts each applicant's stated class-rank percentile into an estimated raw count of classmates who rank above them, which requires knowing the applicant's high school enrollment. Tisha's application states her school's size directly (3,200+ students). Harrison's application states only "Public" with no enrollment figure, so this case substitutes the U.S. national average public high school enrollment (Appendix B) as a stated stand-in. This is a real simplification — an individual school's actual size can differ substantially from the national average, and the answer to D5 is sensitive to this substitution.
- **MA3.** Comparing "class rank percentile" between two different high schools assumes both schools compute rank the same way (typically unweighted or weighted GPA-based rank). Real high schools vary in ranking methodology and in whether they rank at all; neither application states its school's ranking method, so this case does not adjust for that variation.
- **MA4.** Appendix A's box-and-whisker plot (D1) uses Stanford's published 25th and 75th percentile SAT Composite bounds as the box edges. Stanford's Common Data Set does not publish a raw median SAT score, so the median line is estimated as the midpoint of the 25th/75th bounds — a modeling simplification, not a reported Stanford statistic.

## Appendix E — Learn More

Curious about the real-world process behind this case? These are real, external resources — not required for the challenges, but useful if you want to go deeper.

- [Holistic Admission — Stanford University](https://admission.stanford.edu/apply/overview/index.html) — Stanford's own admissions office explains, in its own words, how it reviews each application "as part of an integrated and comprehensive whole" rather than by numbers alone.
- [Common Data Set Initiative](https://commondataset.org/) — the official site of the collaboration (College Board, Peterson's, and U.S. News & World Report) that defines the standardized data categories, like the ones in Appendix A, that colleges report every year.
- [Box and whisker plot: how to construct — Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org/math/probability/data-distributions-a1/box--whisker-plots-a1/v/constructing-a-box-and-whisker-plot) — a free video walking through building a box-and-whisker plot from a data set, step by step.
- [Constructing a scatter plot — Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org/v/constructing-scatter-plot) — a free video on plotting two related variables against each other and reading the pattern (or lack of one) in the result.
