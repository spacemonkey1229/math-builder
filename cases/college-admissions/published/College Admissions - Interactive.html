Overview

"Alright, one more spot to go. We're down to these last two applicants," says Betsy Peterson, one of three Admissions Officers at Stanford University's Office of Undergraduate Admission. Zach Laursen, another officer, leans back in his chair. "Both of them would be great additions to our incoming freshman class."

Betsy turns to Jack Ygarret, who is standing near the large glass window looking out at the foggy Palo Alto campus. "Who do we admit, Jack?"

Jack sets two folders side by side on the conference table, squaring their edges like it might help. He's read each of these files four times now — the essays, the transcripts, the numbers scrawled in the margins by whichever officer read them first. He thinks about the moment an envelope gets opened: a family jumping up from the kitchen table, or a family going quiet. He remembers which one his own family was, the year he applied here.

The room hums with the fluorescent lights and the faint smell of coffee gone cold. Betsy taps her pen against her folder. Zach checks the clock.

"Who's it going to be, Jack?"

Background

Jack Ygarret has worked as an Admissions Officer for Stanford University's Office of Undergraduate Admission for eight years, reading and deciding on thousands of applications. Like every officer on the committee, he does not get to admit everyone he likes — Stanford sets a target class size each year, and once that target is filled, every remaining "yes" has to come with a corresponding "no" (see Appendix A).

The committee is down to its final open spot, and down to two finalists: Tisha Ackerman and Harrison Carter (see Appendix C and Appendix D). Both have strong unweighted GPAs, competitive standardized test scores, a full slate of AP coursework, and personal essays the committee has already read and discussed.

Every year, Stanford — like nearly every college in the United States — publishes a Common Data Set: a standardized report of enrollment, admissions, and test-score statistics for its incoming class (see Appendix A). It is the most recent, publicly available snapshot Jack has of what the rest of this year's admitted class looks like. Appendix A does not, by itself, tell Jack how many classmates rank above a given applicant at that applicant's own high school — that takes combining a rank percentile with a school's actual size, and one of the two files in front of him doesn't state that number at all (see Appendix B, C, and D).

Jack has to weigh Tisha's and Harrison's files against each other, and against Stanford's own published class profile, before the committee's next meeting.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — D4: The SAT Composite gap

    Using Appendix C and D, find the point gap between Tisha's and Harrison's SAT Composite scores, and express that gap as a percent of the lower score.

    Step 2 — D1: Box-and-whisker set-up

    Using Appendix A's 25th/75th percentile SAT bounds, find the interquartile range (the box width), then check whether Tisha's score falls below the box's lower edge.

    Step 3 — D5: Raw headcount above each applicant

    Tisha's school size is given (Appendix C). Harrison's isn't — pick an assumption (Appendix B's national average is a reasonable start, but you can try your own) and compute both estimated headcounts.

    This step is self-checked against your own assumed enrollment, not a single hidden "right" number — the point is to get the arithmetic right, not to guess Jack's exact assumption.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    "One more thing," Jack says, before either officer can vote. "A class-rank percentile only tells you a fraction of the class — it doesn't tell you how many actual people that fraction represents. Tisha's school has more than 3,200 students. Harrison's application doesn't say how big his school is at all. Before you compare '3rd percentile' to '7th percentile' as if they mean the same thing — figure out how many classmates each number actually puts above Tisha and Harrison."
