Overview

The dock door is up, and the driver has checked his phone three times in the last two minutes. Renata Ibarra hasn't signed the release yet.

Behind her, stacked on a pallet, sit the sealed cases of single-use tubing manifolds from Lot 4471 — the sterile connective plumbing that a customer's cell-therapy manufacturing line is waiting on, three states away, to start a run tomorrow morning. Her badge still reads "Quality — 2nd Shift." Her signature on the Certificate of Analysis is the only thing standing between this lot and that truck.

The batch-release screen in front of her — software that walks a Quality Engineer through each required test one at a time, records the actual result next to what the specification requires, and only lets her generate and sign a Certificate of Analysis (the document listing every specification, every actual result, and her signature) once every required line shows a pass — shows two saved sampling plans, same lot number at the top of both. One of them is already highlighted, the way the software always defaults to the plan that clears fastest. The other sits one click away, in a smaller font, unselected.

Her supervisor, half in the doorway with his coat already on, says the thing everyone on this shift says by nine o'clock: "It's the same lot either way — just sign the fast one." Renata doesn't say what she's actually thinking, which is that "fast" and "fully covered" are two different questions, and nobody has actually run the numbers on which sampling plan she's about to click tonight.

The countdown on the driver's dispatch app ticks over. Her cursor sits between the two plans, over a button that will decide how many of these units get pulled and destroyed to check the rest.

Background

Renata Ibarra is a Quality Engineer at Alden Bioprocess Components, a mid-sized manufacturer of single-use tubing and manifold assemblies (a manifold assembly is a branching network of sterile, single-use plastic tubing joined together at multiple ports (an opening on the tubing itself where another line, sensor, or connector attaches), connectors, and clamps, built to route a drug or biologic fluid from one manufacturing step to the next and then be thrown away rather than cleaned and reused) used in biologics (drugs manufactured from living cells, which are grown and held inside a bioreactor — in single-use manufacturing like Alden's, that is typically a large flexible plastic bag holding cells and growth medium (a nutrient-rich liquid that feeds the cells so they can grow and multiply) in liquid suspension (dispersed throughout the liquid, rather than dissolved in it or floating on top), sitting inside a rigid support container rather than a fixed steel tank) and cell-therapy production (a treatment manufactured individually, from one specific patient's own cells, on a fixed clinical timeline — which is why a single-sourced tubing lot with no substitute on hand can stall that one manufacturing run outright, not just delay a shipment) — the same general product category made at far larger scale by named industry players like Sartorius, Thermo Fisher Scientific, and Cytiva, whose single-use systems Alden's assemblies are designed to be compatible with. Every finished production lot must clear a destructive quality control sample before Renata can sign its Certificate of Analysis and release it for shipment (SEE APPENDIX A). "Destructive" is the operative word: every unit pulled for testing is cut open, pressure-tested to failure (filled with air or liquid under increasing pressure until it bursts, to find its actual breaking point), or otherwise consumed in the process, and cannot be sold afterward — so a bigger sample is not free QC, it is real, disposed inventory. A tested unit "fails" in one of a few concrete ways — it leaks at a seal or port under normal pressure, or it survives to some pressure but bursts below the minimum threshold the standard requires — and either one means that unit did not meet spec. Because every unit in the same lot is molded, welded, and sterilized together from the same raw-material batch and the same production run, a defect found in one destructively tested unit is treated as evidence about how the whole lot was made, not just about that one unit — that substitution (a few destroyed units standing in for what could not otherwise be known about the rest without destroying every one of them) is the entire logic behind pulling a sample at all, rather than testing 0 units or all 500.

Tonight's lot, Lot 4471, presents Renata with two named, pre-approved sampling plans loaded into the batch-release software (SEE APPENDIX A): The Lean Draw, which pulls the smaller sample size specified by the ANSI/ASQ Z1.4 acceptance-sampling standard's General Inspection Level II for a lot this size, and The Deep Draw, which pulls the larger sample size specified by the same standard's General Inspection Level III for the identical lot (SEE APPENDIX B). Both plans use the same acceptance rule: the lot is only released if every single tested unit passes; if even one tested unit fails, the whole lot is held for full inspection. Every other line of the two plans — which lot, which product, which acceptance rule — is identical; only the sample size, and therefore the number of units destroyed and the odds of catching a real problem, changes.

The instinct on the floor — her supervisor's instinct, and the software's own default selection — is that the smaller, faster sample is "close enough," since both plans use the same strict pass/fail rule. But a bigger sample doesn't just cost more units; it also changes, in a real and calculable way, the probability that an out-of-spec lot slips through undetected (SEE APPENDIX C). Renata has to weigh the certain, up-front cost of destroying more units against the real, probability-weighted cost of a defect reaching the customer's manufacturing line uncaught — using Appendix C's real industry contamination-occurrence data and the standard's own math (SEE APPENDIX D for the vocabulary and procedures that math requires, and APPENDIX E for every source behind these figures), not a gut feeling about which plan "sounds thorough enough."

There is a further wrinkle worth naming honestly: the industry-wide contamination-occurrence rate in Appendix C describes an average lot, not necessarily this lot. A lot flagged by a supplier deviation notice — a formal document a supplier files, on its own, when a raw material or component it shipped doesn't fully meet the agreed specification — is a different situation than an ordinary lot pulled at random (SEE APPENDIX C). Filing that notice is only the first step: the receiving quality team (Renata's own) is then required to evaluate it before the affected material can be used at all, and that evaluation ends in one of three real outcomes — accept the deviation as-is, reject the affected lot outright, or accept it only under extra scrutiny (such as pulling a larger sample) before it can be used. Whichever plan wins the math for an average lot might not win the same way for a lot Quality already has a specific reason to worry about.

Appendix B — The ANSI/ASQ Z1.4 Standard (Why These Are the Only Two Real Options)

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D1). Renata cannot simply pick any sample size she likes — Alden's quality system requires every lot's sampling plan to come from a real, named acceptance-sampling standard.

Lot size rangeLevel I code letterLevel II code letterLevel III code letter
281 to 500 [S1]F [S1]H [S1]J [S1]

The table above works like a lookup: find the row for your lot's size range, then read across to whichever General Inspection Level your quality system has chosen — the code letter in that cell is what Appendix A's table then converts into an exact sample size. The full standard has many more size-range rows than the single "281 to 500" one shown here (Lot 4471's own row); every row follows the same pattern. A code letter exists as a middle step, rather than the lot size pointing straight to a sample size, because the same letter (and therefore the same sample size) can come up for more than one nearby lot-size range — it is the standard's own shorthand for "look up the actual number in the Master Table," not a number itself. A stricter level's code letter always maps to a larger sample size for the same lot-size row, which is what makes Level III more thorough (and Level I less so) than Level II for an identical lot.

Modeling note: General Inspection Level II is the standard's own default level for ordinary lots; Level III is a tightened level a quality system may specify when it wants more assurance for the same lot. Level I (a reduced level, cheaper still) exists in the standard but is not one of tonight's two loaded plans — Alden's own quality system does not permit Level I for this product family, a real internal constraint, not a choice Renata is making tonight.

    Step 1 — Escape Probability (D2)

    Using the c=0 acceptance rule and Appendix C's MA1 (2.5% defect fraction), compute the probability that a fully out-of-spec lot still passes every tested unit and ships undetected — for each plan.

    Show your work as repeated multiplication: (1 − defect fraction) multiplied by itself once per tested unit — not a single subtraction.

    Step 2 — Expected Total Cost Per Lot (D3)

    Add each plan's certain destructive-testing cost (Appendix A) to its probability-weighted risk cost (Appendix C's baseline lot-occurrence rate × your Step 1 escape probability × the cost of an undetected escape).

    Step 3 — Sample-Size Sensitivity Dot Plot (D5)

    Enter at least four candidate sample sizes (including 50 and 80) and see each one's escape probability plotted — does the probability drop at a steady, even rate, or some other rate?

    Each dot's height is that sample size's own computed escape probability (using MA1 = 2.5%) — nothing here is looked up, it is computed live from whatever sample sizes you enter.
