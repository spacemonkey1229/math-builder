Overview

Two browser tabs, one laptop, one deadline. Dana Ruiz has the hero character's rig open in both.

The performance lab picks up the build at 9 AM — the studio's one certified test bench, a mid-tier phone bolted to a rig that logs every dropped frame and won't let a submission through if the hero character stutters during combat. Dana has been staring at the same skeleton for forty minutes, and one seam in particular: the shoulder, where the upper-arm bone and the torso bone both pull on the same handful of vertices at once. Everywhere else on the rig, one bone controls one patch of mesh, clean and simple. At the shoulder — and the underarm, and the cape's mounting point — two or more bones share the vertices, and how their transforms get blended together is a real choice with a real cost.

Branch one blends directly. It takes each of the two bones' transform matrices, multiplies each by how much influence it has on that vertex, and adds the two weighted matrices together — one sum, applied once. Branch two blends differently: it converts each bone's rotation to a different kind of number first, blends those, and only converts back to a matrix at the very end. Dana's already written the sticky note for the art director: *works with either branch, no model changes either way.* That part's settled.

What isn't settled is which branch survives contact with a phone slower than Dana's laptop — and whether the cheaper branch is actually safe to ship, because Dana has a nagging memory of a "candy-wrapper" bug from a previous job, right at a seam exactly like this one. Dana pulls up a blank sheet, sketches two rotation matrices — R1 and R2 — and starts adding.

Background

Dana Ruiz is a Technical Artist / Rigger at Fenwick Games, a small mobile-game studio building an action combat game for phones. Dana owns the transform pipeline for the hero character's skeletal rig — the system that takes each bone's rotation for the current animation frame and turns it into the numbers that actually move the character's mesh on screen (SEE APPENDIX A). Most of the rig is simple: one bone, one patch of mesh, one straightforward matrix multiplication, unaffected by today's decision (SEE APPENDIX B, the elbow-bone example in D1/D2). But at a handful of **blend zones** — the shoulder, the underarm, the cape's mounting point — two or more bones share influence over the same vertices, and the two working pipelines below disagree about how to combine them:

The Direct Blend (linear blend skinning) takes each influencing bone's transform matrix, multiplies it by that bone's weight, and adds the weighted matrices together directly — one combined matrix, applied once per shared vertex. It is the fastest option, real-world measured, and it is the de facto industry-standard technique (SEE APPENDIX B). But a straight sum of two rotation matrices is not itself guaranteed to be a valid rigid rotation — real, published graphics research explains exactly why, and this case's own Challenge D3 proves it by hand for Fenwick's own shoulder seam.

The Quaternion Blend (direct quaternion blending) converts each influencing bone's rotation to a quaternion first, blends the quaternions instead of the raw matrices, and only converts the result back into a matrix at the end, before applying it to the vertex. Real, measured GPU cost: higher than The Direct Blend's, by an amount Appendix D's raw figures let you work out yourself. Real, published upside: it always produces a valid rigid transformation. Real, published downside: it has its own documented failure mode at more complex blend zones (SEE APPENDIX C).

Neither pipeline changes what bones exist or how the rig is authored — both are drop-in choices for how the engine blends shared vertices at the same handful of seams (SEE APPENDIX B and APPENDIX C). What they do not share is measured GPU compute cost, or how each one behaves mathematically at a seam like the shoulder (SEE APPENDIX D). Fenwick's hero rig, its target frame rate, and its frame budget are all sized to real, published mobile-game guidance; the two blending techniques' relative cost and behavior are sized to real, published, peer-reviewed graphics research, not to Fenwick's own (fictional) numbers (SEE APPENDIX A, APPENDIX D, and APPENDIX F). A real, documented example also exists of the more general kind of per-vertex transform cost this case is built on, blowing well past a studio's frame budget once it is computed honestly instead of estimated by feel (SEE APPENDIX E). Every sourced and assumed figure in this case is listed in APPENDIX G, and the vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in APPENDIX H — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — The elbow bone: M = R·S (D1–D3a)

    Using Appendix B's elbow rotation matrix R and stretch matrix S, compute M = R·S entry by entry (2×2 matrix multiplication), then its determinant, and compare to the identity matrix's determinant (1).

    Step 2 — The shoulder blend-zone: M_blend = w1R1 + w2R2 (D2b–D3b)

    Using Appendix B's two bone rotation matrices R1, R2 and equal blend weights, compute M_blend entry by entry (scalar multiplication + matrix addition), then its determinant, compared to the identity.

    Step 3 — Recover M_blend by solving a system of equations (D4)

    Appendix B gives two known point-mappings the engine logged under M_blend: v1=(1,1)→(0,1) and v2=(2,−1)→(1.5,0.5). Set up and solve the resulting system of linear equations for M_blend's four entries. Confirm your answer matches Step 2.

    Step 4 — Cost table (D5)

    Using Appendix D's raw per-frame costs (0.32ms, 0.39ms) and Fenwick's real 16.6ms/frame budget, compute each option's share of the frame budget, and the percent by which The Quaternion Blend costs more than The Direct Blend.

    Reveal the Twist

    Everything above — D1 through D4 — was worked at the shoulder. And per the same real source that documents The Quaternion Blend's advantage, the shoulder is exactly the case where it "works perfectly": two bones, one clean joint, like an elbow.

    But the hero rig has two other blend zones you haven't touched: the underarm, and the cape's mounting point — both influenced by three or more bones at once, not two. The same real source (Appendix C) says artifacts appear at exactly these more complex joints, because The Quaternion Blend's center of rotation is fixed and doesn't adapt to the actual posture.

    Does your recommendation still hold at the underarm and the cape mount — or does Dana have to pick different pipelines for different blend zones on the same rig?
