## Overview

Marguerite Adeyemi has read the same two index cards so many times that the ink is smearing under her thumb.

The meeting room smells of stale coffee and floor polish. Down the long table a colleague is insisting the whole matter is a fuss over nothing, while another keeps squaring up a stack of printed tournament tables as if neatness could settle it. In the folder in front of her sits a formal complaint from Algeria's football federation (the national body that runs the sport there), and beneath it a newspaper page where a match report was printed, pointedly, in the crime section instead of the sports section, as if a crime had occurred.

Nobody in this room is accusing anybody of anything. The sport's governing body has already announced that no rule was broken. That is exactly why she is here: the rule that allowed it is the rule they must now replace before the next World Cup, and the committee wants one answer by the end of the day.

Her colleagues have sorted themselves into camps, and each camp has brought a different card. One card changes only the clock. The other changes the clock and the way ties are broken, and it will quietly rewrite the tables of every group, for every team, at every tournament after this one.

The chair taps the table and looks her way. "Marguerite, you've been quiet. Which card goes into the minutes?"

She turns both cards face down, then face up again.

## Background

Marguerite Adeyemi is a fictional member of a rules committee at FIFA, football's world governing body, meeting in the weeks after the 1982 World Cup. **This scene is constructed.** No source records how, or by whom, the real rule change was decided, and the committee's analysis in this case is the author's, not a historical record. Her job is to recommend the rule that will govern the last two matches of every first-round group at the next World Cup. She is accountable for defending that recommendation with a count, not a feeling.

The first round of the World Cup is a set of small leagues called **groups**: four teams per group, each team plays the other three once, and the top two advance (SEE APPENDIX A, which teaches the group rules from scratch). The match that started this meeting was West Germany against Austria, the last game of Group 2. Algeria, at its first World Cup, had already finished its own schedule the day before, and had become the first African or Arab team to beat a European team at the tournament (SEE APPENDIX B). Because the Algeria match came first, West Germany and Austria walked onto the pitch (the playing field) already knowing which scorelines would send both of them through and Algeria home. West Germany scored early and the rest of the match was played with little attempt by either side to score. FIFA ruled that no rule had been broken. This case is about incentives, not character: given the rules as they stood, what would two sensible teams do?

Two options are on the table. **Same-Clock Only** (Option A) requires the final two matches of every group to kick off (start) at the same moment, so neither team can see the other result first, and leaves the tie-break ladder exactly as it was in 1982. **Same-Clock, Goals First** (Option B) requires the same simultaneous kickoffs and also changes the ladder, so goals scored are checked before goal difference (SEE APPENDIX C). A colleague wants the committee to test a simpler, tempting fix first, awarding three points for a win instead of two. The committee should know whether that changes anything before it votes (SEE APPENDICES C AND E). What FIFA actually did is recorded in Appendix E; read it after you have your own count.

Because the other match will be unfinished at kickoff, the two teams cannot know its result, so staff model that uncertainty by listing every Algeria-Chile result from 0 to 6 goals each: a grid of cells. (In the real 1982 schedule the Algeria-Chile match was already over; the grid pictures the world Option A creates, where it is not.) A scoreline that is safe in many cells is one two teams could agree to chase with little risk; a scoreline safe in few cells is a bad bet for them. The cells are a counting device, not probabilities. For each of two benchmark West Germany-Austria scorelines, 1-0 and the highest-scoring one-goal West Germany win the grid allows, staff count the cells in which both teams would go through if that cell's Algeria-Chile result were the real one, and write the count as a percent of all the cells. The committee's primary number is the **ceiling**: the larger of those two percents. The committee wants the ceiling low, because a low ceiling means even the best scoreline gives two teams only a shaky deal. Staff also report the **average** of the same two percents, the typical deal rather than the best one; the committee has not agreed which summary should count for more. A rule also has a cost beyond that number: staff can re-run each option on the other five 1982 first-round groups to see whether it rewrites a real result (SEE APPENDIX D), and any rule changes the incentives of every future group, not only 1982's.

Every figure in this case is either cited (SEE APPENDIX G) or a stated assumption (SEE APPENDIX F). The vocabulary and "how to..." skills this case asks you to research are listed in Appendix H.

## Appendix A — How a World Cup Group Works (1982)

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D1, D2). A **round-robin** is a schedule in which every team plays every other team once. In 1982 the first round of the World Cup was six round-robin **groups** of four teams, so each team played three matches and each group played six. The team that finishes first and the team that finishes second in a group advance to the next round; the other two go home. A team that advances plays on in the tournament; a team that does not is out.

**Points.** A win earns 2 points, a draw 1 point and a loss 0 points [S1] [S9].

**The tie-break ladder.** When two or more teams have the same points, they are separated by the first of these tests that gives different answers [S9]:

1. Points in all of the group's matches.
2. **Goal difference** in all group matches: goals a team scored minus goals scored against it. Goal difference can be negative.
3. Goals scored in all group matches.
4. A drawing of lots (a random draw).

If the first test that matters is a tie between the second- and third-placed teams and the first three tests cannot separate them, the cut line is decided by lot, not by a rule you can compute. In this case such a result is called **unresolved** (SEE APPENDIX C).

**In plain words: why the clock matters and why a team might coast.** A team only needs to finish in the top two. Suppose two teams are each one result away from both going through, and some scorelines in their match give them both a place. If they can see the other match's result first, they can pick the scoreline that works and play it out, because neither loses by doing so. Kickoff is the moment a match starts; if both last matches start at the same moment, neither team can see the other result first, so they cannot be sure which scoreline works. That is the idea behind Option A. A tie-break ladder decides who finishes second when teams are level, so changing the order of its tests changes which scorelines are good ones for the teams. That is the idea behind Option B: a small win adds little to a team's goals scored, so putting goals scored first changes which scorelines are good for the teams.

**Kickoff.** Before 1986 the last two matches of a group could be played on different days, so the team playing second knew the result of the first. Appendix E records what changed.

## Appendix C — The Two Options and the Counting Method

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D2, D3, D4, D6). All of this appendix's definitions are this case's own method (SEE APPENDIX F).

| | Option A — Same-Clock Only | Option B — Same-Clock, Goals First |
|---|---|---|
| Last two group matches | kick off at the same moment | kick off at the same moment |
| Tie-break ladder | points, goal difference, goals scored, lot | points, goals scored, goal difference, lot |

**The starting table.** Matches 1 to 4 in Appendix B are fixed in every cell. The two last matches are treated as not yet played: each cell supplies the Algeria-Chile result (in place of the 3-2 that was actually played, except where D2 holds it at 3-2), and the West Germany-Austria match takes the scoreline being tested. "Home" and "Away" in Appendix B are only listing labels; they do not affect any rule.

**The window and the grid.** Every match score is limited to 0-6 goals for each side. Algeria-Chile has an Algeria score a and a Chile score c, each from 0 to 6, so the grid is a table of cells, one for each (a, c) pair. These cells are an illustrative count, not probabilities: no cell is more likely than another.

**Safe, not safe, unresolved.** Pick one cell and one West Germany-Austria scoreline. Play them both into the group table and rank the four teams by the option's ladder. The scoreline is:

- **safe** in that cell if West Germany and Austria are the top two teams and the ladder fully separates second place from third;
- **unresolved** in that cell if second and third are still tied after the last test before the lot (this case never counts an unresolved cell as safe);
- **not safe** in every other cell.

**The two benchmark scorelines.** 1-0 (West Germany wins by the smallest margin, the scoreline actually played) and 6-5 (the highest-scoring one-goal West Germany win inside the 0-6 window).

**The ceiling.** For an option, the **ceiling** is the larger of the two benchmark scorelines' safe-cell percents. The committee wants the ceiling low.

## Appendix E — What Came Later

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D2, D6). Read the part under "What FIFA actually did" after you have your own recommendation.

**The three-point rule.** The 1994 World Cup was the first to award 3 points for a win in the group stage, a change Wikipedia's account says was meant to motivate attacking play [S11]. That is twelve years after this meeting, so the committee in this case could not have been following it. It appears here only because it is the obvious first idea.

**What FIFA actually did.** After the 1982 tournament FIFA revised the group system so that the final two matches in each group would be played simultaneously [S3]. In the 1986 World Cup's group pages, the last two matches of every group, Group A through Group F, are listed on the same date at the same kickoff time [S10]. Two points per win were still awarded in 1986 [S10], and the 1986 tournament summary describes Group C being won by the Soviet Union "due to goal difference" [S13]. That is Option A. This case does not say that the real choice was the better one; it says it was the one that history made.
