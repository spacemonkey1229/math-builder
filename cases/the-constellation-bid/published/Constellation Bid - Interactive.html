Overview

Aisha Odom has two spreadsheets open and one launch-slot deposit deadline blinking at the top of her inbox. Solara Orbital's board wants her recommendation in writing by tomorrow morning, and the deposit — nonrefundable — is due the same day she has to send it.

Sixty satellites. Six target orbital planes, sixty degrees apart around the equator, each one needed to close a gap in Solara's coverage map before a competitor files for the same radio spectrum. One path puts all sixty on a single rocket, into a single plane, and lets each satellite burn its own propellant afterward to walk itself around to its assigned plane. The other path books six smaller rockets, one aimed straight at each target plane, so every satellite arrives already exactly where it needs to be.

She pulls up the propellant budget her propulsion lead signed off on last month — a number that looked generous on a slide deck and considerably less generous now that she's staring at how far some of these satellites will actually have to travel. Her cursor sits over the sine key on her calculator. Behind her, the office is empty; everyone else left for the night two hours ago.

Aisha bookmarks the launch-cost quote in one tab, the propulsion budget in the other, and starts working the angles.

Background

Aisha Odom is the constellation architect at Solara Orbital, a small-satellite operator, and she owns the launch-strategy recommendation the board will vote on this week. Solara's sixty-satellite constellation is designed as a Walker-style pattern: six orbital planes, evenly spaced sixty degrees apart in right ascension of ascending node (RAAN — the angle that fixes where a satellite's orbital plane crosses the equator), with ten satellites assigned to each plane.

Two paths are open to her:

Consolidated Launch — book one large rocket, carry all sixty satellites to orbit together in a single "seed" plane, and let each satellite that isn't already in its assigned plane spend its own onboard propellant on a plane-change burn to walk itself over (SEE APPENDICES B, C and D).

Distributed Launch — book six smaller, dedicated rockets, one flown directly into each of the six target planes, so every satellite is delivered already correctly placed and no satellite ever needs to change planes after launch (SEE APPENDIX E).

Every satellite's assigned position starts as an angle — how far its target plane sits around the equator from wherever it's actually delivered — and Solara's ground-station network reads a satellite's real-time position in orbit the same way: as an angle, whose sine, cosine, and tangent are exactly what a satellite-tracking analyst computes from the unit circle (SEE APPENDIX A). The Consolidated Launch's plane-change burns are expensive in direct proportion to how large that angle is. Each of Solara's satellites has a fixed, board-approved propellant allowance set aside for this kind of maneuver; a satellite that cannot complete its assigned plane change within that allowance never reaches its target plane at all (SEE APPENDIX D).

None of the figures in this case describing Solara, Aisha, or this specific sixty-satellite mission are invented outright — every real-world figure is either cited to a real, live source or stated openly as a scenario assumption, disclosed in APPENDIX F.

Appendix C — The Plane-Change Maneuver

The required delta-v for a single combined-maneuver plane-change burn is Δv = 2·v·sin(Δθ ÷ 2), the standard astrodynamics relation for a single-burn orbital-plane rotation [S2]. This case applies it to a RAAN change as a disclosed simplification (MA2) — real RAAN changes are normally achieved via slow J2-perturbation drift, not a direct burn.

Appendix I — Plain-English Notes on Real-World Terms

Picturing the "walk." Picture Earth from above the North Pole. Each of the six target orbital planes is a giant circular path, tilted at its own angle around the equator, like six hula-hoops all balanced on the same center point but each rotated to a different angle. A plane-change burn is a single, short engine firing that gives the satellite a sideways shove big enough to swing its entire circular path over until it lines up with a different hoop. An orbit is not a fixed track — it is simply the one circular path consistent with the satellite's current position and current velocity direction, so redirecting that velocity for an instant is the same thing as reassigning which hoop the satellite belongs to.

TermPlain-English explanation
RAANThe angle, measured around the equator, marking where a satellite's orbit crosses from south to north — it says which way a given "hoop" is rotated.
Specific impulse (Isp)How efficiently a thruster converts propellant into thrust — higher means more push per kilogram, the same way miles-per-gallon measures a car's efficiency.
J2 perturbationEarth's slight equatorial bulge slowly rotates every satellite's orbital plane over time, for free — real operators often use this instead of a direct burn.
Sun-synchronous orbitAn orbit shaped so a satellite crosses any given point on Earth at the same local time every day.
Rideshare vs. dedicated launchRideshare splits one rocket's capacity across many customers (cheaper per kg); dedicated reserves the whole rocket for one customer (costlier, but you choose the exact orbit).
