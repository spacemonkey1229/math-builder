## Overview

Mara Okafor is on her knees on the band room floor at 9:40 on a Tuesday night, a ruler in one hand and a marked-up field chart in the other, and the drill charts go to the printer at nine tomorrow morning. Behind her, the head designer's laptop is still open to the transition she's supposed to have finished an hour ago — count 88 through count 104, sixteen counts, the loudest visual moment in the whole show.

Two versions of the same sixteen counts are taped side by side on the wall. In the left one, the block the band opens in stays exactly the size it starts, just spins in place, clean and safe. In the right one, that same block stretches wide open across the width of the field — the move the head designer wants for the climax, the one that makes the drum majors' eyes light up when she describes it, the one nobody has actually checked will fit in sixteen counts.

Mara's phone buzzes. A text from the head designer: "Did the math work out for the expand? Tell me it's fine."

She isn't sure it's fine. She pulls the ruler across the chart one more time, counting steps between two marks that used to be four steps apart and now aren't.

## Background

Mara Okafor is the assistant drill designer for her high school marching band, working under the head designer to finalize the drill chart for counts 88–104 of the competition ballad before it goes to the printer. She has authority to run the numbers and flag anything that won't work; the head designer makes the final call, but wants Mara's honest read, not a rubber stamp.

The band's current formation — **The Block** — is a tight 3×3 grid of nine marker positions, each representing one file of performers, spaced 4 steps apart and centered on the 50-yard line at the front hash (SEE APPENDIX B). Two designs are on the table for what The Block becomes over the next sixteen counts.

**The Expand** dilates The Block by a scale factor of 4.4, centered on the formation's own center point, stretching it into a dramatic full-field visual for the ballad's climax (SEE APPENDIX D). Every marker moves outward from center — the farther out a marker starts, the farther it has to travel.

**The Mirror Turn** rotates The Block 90° about its own center point instead. Because every marker stays the same distance from the center it started at, no marker has to cover nearly as much ground.

Every performer's move has to happen within the fixed sixteen counts, and every count is one step. Marching band has two real, well-established step sizes: a comfortable "8-to-5" step (22.5 inches per step, per count) that performers train and drill on, and a harder "6-to-5" step (30 inches per step, per count) reserved for moments that genuinely need to cover more ground fast (SEE APPENDIX C). A move that needs more than 30 inches per step in sixteen counts is not something a real high school line can execute cleanly — it is not a "try harder" problem, it is a design that has to change.

Before settling on these two designs, the team also sketched a third — rotating The Block 180° instead of 90° — and set it aside for a staging reason that has nothing to do with whether the math works (SEE APPENDIX E).

All figures in this case are sourced in APPENDIX F, or stated as explicit modeling assumptions in APPENDIX E where no public figure exists. The vocabulary and the "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in APPENDIX H — that is where Challenge 1 sends you. If you want to go beyond the required math and learn more about real marching band drill design and step sizes, SEE APPENDIX G for further reading.

## Balance argument

**Argument for A (The Expand).** The dramatic full-field visual is what the head designer and
the ballad's climax genuinely call for, and the real math says it is achievable — 27.05 in/step
at 16 counts, over the comfortable 22.5 in/step standard but with real headroom under the 30
in/step aggressive ceiling. This is not a fantasy number; it is a step size real corps-style lines
train to hit for exactly these moments.

**Argument for B (The Mirror Turn).** Every marker's travel stays small (11.25 in/step) by the
geometry of rotating about the formation's own center — a rotation can never move any point
farther than a chord of its own circle, and this block's markers all start close to center. It is
the safe, always-achievable choice, at the cost of the visual impact the head designer wants.

**Margin.** Option A's required step size (27.05 in/step) sits at **9.84% headroom** under the
real 30 in/step ceiling — inside SPEC §2's ~20% cap, computed directly from real field/step-size
sources and the stated scale factor, not picked to force closeness. See `sensitivity_model.py`.
`margin_pct`: 9.84.

**Five Socratic questions (draft).**

1. Option A's farthest marker needs 27.05 inches every single count for sixteen counts straight, with zero room to stumble. Option B's farthest marker needs less than half that. What does "achievable" actually mean here — hitting the number once, or hitting it sixteen times in a row under pressure?
2. D3 asks whether rotating the other direction changes the farthest travel distance. What did The Block's own D1 symmetry proof already tell you about the answer, before you did a single extra calculation?
3. D4 asks you to call each option's result congruent or similar to the start. Why does that distinction actually matter to a performer, not just to a geometry teacher?
4. The 180° pivot was rejected for a staging reason, not a math reason. Is that a legitimate way to eliminate an option in a math case study — and if the math had also failed for the 180° pivot, would the staging reason still have mattered?
5. Mara's text back to the head designer has to be one sentence. What does she say?

**The trap.** A Hero computes The Expand's step size, sees it clears the real 30 in/step ceiling
with room to spare, and stops — treating "the number checks out" as the end of the analysis. It
misses that a 9.84% margin at 16 counts is one late music edit away from disappearing entirely: a
single cut count doesn't just tighten the margin, it can erase it completely, and a design that is
barely fine today can become genuinely broken by the week of competition.

**Sensitivity note (SPEC §6).** None required — the stakes here are a design deadline and a
performance-execution risk, not personal loss or hardship.
