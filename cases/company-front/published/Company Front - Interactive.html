Overview

Mara Okafor is on her knees on the band room floor at 9:40 on a Tuesday night, a ruler in one hand and a marked-up field chart in the other, and the drill charts go to the printer at nine tomorrow morning.

Two versions of the same sixteen counts are taped side by side on the wall. In the left one, the block the band opens in stays exactly the size it starts, just spins in place, clean and safe. In the right one, that same block stretches wide open across the width of the field — the move the head designer wants for the climax, the one nobody has actually checked will fit in sixteen counts.

Mara’s phone buzzes. A text from the head designer: “Did the math work out for the expand? Tell me it’s fine.” She isn’t sure it’s fine. She pulls the ruler across the chart one more time.

Background

Mara Okafor is the assistant drill designer for her high school marching band, working under the head designer to finalize the drill chart for a sixteen-count transition before it goes to the printer. She has authority to run the numbers and flag anything that won’t work.

The band’s current formation — The Block — is a tight 3×3 grid of nine marker positions, spaced 4 steps apart and centered on the 50-yard line at the front hash.

The Expand dilates The Block by a scale factor of 4.4, centered on the formation’s own center point, stretching it into a dramatic full-field visual.
The Mirror Turn rotates The Block 90° about its own center point instead — every marker stays the same distance from center, so nobody has to cover nearly as much ground.

Marching band has two real step sizes: a comfortable “8-to-5” step (22.5 inches per step, per count) and a harder “6-to-5” step (30 inches per step, per count) reserved for moments that genuinely need to cover ground fast. A move that needs more than 30 inches per step in sixteen counts is not something a real high school line can execute cleanly.

Before settling on these two designs, the team also sketched a third — rotating The Block 180° instead of 90° — and set it aside for a staging reason that has nothing to do with whether the math works.

If you want to go beyond the required math and learn more about the real craft of marching band drill design, see Appendix G for further reading.

    Step 1 · Symmetry Check (D1)

    Reflect each right-half marker (x=4) across the Y-axis and confirm it lands on its left-half partner.

    Step 2 · The Expand (D2)

    Dilate every marker by the stated scale factor. Find the farthest-traveling marker's required step size.

    Travel for a marker at radius r under dilation by S = (S−1)×r.

    Step 3 · The Mirror Turn (D3)

    Rotate every marker 90° about center. Find the farthest-traveling marker's required step size.

    Step 4 · The Verdict (D6)

    Compare The Expand's step size to the real 30 in/step ceiling. Toggle the Twist below to re-check at 14 counts.
