Overview

Nina Odom sets two foam models side by side on the light table and steps back. One tapers like a horn, wide at the base and narrowing to a flat crown. The other is a perfect ball, matte white, turning slowly where she nudges it with one finger. Both are the same speaker inside — same driver, same battery, same guts — wearing two different shells.

Across the table, the client's audio engineer taps the tapered model. "This is the shape. The dispersion pattern doesn't lie." Two desks over, her own studio's creative director has the sphere pinned to the mood board with a dozen lifestyle photos of it catching light on a kitchen counter. "That's the shape people pick up in a store," he says, not looking up.

Nina's laptop is open to the cost model her studio's ops lead sent an hour ago, half-finished, tabs still loading. She has the mold quotes in one window and the freight broker's numbers in another, and neither one is done yet. Friday's client review is the last checkpoint before tooling money gets committed — after that, this shape is the shape, on every unit they ship for the life of the product. She pulls the two windows into view, side by side, the same way she set the foam models down.

She picks up the tapered shell first, turns it once in her hand, and sets it back down next to the sphere.

Background

Nina Odom is an industrial designer at a consumer-product design studio under contract to build the enclosure for a client's new portable speaker. She owns the enclosure recommendation she brings to Friday's client review, and she is accountable for defending it in front of both the client's engineering team and her own studio's creative director — whichever shape she recommends is the one whose mold gets ordered immediately afterward (see Appendix A).

Project Horn

A cone frustum — a tapered body, wide at the base and narrower at the crown, the shape the client's audio engineer wants for its acoustic dispersion pattern (see Appendix A). A tapered mold costs more to cut than a simple one, and frustum-shaped units do not nest as tightly for shipping as a flat-sided box would (see Appendices B and C).

Project Orb

A spherical enclosure — the studio's preferred signature shape, chosen for shelf appeal and the way it photographs in lifestyle marketing (see Appendix A). A sphere is a simpler shape to mold, but spheres are the well-known worst case for packing objects into a rectangular space — a stack of spheres cannot fill a shipping container the way a stack of flat-sided objects can, which drives up how much it costs to ship each finished unit (see Appendices B and C).

Nina's recommendation must be judged on one number: total cost per unit at the client's target first-year production volume — materials plus mold tooling plus shipping, computed from each shape's actual surface area and each shape's actual packing efficiency in a shipping container (see Appendices A, B and C). The client has stated a target production volume for the first year of manufacturing (see Appendix A); every dollar figure in this case is scaled against that same volume. Every sourced and assumed figure in this case is listed in Appendix D and the Modeling Assumptions table, and the vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in Appendix E — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 · Shape Geometry (D1)

    Project Horn is a cone frustum: base radius R = 3.5 in, top radius r = 2.0 in, height h = 6.0 in (Appendix A). First find the slant height, l = √((R−r)² + h²), a Pythagorean-theorem step. Then find each shape's total exterior surface area: the frustum's is π(R+r)l + πR² + πr² (lateral face plus both flat ends); the sphere's is 4πr² using Project Orb's radius, 3.5 in.

    Step 2 · Material Cost (D2)

    Using Step 1's surface areas and Appendix B's material cost per sq in, find each option's material cost per unit.

    Step 3 · Packing and Shipping (D3)

    First find each shape's own volume (frustum: (πh/3)(R²+Rr+r²); sphere: (4/3)πr³), convert cubic inches to cubic feet (1 cu ft = 1,728 cu in). Then, using Appendix C's container volume and each shape's packing efficiency, find how many units fit per container, and divide the per-container freight cost by that count to get shipping cost per unit.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    New information just came in from the client's engineering team. The acoustic dispersion certification the client requires — the one the audio engineer has been talking about — is not just a preference. It's contractual. And it turns out Project Orb's spherical shell has never been certified for that specific dispersion pattern. If Nina recommends Project Orb, the studio needs a one-time redesign-and-retest pass to get it certified: a $45,000 cost, amortized over the same 250,000-unit production run, landing on Project Orb only.
