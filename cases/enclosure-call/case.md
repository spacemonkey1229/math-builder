## Overview

Nina Odom sets two foam models side by side on the light table and steps back. One tapers like a
horn, wide at the base and narrowing to a flat crown. The other is a perfect ball, matte white,
turning slowly where she nudges it with one finger. Both are the same speaker inside — same
driver, same battery, same guts — wearing two different shells.

Across the table, the client's audio engineer taps the tapered model. "This is the shape. The
dispersion pattern doesn't lie." Two desks over, her own studio's creative director has the sphere
pinned to the mood board with a dozen lifestyle photos of it catching light on a kitchen counter.
"That's the shape people pick up in a store," he says, not looking up.

Nina's laptop is open to the cost model her studio's ops lead sent an hour ago, half-finished,
tabs still loading. She has the mold quotes in one window and the freight broker's numbers in
another, and neither one is done yet. Friday's client review is the last checkpoint before
tooling money gets committed — after that, this shape is the shape, on every unit they ship for
the life of the product. She pulls the two windows into view, side by side, the same way she set
the foam models down.

She picks up the tapered shell first, turns it once in her hand, and sets it back down next to
the sphere.

## Background

Nina Odom is an industrial designer at a consumer-product design studio under contract to build
the enclosure for a client's new portable speaker. She owns the enclosure recommendation she
brings to Friday's client review, and she is accountable for defending it in front of both the
client's engineering team and her own studio's creative director — whichever shape she recommends
is the one whose mold gets ordered immediately afterward (SEE APPENDIX A).

Two shell geometries are on the table:

**Project Horn** — a cone-frustum enclosure: a tapered body, wide at the base and narrower at the
crown, the shape the client's audio engineer wants for its acoustic dispersion pattern (SEE
APPENDIX A). A tapered mold costs more to cut than a simple one, and frustum-shaped units do not
nest as tightly for shipping as a flat-sided box would (SEE APPENDICES B and C).

**Project Orb** — a spherical enclosure: the studio's preferred signature shape, chosen for shelf
appeal and the way it photographs in lifestyle marketing (SEE APPENDIX A). A sphere is a simpler
shape to mold, but spheres are the well-known worst case for packing objects into a rectangular
space — a stack of spheres cannot fill a shipping container the way a stack of flat-sided objects
can, which drives up how much it costs to ship each finished unit (SEE APPENDICES B and C).

Nina's recommendation must be judged on one number: **total cost per unit at the client's target
first-year production volume** — materials plus mold tooling plus shipping, computed from each
shape's actual surface area and each shape's actual packing efficiency in a shipping container
(SEE APPENDICES A, B and C). The client has stated a target production volume for the first year
of manufacturing (SEE APPENDIX A); every dollar figure in this case is scaled against that same
volume. Every sourced and assumed figure in this case is listed in **APPENDIX C**, and the
vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in **APPENDIX D** — that is
where Challenge 1 sends you.

## Dilemma

tapered mold and less efficient shipping stacking OR

mold but real, well-known sphere-packing shipping waste

**Why the sphere's packing number needs no separate citation.** Unlike the tapered mold cost or
the freight rate, the ≈74.05% figure is not a market price that changes year to year — it is a
mathematical upper bound on how densely any collection of equal-sized spheres can ever be packed,
proved as the Kepler conjecture. Project Orb's real packing efficiency in an actual shipping
container, with real cardboard dividers and real handling clearance, can only be at or below this
ceiling, never above it.
