Overview

Elena Kowalski has forty minutes, a whiteboard, and a room full of employees who think cybersecurity training is going to be boring. She is about to prove them wrong with two numbers.

Every time someone logs into a bank, a hospital portal, or a company's internal system, a real mathematical fact is quietly doing the work of keeping that connection private: multiplying two huge prime numbers together is fast, but taking the result apart again — factoring it back into those two primes — is so slow it can take longer than a human lifetime, even on a fast computer, once the numbers get big enough. That gap between “easy one way, brutal the other way” is the whole reason online security works at all.

Elena cannot hand a room of trainees a real key-sized number — nobody is factoring anything close to that by hand in a forty-minute session. So she has built two small, honest stand-ins: two numbers, each one built the exact same way real keys are, each one crackable by hand if a trainee is patient enough. The exercise only works if the number is hard enough to feel real, but not so hard the room gives up before the lesson lands.

She has two candidates on her desk. Only one of them is going on the slide tomorrow morning.

Background

Elena Kowalski is a cybersecurity trainer designing a hands-on “factor this practice key” exercise for a company's security-awareness training session. The exercise is built on a real fact: modern public-key cryptography (like RSA) generates a key by multiplying two large prime numbers together, and the entire security of the system depends on the fact that multiplying is fast but factoring the result back apart is slow — and gets dramatically slower as the numbers grow larger (see Appendix A).

For training purposes, Elena needs small, hand-factorable numbers that still demonstrate the real process: a trainee tests candidate prime divisors, one at a time, until a factor is found — and a real, provable fact from number theory means nobody ever needs to test past the square root of the number being factored, because if no factor exists below that point, none exists at all (see Appendix B).

Two candidate practice numbers exist. Candidate A is 143. Candidate B is 323. Elena has worked out, from the session's real time budget, roughly how much searching she wants the exercise to demand — not so little that it feels trivial, not so much that trainees give up before the lesson lands (see Appendix C). Whichever candidate's actual difficulty lands closer to that target is the one that goes on tomorrow's slide.

Real public-key cryptography's security depends entirely on how hard the underlying number is to factor — which is exactly what this exercise's trial-division process demonstrates, at a scale a human can actually do by hand.

Appendix B — Why You Only Test Up To the Square Root

If a number N has no factor at or below its own square root, it cannot have any factor at all (other than 1 and itself) — because any factor pair p × q = N must have at least one of p or q at or below √N. This is why Elena's exercise design uses √N as the real measure of “how far a trainee has to search.”
