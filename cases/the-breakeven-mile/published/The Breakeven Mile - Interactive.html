Overview

The dealership has texted twice today, same message both times: hold expires five p.m., can't extend past close. Renata Osei reads it a third time anyway, thumb over the reply box, saying nothing back yet. On her other screen, a banking app shows the number she's been building toward for two years — enough, just barely, for the down payment on the gray sedan she test-drove Saturday, the one still sitting under a paper price tag in the dealer's side lot.

Her shift ends in twenty minutes. She's spent it, like she spends most of it, driving between four different houses on the east side — Mr. Adeyemi's blood pressure check, then across town for Ms. Whitfield's dressing change, then back again for the Ortegas — her own ride request buzzing dead on her phone screen for the third time this week, drivers passing on a fare this short and this cheap. She has made this exact loop, one way or another, almost every weekday for a year now, and she still can't say with a straight face which way is actually cheaper: the loan payment she'd be signing up for tonight, or the ride-app bill she already dreads opening every Sunday.

She pulls into the clinic lot, puts the car she's borrowing in park, and opens both tabs side by side — the loan paperwork on one side, twelve months of ride-app receipts on the other. The clock on her dash reads 4:41. She doesn't touch either tab yet.

Background

Renata Osei is a home health aide who visits patients at their own houses across the city on a fixed daily route, using whatever transportation she can arrange each morning — usually a rideshare, sometimes a coworker's car, never reliably her own. She is the only person deciding this: no household car to share, no second income to fall back on if she guesses wrong, and no one else's name on either option (see Appendix D).

Renata and the friend who's been talking through the decision with her have started calling the two paths by shorthand. Keys of Her Own means buying the used mid-size sedan currently on hold at the dealership — she would take on a loan payment, an insurance policy, and state registration fees that come due every year whether she drives the car one mile or twenty thousand (see Appendix A), on top of a much smaller, steady per-mile cost for gas, tires, and routine maintenance every time she actually drives it (see Appendix B). Both of those pieces together — a flat yearly amount plus a constant rate per mile — make Option A's total annual cost a straight-line function of how many miles she drives.

The All-App Life means staying car-free: every mile she drives for work or errands goes on a rideshare app at a flat per-mile rate, with no loan, no insurance policy, and no registration fee to carry in months she barely drives at all (see Appendix C). Option B's total annual cost is also a straight-line function of mileage — just a steeper one, with no fixed amount to start from.

Two straight lines that start in different places and climb at different rates cross exactly once. Below that crossing mileage, the flatter-but-higher-starting line (Keys of Her Own) costs more; above it, the flatter line costs less. Renata's own annual mileage — how much she actually drives in a year — decides which side of that crossing point she's standing on, and it is not a number either option gets to set for her (see Appendix D). The dealership's hold on the sedan expires today, and it will not be extended (see Appendix D). Your pitch must include four pieces: D1 — two cost equations, C_A(m) (from Appendix A's fixed costs and Appendix B's per-mile rate) and C_B(m) (from Appendix C's rideshare rate), presented as a system of two linear equations in m and total cost C.     Reveal the Twist

    One more thing. RideWise, the source behind Appendix C's $1.50/mile rideshare rate, reports that rate as a national average — but its own data shows real city-by-city rates from about $0.90/mile in most US cities up to $2.50+/mile in the highest-cost metros (New York City among them). Renata doesn't live in an average metro; she lives in one specific city, and we don't know which end of that range she's actually on.
