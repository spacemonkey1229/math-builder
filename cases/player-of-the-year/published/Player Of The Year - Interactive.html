Overview

The green room smells like hairspray and cold coffee. Renata Voss checks her phone one more time — nothing new from either committee member — then sets it face-down on the table between two laptops, each one open to a spreadsheet she has rebuilt from scratch three times tonight. Through the wall, she can hear the crowd settling into their seats, the low hum of fourteen families who flew in not knowing which of their daughters is about to be called the best volleyball player in the country. Marcus, two seats down, is still muttering about block rates. Diane hasn't looked up from her laptop in twenty minutes. Both of them handed Renata a finished formula an hour ago, confident, printed, signed. Both formulas are honest. Both are defensible. And they don't agree.

Renata is the chair of the AVCA Player Of The Year Committee, which means the split doesn't get to stay a disagreement — in eleven minutes, a name goes on a card, and Kerri Walsh-Jennings reads it out loud to a theater full of cameras. Renata pulls both spreadsheets toward her, scrolls to the bottom row of each, and stares at the two totals sitting side by side. A stagehand leans through the doorway. “Two minutes to places.” Renata doesn't answer. She's still reading.

Background

The 2027-2028 NCAA Division I Women's Volleyball season ended with the University of Wisconsin upsetting the heavily favored University of Louisville for the national championship. With the season closed, the American Volleyball Coaches Association (AVCA) runs the sport's most prestigious postseason honors. The process happens in two stages. First, a nationwide committee of coaches reviews the season and names 14 players — the best of the more than 340 Division I women's volleyball programs — to the AVCA First-Team All-American squad. The AVCA's own published materials describe this as a multi-stage process (preseason watch list, midseason additions, semifinalists, and finalists) run by a standing Awards Committee (see Appendix F). Once the 14 are set, a second body, the AVCA Player Of The Year Committee, meets to select one name from among them.

Renata Voss chairs that second committee. Its instructions, inherited from decades of past committees, are deliberately loose: weigh the players' individual statistics, and also “weigh the quality of the teams” behind them (see Appendix F). Two of Renata's committee members took that instruction and built two different, fully-worked scoring systems from it.

Marcus's system — the All-Purpose Production Index — scores all 14 finalists identically, position blind, as an unweighted average of how many standard deviations above or below the national field each player sits across five countable per-set categories: kills, aces, blocks, assists, and digs (see Appendix B, Appendix D).

Diane's system — the Role-Weighted × Team-Success Composite — scores each finalist only on the categories that define excellence at her specific position, then multiplies that score by a bonus tied to how far her team ran in the national rankings (see Appendix D).

Both systems must be run, in full, consistently, across all 14 named finalists (see Appendix A) using the same real, published national statistics and the stat definitions both committee members work from (see Appendix A, Appendix B, Appendix C). The committee cannot pick a favorite player and then choose whichever formula gets her there — it must adopt one methodology and let that methodology name the winner, whoever that turns out to be. Both formulas also rest on a short list of scenario-level design choices that a rigorous committee discloses rather than buries (see Appendix E).

Appendix C — Volleyball Statistics Glossary

Definitions below are reproduced from the AVCA National Volleyball Statistics Manual (the same public glossary AVCA-affiliated broadcasts and stat sheets use).

  • Kill — awarded any time a player's attack is unreturnable by the opposition, or leads directly to a blocking error by the opposition; results in a point or side-out.
  • Attack Attempt — any ball played over the net in a genuine attempt to score a point or side-out (spiked, tipped, or hit overhead).
  • Hitting Efficiency (Hitting %) — (Kills − Errors) / Total Attack Attempts. Compared on a similar scale to a batting average; a mark over .300 is considered elite.
  • Ace — a serve that results directly in a point (untouched, unplayable, or the receiving team is caught out of rotation).
  • Block — awarded whenever a player (alone or with a teammate) blocks the ball into the opponent's court, leading directly to a point or side-out. A Block Assist is credited to every player involved in a multi-player block; a Block Solo is one player alone.
  • Assist — awarded whenever a player passes or sets the ball to a teammate who then attacks it for a Kill (not for any other outcome).
  • Dig — awarded whenever a player passes a ball that the opposition has attacked, and keeps it in play.

Source: Stat_Definitions.pdf, AVCA National Volleyball Statistics Manual excerpt, provided with the case materials (also published at avca.org).

Appendix D — The Two Proposed Scoring Methodologies

Methodology A — Marcus's “All-Purpose Production Index”

Score every finalist identically, position blind, across the same five per-set categories every rally can generate: Kills, Aces, Blocks, Assists, Digs. Convert each to a z-score against the national field (Appendix B), then average the five z-scores with equal weight. If a finalist isn't nationally ranked in a category, that category is scored at league average (z = 0) rather than penalized — see Appendix E, Note 1 for why.

CompositeA(player) = mean( z(Kills/Set), z(Aces/Set), z(Blocks/Set), z(Assists/Set), z(Digs/Set) )
where any category the player isn't nationally ranked in contributes z = 0

Methodology B — Diane's “Role-Weighted × Team-Success Composite”

Score each finalist only on the categories that define excellence at her position, then multiply by a team-success factor tied to her team's final national RPI rank.

Role-relevant categories, by position:

PositionCategories scored
OH / RS (pin hitters)Kills/Set, Hitting %, Aces/Set
MB (middle blocker)Blocks/Set, Hitting %, Kills/Set
S (setter)Assists/Set
L/DS (libero / defensive specialist)Digs/Set

Team-success multiplier, based on the team's final-season RPI rank (out of roughly 340 Division I programs; see Appendix A for each finalist's Team RPI):

RoleZ(player) = mean( z(category) for each category in that player's role-category list )
TeamMult(RPI) = 1 + 0.5 × (1 − (RPI − 1) / 49)   if RPI ≤ 50
TeamMult(RPI) = 1.0                    if RPI > 50
CompositeB(player) = RoleZ(player) × TeamMult(Team RPI)

For example, the nation's #1-RPI team's players receive the maximum +50% bonus (TeamMult = 1.50); a team ranked exactly 50th receives no bonus at all (TeamMult = 1.00); a team ranked outside the top 50 also receives no bonus.

Appendix E — Modeling Assumptions

Every real formula still requires a few scenario-level design choices. Both are stated here, with the reasoning behind them — not their effect on the outcome. Both are worth stress-testing: try flexing each one by ±50% and see whether your recommendation changes.

  • Note 1 — the z = 0 convention (both methodologies). A finalist who isn't nationally ranked in a category is scored at league average (z = 0) for that category, not penalized as if she failed at it. The reasoning: a setter's missing kill total reflects “this isn't her job,” not “she attempted kills and scored zero.” Scoring it as a hard zero would systematically punish every specialist position (setters, liberos) purely for not doing something outside their role. This convention applies identically under Methodology B when one of a player's own role categories (Appendix D's table) is unranked for her — for example, Betsy Torres (OH) has no ranked Hit%, one of the three OH/RS role categories, and scores z = 0 for it under Methodology B just as she would under Methodology A. Some of the “not nationally ranked” cells in Appendix A were still recoverable by back-computing from raw counts in a different leaderboard tab rather than that stat's own ranked list (marked (d)) — those are real, derived numbers, not assumptions.
  • Note 2 — the national reference population (both methodologies). The mean/SD baseline in Appendix B is computed only from each stat's own national leaderboard (the top 150–1,500 ranked players), not the full D-I field of roughly 5,000-plus players. This is the only publicly published national ranking data available, and using it as “the field” is a reasonable modeling choice — but it means a z-score of 0 represents “average among an already elite subset,” not “average among every D-I player.”
  • Note 3 — the team-success multiplier curve (Methodology B). The specific shape of TeamMult(RPI) — linear decay from RPI 1 to RPI 50, capped at +50%, with zero bonus beyond RPI 50 — is a scenario assumption representing “weigh the quality of the teams,” not a universal formula every analyst would agree on. A steeper curve, a different cutoff, or a cap other than 50% would change how much team success counts relative to individual production.
Appendix F — How the AVCA Selects Its Player Of The Year

The American Volleyball Coaches Association's Division I Women's Volleyball Awards Committee is responsible for the entire multi-stage postseason honors process each year: a preseason watch list, midseason watch-list additions, a semifinalist announcement, and a final group of finalists before the Player Of The Year is named. The Awards Committee — not any single coach — runs every phase of that process.

    Working Notebook

    Step 1 — One category z-score (D1/D2)

    Pick any finalist and any of the five production categories, then compute her z-score against the national field: z = (player's value − national mean) / national SD (Appendix B). If she isn't nationally ranked in that category, remember the z = 0 convention (Appendix E, Note 1).

    Tolerance is ±0.01 — small rounding differences are fine.

    Step 2 — CompositeA for one finalist (D1)

    Compute Methodology A's CompositeA for any one finalist: the mean of her five z-scores (Kills, Aces, Blocks, Assists, Digs), scoring any unranked category at z = 0.

    Step 3 — CompositeB for one finalist (D2)

    Compute Methodology B's CompositeB for any one finalist: her RoleZ (mean of her role-relevant category z-scores, Appendix D's table) times her team's TeamMult (from the RPI formula).

    Step 4 — The margin between two finalists (D5)

    Pick a methodology and two finalists (whichever two you found closest together, or farthest apart, in your own D3 ranking). Compute the gap between their composite scores in points, and as a percent gap relative to the lower of the two.
