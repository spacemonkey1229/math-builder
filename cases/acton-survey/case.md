Xander scrolls further. Campus after campus. Some with a couple hundred learners. Some with barely a dozen. All of them expecting a call.

"You know the clock we're on," Jeff says. "Whatever method you pick, it has to actually fit — every last call of it."

Xander's cursor hovers over two columns he's already sketched out on a legal pad beside the laptop: one labeled *Simple*, one labeled *Fair*. He hasn't circled either one yet.

"Well?" Jeff says. "Which way you calling this?"

## Background

Laura and Jeff Sandefer founded the first Acton Academy and have spent years building out the wider Acton network — dozens of independently run campuses worldwide, each using the same learner-driven model but each free to call its learners by its own nickname: Eagles at one campus, Heroes at another, Explorers, Champions, Stars. With their retirement three months away, Jeff and Laura want to settle a question that's been informally debated for years: should every Acton campus share one common learner mascot?

Rather than guess, Jeff wants a real, statistically defensible survey. Because he wants every age of learner to have a voice, he has decided the survey will be conducted by personal phone call — Jeff will call each campus's guide, and the guide will put the learner on the line. Two questions will be asked of every learner reached: whether the network should adopt one common mascot, and, if so, what that mascot should be. Every one of these calls, guide coordination and learner interview alike, runs **15 to 20 minutes** (SEE APPENDIX A).

It is not possible, and Jeff and Xander agree it is not necessary, to call every learner at every campus. Some Acton campuses are brand new with only a handful of learners; others are long established with well over a hundred. Jeff and Xander have restricted the survey to campuses with at least two studios — fifty campuses network-wide, a real, complete 2021 roster of **1,420 learners** total (SEE APPENDIX A). Jeff and Xander have agreed the survey will interview a sample of **142 learners — exactly 10% of the population** — enough real breadth to speak for the network without blowing past his three-month window.

Xander Morgan, Jeff's right-hand for anything statistical, has been asked to design *how* those 142 learners get chosen. He has narrowed the real options down to two. Every single call in this survey — coordinating with a campus guide and interviewing a learner alike — is placed personally by Jeff himself, one call at a time; nothing here can be split across a team (SEE APPENDIX A). That makes total personal call-time, and whether it fits Jeff's three-month runway, a real constraint, not a decorative one (SEE APPENDIX D).

The fifty campuses vary enormously in size — from a dozen learners to well over a hundred and sixty (SEE APPENDIX B). Xander must decide whether that size difference is something his sampling method needs to correct for, or something he can safely leave to chance.

All figures in this case are sourced or explicitly stated as scenario assumptions (SEE APPENDICES E AND F). The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in Appendix G — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

## Dilemma

You are Xander Morgan.

Do you:

A.) Simple Random Sample — the simplest method to administer: one flat draw of 1,420 IDs, no per-campus quotas to track, while Jeff places every 15–20-minute call himself, one at a time OR

B.) Stratified Sample — more calls and coordination required, but it corrects for wildly uneven campus sizes so small and large campuses are proportionally represented

### Appendix C — Computing Each Method

**Option A, Simple Random Sample — probability a campus is shut out entirely.**

Drawing 142 learners at random from all 1,420, without regard to which campus anyone belongs to, is a **hypergeometric** sampling problem: the chance that *zero* of a given campus's $N_i$ learners land in the sample of $n = 142$ drawn from the full population of $N = 1{,}420$ is

$$P(\text{zero from campus } i) = \dfrac{\binom{N - N_i}{n}}{\binom{N}{n}}$$

This is the **hypergeometric distribution** — the correct probability model any time you're drawing a fixed-size sample from a finite population *without* putting anything back, which is exactly what a phone survey does (you don't call the same learner twice) [S2].

Summing that probability across all fifty campuses gives the **expected number of campuses that receive zero calls** under Option A — a real number, not a worst case, because it is the average outcome over every possible random draw.

**Option B, Stratified Sample — proportional allocation.**

$$n_i = \text{round}\left(142 \times \dfrac{N_i}{1{,}420}\right)$$

applied to every campus. Because 142 does not divide evenly across fifty campuses of different sizes, rounding every campus's raw share independently does not sum back to exactly 142 — the **largest-remainder method** (also called Hamilton's method, first used to apportion the real U.S. House of Representatives) [S3] fixes this: round every campus down first, then hand the leftover seats, one at a time, to whichever campuses had the largest rounded-off remainder, until the total reaches 142 exactly.

### Appendix D — Jeff's Call-Time Budget

| Call type | Who it's with | Length |
|---|---|---|
| Learner interview | The learner (via their guide) | 15–20 min (this case uses 17.5 min, the midpoint — MA2, Appendix E) |
| Guide coordination call | The campus guide, to schedule that campus's block of learner calls | 15 min (MA1, Appendix E) |

- Every campus a method actually reaches needs **exactly one** guide-coordination call, regardless of how many of that campus's learners end up interviewed.
- **Option B** reaches all 50 campuses by construction (Appendix C) — 50 guide-coordination calls, guaranteed.
- **Option A** reaches only however many campuses happen to have at least one learner drawn into the random sample — use your Appendix C(A) expected-shutout figure to estimate how many campuses that is, on average.
- Learner-interview time is identical either way: 142 interviews × 17.5 minutes.
- Total call time = learner-interview time + guide-coordination time, converted to hours, checked against Jeff's 3-month runway.
