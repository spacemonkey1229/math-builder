Overview

The boil-water notice has been taped to the door of the county clerk's office for six weeks now, and Devon Aldrich can see it through the windshield from the parking lot: a single sheet of paper, curling at the corner, telling four thousand people to keep boiling their tap water. Devon's phone buzzes against the truck's cupholder — a text from the mayor's office. Ballot language due Friday. One number. They won't print a range.

Devon has spent nine years keeping this town's water system running, and the inspection report sitting on the passenger seat is the reason the old tank got condemned: a rust bloom the size of a dinner plate, eating through steel nobody had budgeted to repaint in time. Two engineering firms sent back two very different tank designs to replace it — one shaped like a giant teardrop balanced on a single pole, the other a squat cylinder standing on six legs like a water bug. Both hold exactly the same amount of water. Both firms swear their design is the smarter bet.

Devon flips past the cover pages to the cost tables, past the spec sheets, past a hand-drawn note from the county attorney: the resolution shall state a specific dollar amount, not to exceed. No ranges. No “up to.”

One number. Due Friday. Devon opens a blank spreadsheet and starts typing.

Background

Devon Aldrich is the sole water-system engineer for a town of roughly 4,000 residents, responsible for a single elevated storage tank that supplies gravity-fed pressure to the entire distribution system. The town's only elevated tank failed a corrosion inspection this spring and has been taken out of service, forcing a boil-water advisory that has now run six weeks. A bond measure to fund a full replacement goes before voters in three weeks, and state election code requires the ballot resolution to name one specific dollar amount for the bond — not a range, not a not-to-exceed figure (see Appendix A).

The town's engineer of record sized the replacement tank's required capacity at 750,000 gallons (MA2), based on the town's population and standard water-system sizing guidance (see Appendix A). Both competing designs must hold exactly that capacity — this is a hard constraint, not a variable either firm can change to make its own design look better.

The vocabulary and “how to…” procedures needed to work through this case are listed in Appendix E. Two real, commonly built elevated-tank designs are on the table (see Appendices B and C). The Spheroid is a single-shaft, teardrop-shaped steel tank — the “golf ball on a stick” — that fabricators build with a smaller support footprint and less coated exterior surface than most other elevated shapes, but the specialized curved-steel knuckle welding it requires is a slower, costlier build. The Multi-Column is a plain cylindrical steel tank standing on several straight support legs — cheaper and faster to fabricate because its steel components are simple and repeatable, but it exposes more total surface area to the weather, and every square foot of that surface has to be recoated on the same schedule the Spheroid's smaller surface does.

There is a second real complication buried in the same engineering study, and it has nothing to do with surface area: at this capacity, only one company in the country is currently qualified to build a Multi-Column tank, while three separate companies are qualified to build a Spheroid (see Appendix G). A design only one company can build cannot be competitively bid — the town would have to take whatever price and schedule that single fabricator offers, with no other bidder to check either one. For a town six weeks into a boil-water advisory, already racing a Friday ballot deadline, “the cheaper design on paper” and “the design least likely to blow its price or its timeline” are not automatically the same design.

Neither design is a mistake. Every figure in this case — construction cost, maintenance cost, and coated surface area — is drawn from a real 2019 engineering cost study of an actual elevated-tank replacement project, adapted to Devon's town (see Appendix F). The tank's manufacturer-rated exterior surface area is what a recoating contractor actually has to cover, on a fixed maintenance cycle, for as long as the tank stands (see Appendix D). Devon has to turn two engineering reports, a sizing requirement, and a state law that only accepts one number, into a single dollar figure the town can vote on by Friday.

A design only one company can build cannot be put out to competitive bid: the town has no second quote to check the first one against, no leverage if that single fabricator's price rises or its schedule slips, and no fallback bidder if that company cannot take the job.

Scope note: the real memo states these fabricator counts specifically “at 0.5-0.75 MG capacity” — the two capacities it evaluated.     Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Solve for each shape's unknown dimension (D1)

    Given the required capacity (750,000 gal, converted to cubic feet) and each design's real diameter, solve for the Spheroid's idealized radius and the Multi-Column's idealized height.

    Step 2 — Idealized surface-area comparison (D2)

    Using your D1 dimensions, compute each idealized shape's total surface area.

    Step 3 — Steel-area-per-gallon rate (D3)

    Divide each idealized area from D2 by the required capacity, converted to a rate per 1,000 gallons.

    Step 4 — Real cost-per-square-foot (D4)

    Divide each design's real total capital cost (Appendix B/C) by its real fabricated exterior area.

    Now that you've checked your rates — in your own written pitch, explain in a paragraph why these two rates differ even though both tanks are welded, coated steel.

    Step 5 — Lifecycle cost per gallon and your ballot figure (D5)

    Compute each design's real total 60-year lifecycle cost per gallon (Appendix D), then build your own bar chart or equivalent visual comparing them in your write-up.

    This calculator checks your arithmetic — it does not tell you which design to recommend, or what dollar figure to put on the ballot. That's your D5 write-up.

    Step 6 — Fabricator-competition response (D6)

    Not a number to check — a short written response. Use this box to draft it before you finalize your pitch.

    Does the real difference in qualified fabricators (3 vs. 1, Appendix G) change your D5 recommendation? A strong answer names a specific reason either way — not just a restated position.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    Once D2's idealized areas are on the table, a recommendation usually starts to form around “the Spheroid has less area, so it must be the smarter build.” It has less area — and it still costs more per gallon, over 60 years, than the design with more area. Building a smaller, more elegant curved shape well enough to hold water safely at height takes far more specialized, slower, costlier fabrication per square foot than a plain cylinder does, and that fabrication-cost gap is large enough to overwhelm the area advantage.

    There's a second layer once the dollar comparison settles: the real engineering firm behind every number in this case also flagged that the cheaper design, by dollars, has only one qualified fabricator — no competitive bid, no second quote, no fallback if that one company's price or schedule slips. A purely least-cost recommendation has to reckon with that real, sourced risk too.
