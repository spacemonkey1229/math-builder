Case Study: The Painted Gallon
MS | Math | Case Study | 2026 | Adventure

## Overview

The boil-water notice has been taped to the door of the county clerk's office for six weeks now, and Devon Aldrich can see it through the windshield from the parking lot: a single sheet of paper, curling at the corner, telling four thousand people to keep boiling their tap water. Devon's phone buzzes against the truck's cupholder — a text from the mayor's office. *Ballot language due Friday. One number. They won't print a range.*

Devon has spent nine years keeping this town's water system running, and the inspection report sitting on the passenger seat is the reason the old tank got condemned: a rust bloom the size of a dinner plate, eating through steel nobody had budgeted to repaint in time. Two engineering firms sent back two very different tank designs to replace it — one shaped like a giant teardrop balanced on a single pole, the other a squat cylinder standing on six legs like a water bug. Both hold exactly the same amount of water. Both firms swear their design is the smarter bet.

Devon flips past the cover pages to the cost tables, past the spec sheets, past a hand-drawn note from the county attorney: *the resolution shall state a specific dollar amount, not to exceed. No ranges. No "up to."*

One number. Due Friday. Devon opens a blank spreadsheet and starts typing.

## Background

Devon Aldrich is the sole water-system engineer for a town of roughly 4,000 residents, responsible for a single elevated storage tank that supplies gravity-fed pressure to the entire distribution system. The town's only elevated tank failed a corrosion inspection this spring and has been taken out of service, forcing a boil-water advisory that has now run six weeks. A bond measure to fund a full replacement goes before voters in three weeks, and state election code requires the ballot resolution to name one specific dollar amount for the bond — not a range, not a not-to-exceed figure (SEE APPENDIX A).

The town's engineer of record sized the replacement tank's required capacity at 750,000 gallons (MA2), based on the town's population and standard water-system sizing guidance (SEE APPENDIX A). Both competing designs must hold exactly that capacity — this is a hard constraint, not a variable either firm can change to make its own design look better.

The vocabulary and "how to…" procedures needed to work through this case are listed in (SEE
APPENDIX E). Two real, commonly built elevated-tank designs are on the table (SEE APPENDICES B AND
C). The Spheroid is a single-shaft, teardrop-shaped steel tank — the "golf ball on a stick" — that fabricators build with a smaller support footprint and less coated exterior surface than most other elevated shapes, but the specialized curved-steel knuckle welding it requires is a slower, costlier build. The Multi-Column is a plain cylindrical steel tank standing on several straight support legs — cheaper and faster to fabricate because its steel components are simple and repeatable, but it exposes more total surface area to the weather, and every square foot of that surface has to be recoated on the same schedule the Spheroid's smaller surface does.

There is a second real complication buried in the same engineering study, and it has nothing to do with surface area: at this capacity, only one company in the country is currently qualified to build a Multi-Column tank, while three separate companies are qualified to build a Spheroid (SEE APPENDIX G). A design only one company can build cannot be competitively bid — the town would have to take whatever price and schedule that single fabricator offers, with no other bidder to check either one. For a town six weeks into a boil-water advisory, already racing a Friday ballot deadline, "the cheaper design on paper" and "the design least likely to blow its price or its timeline" are not automatically the same design.

Neither design is a mistake. Every figure in this case — construction cost, maintenance cost, and coated surface area — is drawn from a real 2019 engineering cost study of an actual elevated-tank replacement project, adapted to Devon's town (SEE APPENDIX F). The tank's manufacturer-rated exterior surface area is what a recoating contractor actually has to cover, on a fixed maintenance cycle, for as long as the tank stands (SEE APPENDIX D). Devon has to turn two engineering reports, a sizing requirement, and a state law that only accepts one number, into a single dollar figure the town can vote on by Friday.

## Dilemma

You are Devon Aldrich.
Do you:
A.) Recommend The Spheroid — the single-shaft, teardrop-shaped elevated tank OR
B.) Recommend The Multi-Column — the cylindrical tank on multiple support legs

## Appendix G — Fabricator Competition and Procurement Risk

Referenced in Background and Challenge 2c. Real data from the same 2019 Black & Veatch memorandum
(SEE APPENDIX F, S17) — not an invented scenario detail.

| Design | Qualified fabricators at this capacity | Real engineering-firm note |
|---|---|---|
| The Spheroid | 3 (McDermott, Caldwell Tanks, Landmark Structures) [S17] | Multiple qualified bidders — a competitive bidding environment is expected. |
| The Multi-Column | 1 (Caldwell Tanks only) [S17] | "Because Caldwell is the only manufacturer that is qualified to manufacture this style of tank, there is added risk in choosing this tank alternative due to lack of competition." [S17] |

This is not a hypothetical concern raised by this case's author — it is the real engineering
firm's own stated caution, in the same memo that supplies every cost and area figure in Appendices
B-D. A design only one company can build cannot be put out to competitive bid: the town has no
second quote to check the first one against, no leverage if that single fabricator's price rises
or its schedule slips, and no fallback bidder if that company cannot take the job. This does not
change any dollar figure in Appendix D — it is a real, separate risk dimension the numbers alone
do not capture, and it is the strongest sourced argument for choosing the more expensive design.

**Scope note:** the real memo states these fabricator counts specifically "at 0.5-0.75 MG
capacity" — the two capacities it evaluated. This case's required capacity (750,000 gal = 0.75 MG)
sits inside that sourced range. The fabricator-count figures in this table are not claimed to hold
at capacities meaningfully outside 0.5-0.75 MG.
