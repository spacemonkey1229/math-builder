Overview

Dr. Renata Cho has one laptop, two tabs open, and a mobile vaccination-and-testing unit idling in a parking lot on the wrong side of the county. On the left tab: a case-count model she built herself, fitted to Eastgate's earliest numbers, curving upward in a way that makes her stomach tighten. On the right: the county's own daily dashboard — the numbers her director actually watches, the numbers that will show up in tomorrow's briefing — climbing, but slower, gentler, nothing like the shape on the left.

Her phone buzzes. It's the mobile unit's driver: “Still in the Riverside lot. You said stand by. Standing by.” Riverside has been quiet for two weeks. Eastgate hasn't.

The gap between her two screens is the whole problem. One shows what she believes is actually happening. The other shows what the county can currently prove is happening. Moving the unit off a model nobody has confirmed costs her department real money and real credibility if she's wrong. Waiting for the dashboard to catch up costs something else entirely — every day the unit sits in Riverside is a day Eastgate goes uncovered while its numbers keep climbing underneath the surface everyone else can see.

She pulls up her derivative work again. Somewhere in these two curves is the exact day each of them crosses the line her department already agreed would trigger a move. She just has to find it — and defend which curve she trusted.

Background

Dr. Renata Cho is the epidemiologist responsible for mobile-resource allocation at the Two Rivers County Health Department. She owns the call on when the department's single mobile vaccination-and-testing unit — currently stationed in the Riverside neighborhood — gets redeployed to Eastgate, where a new respiratory-illness cluster is accelerating (SEE APPENDIX A). The unit cannot be split or duplicated; moving it is a same-day, one-time decision, and the department's own policy requires that decision be defended with the math behind it, not a gut call.

Two curves are available to make that call. The first is Eastgate's true daily new-case count — never directly observable, but modeled by fitting an exponential growth curve to the earliest confirmed cases (SEE APPENDIX A). The second is Eastgate's reported daily new-case count — the only curve the department can actually see in real time, because it depends on how large a share of true cases the county's testing sites are actually catching, a share that is not constant (SEE APPENDIX C). Two trigger designs are on the table, and they disagree about which curve to trust (SEE APPENDIX B): the department's existing policy sets a numeric growth-rate threshold for each curve, and neither option is free — a wrong or late call has a real, disclosed cost either way (SEE APPENDIX D). The vocabulary and “how to…” procedures needed to build and compare both trigger designs are listed in (SEE APPENDIX E), and every sourced or assumed figure in this case is documented in (SEE APPENDIX F).

The two options are named on Dr. Cho's whiteboard: The True-Curve Trigger, which redeploys the unit the moment the modeled true-infection curve's growth rate crosses its threshold, or The Reported-Curve Trigger, which waits for the only curve the department can directly observe to cross its own threshold. Dr. Cho has to pick one, defend the total-cost math behind it, and radio the driver before the day is out.

Appendices

Appendix A — Outbreak Model Inputs

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D1, D3). Eastgate's true daily new-case count is modeled as exponential growth, unmitigated by any intervention yet in this window:

I(t) = I0 · e(k·t), where t is measured in days since the model's start date.

InputValue used
I0 — true new cases on day 04 cases/day (MA1)
k — true daily growth-rate constant0.094 per day (MA2)

RESEARCH FLAG RESOLVED: no public source publishes Two Rivers County's own real-time case count or growth-rate constant — this is internal, day-to-day operational surveillance data specific to one (fictional) county. I0 is a stated, disclosed Modeling Assumption (MA1). k is also a stated Modeling Assumption (MA2), directly informed by a real, cited early-outbreak growth-rate figure — see Appendix D's Modeling Assumptions and Appendix F, [S1].

Appendix B — Two Proposed Trigger Designs

Referenced in Background and Dilemma. Both designs watch for the same underlying kind of signal — a case-count curve's growth rate crossing a threshold — but apply it to two different curves.

DesignSignal watchedTrigger conditionProposed by
Option A — The True-Curve TriggerI(t), Appendix A's modeled true-infection curveI′(t) ≥ Appendix D's True-Curve thresholdDr. Cho's own design
Option B — The Reported-Curve TriggerD(t) = I(t) · c(t), Appendix C's reported-case curveD′(t) ≥ Appendix D's Reported-Curve thresholdDepartment's existing default policy

Neither design is free to act on. Option A relies on an unconfirmed model of a curve nobody can directly observe, which is why the department's own protocol requires a paid verification step before committing the unit on that signal alone (SEE APPENDIX D). Option B relies only on data the department already has, but Appendix C's declining testing-capture rate means D(t) is not simply a scaled-down copy of I(t) — real, published research on outbreak response documents that testing and reporting capacity commonly comes under strain as case volume rises, reducing the share of true infections that get captured and reported in real time, which is exactly the mechanism Appendix C's capture-rate model represents.

Appendix C — Testing Capture-Rate Model

Referenced in Background, Appendix B, and Challenge 3 (D2, D4). Not every true infection in Eastgate is caught and reported — only a share, and that share is not constant. It is modeled as a declining linear function of time:

c(t) = c0 − m·t, where c(t) is the fraction of that day's true cases that show up in the county's reported numbers.

InputValue used
c0 — capture rate at day 00.65 (65% of true cases captured) (MA3)
m — daily rate of capture-rate decline0.015 per day (MA3)

The reported-case curve is then D(t) = I(t) · c(t) — Appendix A's true-infection curve, scaled down by whatever share of it the county's testing capacity can actually catch that day.

RESEARCH FLAG RESOLVED: no public source publishes any specific county's real-time testing-capture-rate curve for a specific (fictional) local outbreak — stated as Modeling Assumption MA3.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Chain rule and the True-Curve trigger day (D1)

    Differentiate I(t) = I0·e(k·t) using the chain rule (outer function eu, inner function u=k·t). Then find t_A, the day I′(t) first equals Appendix D's True-Curve threshold (1.00).

    Step 2 — Product rule and the Reported-Curve trigger day (D2)

    Build D(t) = I(t)·c(t) from Appendices A and C, differentiate using the product rule, and find t_B, the day D′(t) first equals Appendix D's Reported-Curve threshold (0.40).

    Step 3 — D(t)'s local maximum (D3)

    Find the day D′(t)=0 (D(t)'s one critical point in this window) and D(t)'s value there. Then check: does I(t) have any turning point of its own anywhere in this window?

    Step 4 — Quotient rule, the lag, and total cost (D4)

    Differentiate R(t)=D(t)/I(t) via the quotient rule (full (D′I−DI′)/I² setup) and confirm it reduces to c(t). Then use t_A and t_B to find the lag, the gap in that day's new-case count, and each option's total cost.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    Once D3's classification is on the table, Dr. Cho's director asks the obvious follow-up: “If reported cases start falling around day 32.695, doesn't that mean the outbreak is slowing down?” The honest answer, per D3, is no — I(t) never turns over in this window; D(t)'s own peak is an artifact of the declining testing-capture rate (Appendix C), not a real slowdown. Spooked by that realization, the director tightens the verification protocol out of extra caution: its cost rises to the amount the calculator below computes when you apply the twist.
