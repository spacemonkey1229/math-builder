## Background

**Neil and Rebecca Fawson** moved to Aurora, Colorado for Neil's work as a logistics strategist at a regional distribution firm. They recently bought their first home and have two young children, with hopes of having more. Rebecca works part-time running her own interior-design consultancy. The couple wants a life insurance policy that protects the family financially if Neil dies — specifically, enough to pay off the remaining mortgage and replace Neil's income until their youngest child turns 18 (**SEE APPENDIX B**).

**Jake Johnson** is a licensed life insurance agent at **Meridian Mutual**, certified to sell in Colorado and two neighboring states. Working from the family's stated needs and Neil's health profile (**SEE APPENDIX C**), Jake has narrowed the choice to two real categories of policy, priced using current, publicly available market rate data rather than a single company's private quote (**SEE APPENDIX D**):

- **Whole Life Insurance** — coverage that lasts Neil's entire life, with a guaranteed cash-value component that grows over time and can be borrowed against or cashed out. Premiums are level for life, but substantially higher than term for the same death benefit.
- **Term Life Insurance** — coverage for a fixed number of years (commonly 10 to 30). Premiums are level for the chosen term and dramatically lower than whole life, but the policy pays nothing and has no cash value if Neil outlives the term.

Both options can be written for any death-benefit amount the family qualifies for and chooses to pay for — the first number Jake has to help them pin down is how much coverage they actually need (**SEE APPENDIX B**), before either policy type can be priced (**SEE APPENDIX D**).

If you want to go beyond the required math and read more about how real families and agents think through this decision, **SEE APPENDIX E** for further reading.

The vocabulary and the "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in **Appendix F** — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

## Appendix D: Life Insurance Market Data & Modeling Assumptions

**How much coverage, in general?** A standard, real-world method for estimating a family's coverage need is to add the household's other major debts (here, just the mortgage) to an income-replacement figure found by multiplying the insured person's annual income by the number of years of support the family wants — the core arithmetic behind both the simple "multiple of income" method and the more detailed DIME (Debt, Income, Mortgage, Education) method real agents use.
*Source: Ritter Insurance Marketing, "4 Ways to Calculate Your Clients' Life Insurance Needs," 2025.*

**Standard term lengths.** Term policies are sold in a small number of standard lengths: 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 years. A buyer picks the shortest one that still covers their full stated need — a longer term than necessary costs more for protection the family will no longer need by the time it ends.

**Real 2026 market premium benchmarks (Preferred/Preferred-Plus-adjacent, non-smoker, average health, $500,000 of coverage, age ~30, 20-year level term for the term column):**

| | Term (20-year level) | Whole Life (level, lifetime) |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly premium, $500,000 coverage | $36/month | $444/month |

*Source: MoneyGeek, "Term Life vs. Whole Life Insurance (Cost, Pros and Cons)," updated August 2026. Rates for other coverage amounts scale roughly in proportion to the $500,000 benchmark — insurers price term and whole life per $1,000 of face amount, so a policy for a different amount can be estimated by scaling this benchmark by the ratio of the two face amounts.*

**Guaranteed whole life cash-value growth.** Whole life policies credit a **guaranteed minimum** interest rate to the policy's cash value every year, compounded annually. Across major U.S. mutual insurers in 2026, published guaranteed minimum rates fall in a **2%–4%** range.
*Source: InsuranceGeek, "Whole Life Insurance Dividends | 2006–2026 Rate History," updated July 2026.*

**Whole life dividend-inclusive crediting rate.** On top of the guaranteed minimum, many whole life policies (called "participating" policies) may also receive an annual dividend, declared by the insurer. The source below does not publish a single combined average — it lists each major mutual carrier's individual 2026 dividend interest rate: MassMutual 6.60%, New York Life 6.40%, Guardian 6.25%, Penn Mutual 6.00%, Northwestern Mutual 5.75%. Averaged across those 5 real, currently-published rates, the **typical total (dividend-inclusive) crediting rate is about 6.20%**.
*Source: InsuranceGeek, "Whole Life Insurance Dividends | 2006–2026 Rate History," updated July 2026.*

**A real, safe alternative.** As of August 2026, the best publicly advertised 5-year Certificate of Deposit (CD) rate available to any saver — fully insured, contractually guaranteed for the full 5 years — is **4.50% APY**.
*Source: CNBC Select, "The best 5-year CD rates of August 2026," accessed August 2026.*

**8 Common Life Insurance Riders** (a rider is an optional add-on that changes what a policy covers, at an added cost):

| Rider | What it does |
|---|---|
| Accelerated Death Benefit | Lets the insured access part of the death benefit early if diagnosed with a qualifying terminal or critical illness. |
| Accidental Death and Dismemberment | Pays an additional benefit if death or serious injury results from an accident. |
| Child Protection Rider | Adds a small term policy covering the insured's children. |
| Critical Illness Rider | Pays a benefit on diagnosis of a covered serious illness. |
| Disability Income / Long-Term Care Rider | Pays a benefit, or waives premiums, if the insured becomes disabled or needs long-term care. |
| Return of Premium Rider | Refunds some or all premiums paid if the insured outlives the policy term. |
| Term Conversion Rider | Allows converting a term policy to a permanent policy later without new medical underwriting. |
| Waiver of Premium Rider | Waives future premiums if the insured becomes totally disabled. |

*Source: Baily Insurance Agency, "Common Life Insurance Riders (What Do They Cost? Who Should Buy Them?)."*

## Modeling Assumptions

**MA1 — Fictionalized company and buyer.** This case fictionalizes the insurance company (as "Meridian Mutual") and every family/agent name, because a real premium quote and a real cash-value crediting schedule both require an actual, individualized, medically underwritten application — not something obtainable or verifiable without one. In their place, this case uses real, current, publicly published *market-average* rate data (Appendix D) for a buyer matching Neil's real profile, the same benchmarking approach a real agent uses to sanity-check any single company's quote.

**MA2 — Nearest published age bracket.** The real rate benchmarks in Appendix D are published at 5–10 year age intervals; no publicly available source breaks out a rate specifically for age 26. This case uses the nearest published bracket (age ~30) as its real-data anchor, rather than interpolating or estimating an unpublished number.

**MA3 — Linear scaling by face amount.** The Appendix D rate benchmarks are quoted for $500,000 of coverage. This case scales them in proportion to whatever coverage amount D1 computes, because both term and whole life premiums are conventionally priced per $1,000 of face amount — a standard actuarial pricing convention, not an assumption invented for this case.

**MA4 — Simplified whole life cash-value starting point.** This case models the whole life policy's guaranteed cash value as starting, in year 1, at an amount equal to one year's premium, and growing geometrically from there at the guaranteed rate. Real whole life cash-value schedules are insurer-specific, grow more slowly than this in the earliest years (due to underwriting and acquisition costs), and are only available from an actual policy illustration — this case's simplified starting point is a stated, disclosed approximation that keeps the projection computable from public data alone.

**MA5 — A single guaranteed growth rate for projections.** D3's projection uses 3% as the guaranteed annual cash-value growth rate — the midpoint of the real, currently published 2%–4% guaranteed-rate range (Appendix D) — since no single universal "the" guaranteed rate exists across insurers; a specific number requires a specific company's policy.
