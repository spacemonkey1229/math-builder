Overview

The porch light is already on when Jake Johnson pulls into the Fawsons' driveway, laptop bag over one shoulder, a manila folder tucked under the other arm. Two folders, actually — he switches them so the thicker one is on top, then switches back.

Rebecca answers the door with a one-year-old on her hip and a four-year-old already asking who's here. "Come in, come in — Neil's just getting the good chairs from the garage."

At the kitchen table, Neil sets down two mismatched folding chairs and drops into one of them. "So," he says. "You said you'd have an answer for us tonight."

Jake opens his laptop instead of either folder. Two browser tabs sit open side by side — one labeled Whole Life Illustration, the other Term Quote — both running the same numbers through the same calculator, spitting out two very different monthly figures. He's run this comparison a hundred times for a hundred families. He has never once been sure, watching a young couple's faces across a kitchen table, which one he'd choose if it were his own name on the application.

Rebecca sits down, bouncing the baby lightly. "We trust you, Jake. Just tell us which one."

Jake looks at the two tabs. He clicks between them once, twice, then leaves the cursor hovering between the two numbers.

He opens his mouth.

Background

Neil and Rebecca Fawson moved to Aurora, Colorado for Neil's work as a logistics strategist at a regional distribution firm. They recently bought their first home and have two young children, with hopes of having more. Rebecca works part-time running her own interior-design consultancy. The couple wants a life insurance policy that protects the family financially if Neil dies — specifically, enough to pay off the remaining mortgage and replace Neil's income until their youngest child turns 18 (Appendix B).

A single-family home in Aurora, Colorado — the Fawsons' city. Photo: Jeffrey Beall, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0.

Working through the numbers — the same kind of arithmetic Jake runs for every family. Photo: Dave Dugdale, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 2.0.

Jake Johnson is a licensed life insurance agent at Meridian Mutual, certified to sell in Colorado and two neighboring states. Working from the family's stated needs and Neil's health profile (Appendix C), Jake has narrowed the choice to two real categories of policy, priced using current, publicly available market rate data rather than a single company's private quote (Appendix D):

Whole Life Insurance — coverage that lasts Neil's entire life, with a guaranteed cash-value component that grows over time and can be borrowed against or cashed out. Premiums are level for life, but substantially higher than term for the same death benefit.

Term Life Insurance — coverage for a fixed number of years (commonly 10 to 30). Premiums are level for the chosen term and dramatically lower than whole life, but the policy pays nothing and has no cash value if Neil outlives the term.

Both options can be written for any death-benefit amount the family qualifies for and chooses to pay for — the first number Jake has to help them pin down is how much coverage they actually need (Appendix B), before either policy type can be priced (Appendix D).

Want to go beyond the required math and read more about how real families and agents work through this decision? See Appendix E for further reading.

Appendix D — Life Insurance Market Data & Modeling Assumptions

How much coverage, in general? A standard, real-world method for estimating a family's coverage need is to add the household's other major debts (here, just the mortgage) to an income-replacement figure found by multiplying the insured person's annual income by the number of years of support the family wants — the core arithmetic behind both the simple "multiple of income" method and the more detailed DIME (Debt, Income, Mortgage, Education) method real agents use. (Source: Ritter Insurance Marketing, 2025.)

Standard term lengths. Term policies are sold in a small number of standard lengths: 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 years. A buyer picks the shortest one that still covers their full stated need.

Real 2026 market premium benchmarks (Preferred/Preferred-Plus-adjacent, non-smoker, average health, $500,000 coverage, age ~30, 20-year level term):

Term (20-year level)Whole Life (level, lifetime)
Monthly premium, $500,000 coverage$36/month$444/month

Source: MoneyGeek, "Term Life vs. Whole Life Insurance," updated August 2026. Rates for other coverage amounts scale roughly in proportion to this $500,000 benchmark — insurers price per $1,000 of face amount.

Guaranteed whole life cash-value growth. Across major U.S. mutual insurers in 2026, published guaranteed minimum rates fall in a 2%–4% range. (Source: InsuranceGeek, updated July 2026.)

Whole life dividend-inclusive crediting rate. The source below lists each major mutual carrier's individual 2026 dividend rate (5.75%–6.60%), not a combined average; averaged across those 5 real rates, the typical total (dividend-inclusive) crediting rate is about 6.20%. (Source: InsuranceGeek, updated July 2026.)

A real, safe alternative. As of August 2026, the best publicly advertised 5-year CD rate available to any saver — fully insured, contractually guaranteed for the full 5 years — is 4.50% APY. (Source: CNBC Select, August 2026.)

Modeling Assumptions

MA1 — Fictionalized company and buyer. This case fictionalizes the insurance company and every name because a real premium quote and a real cash-value schedule both require an actual, individualized, medically underwritten application. In their place: real, current, publicly published market-average rate data for a buyer matching Neil's real profile.

MA2 — Nearest published age bracket. Real rate benchmarks are published at 5–10 year intervals; no public source breaks out age 26 specifically. This case uses the nearest published bracket (age ~30).

MA3 — Linear scaling by face amount. The $500,000 rate benchmarks are scaled in proportion to D1's coverage amount, since both policy types are conventionally priced per $1,000 of face amount.

MA4 — Simplified whole life cash-value starting point. This case models the guaranteed cash value as starting, in year 1, at an amount equal to one year's premium, growing geometrically from there — a stated, disclosed simplification of real (insurer-specific, slower-starting) cash-value schedules.

MA5 — A single guaranteed growth rate. D3's projection uses 3% — the midpoint of the real published 2%–4% guaranteed range — since no single universal guaranteed rate exists across insurers.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 · Coverage Need (D1)

    From Appendix B: add the mortgage balance to Neil's income × the number of years until the youngest child turns 18. Round to the nearest $50,000.

    Years until youngest (age 1) turns 18 = 17. This coverage amount is what both D2 and D3 are priced against.

    Step 2 · Term Premium Total (D2)

    Scale Appendix D's $500,000 benchmark ($36/month) to your Step 1 coverage amount, find the annual premium, then multiply by a 20-year term.

    Scale factor = your coverage ÷ $500,000. 17 years of need rounds up to the nearest standard term (20 years).

    Step 3 · Whole Life Cash Value (D3)

    Write CVn = CVn-1 × 1.03 as a recursive rule, convert to the explicit form CVn = CV0 × 1.03n (CV0 = one year's whole life premium, MA4/MA5), then evaluate at age 65 (n=39) and age 80 (n=54).

    First compute your Step 1–scaled whole life annual premium (Appendix D's $444/month benchmark, scaled the same way as Step 2), then apply the explicit formula.

    Step 4 · The Rate Comparison (D4)

    Compare whole life's dividend-inclusive crediting rate (6.20%) to the best available 5-year CD rate (4.50%) from Appendix D. Find the gap in percentage points.

    This is the comparison Jake's two browser tabs are really showing him. Don't decide yet whether it settles the question — write out, in your D4 explanation, what this comparison assumes about how much money is actually growing at each rate.

    Twist

    Reveal the Twist

    One more thing. The 6.20% whole life rate you just compared to the CD isn't guaranteed. It's a dividend — declared year to year by the insurer, based on how well the company's investments and mortality experience actually performed. In a bad year, that dividend can be reduced, or not paid at all. The CD's 4.50% is not like that: it is contractually fixed, insured, and guaranteed for the full five years no matter what happens in the market.

    If you strip out the non-guaranteed dividend and compare whole life's guaranteed minimum rate (3%, the midpoint of the real published 2%–4% range) to the CD's 4.50% instead — does your Step 4 comparison still favor the same option?
