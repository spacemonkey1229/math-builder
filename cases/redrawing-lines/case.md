Case Study: Redrawing Lines
MS | Math | Case Study | 2026 | Adventure

## Overview

Margo Thibodeau has the whole state up on the wall of the commission's basement workroom in Augusta, and she has been staring at one county in the middle of it since before the coffee went cold. Outside, a maintenance crew is running a leaf blower down the State House walk, and the sound comes through the window in waves. The commission votes Monday.

Two plans are printed and taped up side by side, and from across the room they look almost identical — the same long shape running north, the same crowded south. The difference is a single line, and where it falls. On one sheet that line runs the whole way along county borders, and every county in Maine stays in one piece. On the other it turns inward and cuts through the middle of Kennebec County — the capital's own county — dividing towns that share a school district, a hospital, a newspaper.

Margo has the arithmetic for both. That is not the problem. The problem is that the arithmetic does not settle it: one plan is fairer counted in people, the other counted in communities, and the gap between them is small enough that either number could be called noise by somebody who wanted it to be.

Her supervisor leans in the doorway with a legal pad and asks which one is going in the report. Margo picks up a marker and looks at the two sheets.

## Background

Margo Thibodeau is a senior staff analyst to Maine's apportionment commission, the body that delivers a redistricting report and plan to the Legislature after each decennial census (SEE APPENDIX H, MA4). Staff do not vote. Staff write the report, and the report carries one recommended plan and the arithmetic that defends it. The plan Margo recommends is the plan the commission will argue about, and her name is on the memo.

Two hard constraints bind every plan, and neither is negotiable. **Contiguity** — each district must be one connected piece; a county cannot be assigned to a district it cannot reach through other counties of that same district. **Population equality** — for congressional districts the federal standard is equality "as nearly as practicable," and any surviving difference has to be justified in the record (SEE APPENDIX E). Maine was apportioned **2** seats in the U.S. House on an apportionment population of **1,363,582** [S1]; the population that district lines are actually drawn against is the resident count of **1,362,359** [S2], which makes an ideal district of **681,179.5** people [S6] (SEE APPENDIX A).

Five counties — Cumberland, Knox, Lincoln, Sagadahoc and York — sit wholly inside the southern district today and stay together in both plans on the table. Together they hold **627,584** people [S2], which leaves them **53,595.5** short of a full district (SEE APPENDICES A AND D, APPENDIX H MA3). Every plan has to make up that shortfall from somewhere, and there are only two kinds of somewhere.

**The Kennebec Split** — the southern district takes the twelve Kennebec County municipalities the enacted statute names and leaves Kennebec's other eighteen in the northern district [S5]. Population comes out as close to exactly even as Maine's arithmetic permits, and Kennebec County is divided between two members of Congress (SEE APPENDIX C).

**The Hancock Line** — the southern district takes all of Hancock County instead. No county in Maine is divided, and the district line runs along county borders for its entire length. The two districts do not come out even; one runs over the ideal and one runs under (SEE APPENDICES C AND D).

Margo scores plans on **county-line crossings**: the number of Maine county borders the district line runs along, plus a stated weight for each county the line runs *through* (SEE APPENDIX B for the border list, APPENDIX H MA1, MA2 and MA5 for the weight and the two counting conventions). Staff practice — not statute — keeps those five southern counties together in every shortlisted plan, to hold down the number of Mainers who change representatives, and picks the survivor on population rather than on crossings (SEE APPENDIX H, MA3). The commission's screening tool claims to have examined every whole-county plan there is; APPENDIX F is the count that claim rests on, and it is only as good as the proof behind it.

Every figure in this case is sourced in APPENDIX G or stated as an explicit assumption in APPENDIX H. The vocabulary and the "how to…" skills this case asks of you are listed in APPENDIX I — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

This case uses population and geography only. It uses no election results and no party registration data, and neither plan is a partisan position.

## Dilemma

You are Margo Thibodeau.
Do you:
A.) Recommend The Kennebec Split — near-exact population equality, bought by dividing one county between two members of Congress OR
B.) Recommend The Hancock Line — every county in Maine kept whole, bought by leaving the two districts unequal

## Appendix E — What the Courts Have Said About Population Equality

The standard for congressional districts is not a fixed percentage, and reasonable lawyers
disagree about where the line sits. Two Supreme Court decisions are usually cited against each
other, and this case does not pretend they point the same way:

**How both percentages below are computed, so that yours can be compared to them.** Each is a
**maximum deviation**: the largest district minus the smallest district, divided by the average
(ideal) district. It is not the distance from one district to the ideal, which for a two-district
state is exactly half as large. Karcher's 3,674 is 527,472 − 523,798, and dividing that by the
average New Jersey district is the 0.6984% below. Score your plans the same way or the comparison
means nothing — and say which of the two figures you are quoting every time you quote one.

- **Karcher v. Daggett (1983)** [S8] — the Court **struck down** a New Jersey congressional plan
  whose largest district (527,472) and smallest (523,798) differed by **3,674** people, a maximum
  deviation of **0.6984%** of the average district. The holding: "there are no de minimis
  population variations, which could practicably be avoided, but which nonetheless meet the
  standard of Art. I, § 2 without justification."
- **Tennant v. Jefferson County Commission (2012)** [S9] — the Court **upheld** a West Virginia
  congressional plan with a variance of **4,871** people, **0.79%**, on the ground that the state
  had legitimate reasons for it: "our cases leave little doubt that avoiding contests between
  incumbents and not splitting political subdivisions are valid, neutral state districting
  policies." West Virginia "had never in its history divided a county between two or more
  congressional districts."

Karcher says a small deviation still has to be justified. Tennant says keeping counties whole is
the kind of thing that can justify one. Which case governs a given plan is exactly the argument
Margo's memo has to win — and how far past 0.79% a court would still go is not settled by either
opinion.
