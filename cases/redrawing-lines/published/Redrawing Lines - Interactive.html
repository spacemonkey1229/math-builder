Overview

Margo Thibodeau has the whole state up on the wall of the commission’s basement workroom in Augusta, and she has been staring at one county in the middle of it since before the coffee went cold. Outside, a maintenance crew is running a leaf blower down the State House walk, and the sound comes through the window in waves. The commission votes Monday.

Two plans are printed and taped up side by side, and from across the room they look almost identical — the same long shape running north, the same crowded south. The difference is a single line, and where it falls. On one sheet that line runs the whole way along county borders, and every county in Maine stays in one piece. On the other it turns inward and cuts through the middle of Kennebec County — the capital’s own county — dividing towns that share a school district, a hospital, a newspaper.

Margo has the arithmetic for both. That is not the problem. The problem is that the arithmetic does not settle it: one plan is fairer counted in people, the other counted in communities, and the gap between them is small enough that either number could be called noise by somebody who wanted it to be.

Her supervisor leans in the doorway with a legal pad and asks which one is going in the report. Margo picks up a marker and looks at the two sheets.

Background

Margo Thibodeau is a senior staff analyst to Maine’s apportionment commission, the body that delivers a redistricting report and plan to the Legislature after each decennial census (see Appendix H, MA4). Staff do not vote. Staff write the report, and the report carries one recommended plan and the arithmetic that defends it. The plan Margo recommends is the plan the commission will argue about, and her name is on the memo.

Two hard constraints bind every plan, and neither is negotiable. Contiguity — each district must be one connected piece; a county cannot be assigned to a district it cannot reach through other counties of that same district. Population equality — for congressional districts the federal standard is equality “as nearly as practicable,” and any surviving difference has to be justified in the record (see Appendix E). Maine was apportioned 2 seats in the U.S. House on an apportionment population of 1,363,582 [S1]; the population that district lines are actually drawn against is the resident count of 1,362,359 [S2], which makes an ideal district of 681,179.5 people [S6] (see Appendix A).

Five counties — Cumberland, Knox, Lincoln, Sagadahoc and York — sit wholly inside the southern district today and stay together in both plans on the table. Together they hold 627,584 people [S2], which leaves them 53,595.5 short of a full district (see Appendices A and D, Appendix H MA3). Every plan has to make up that shortfall from somewhere, and there are only two kinds of somewhere.

The Kennebec Split — the southern district takes the twelve Kennebec County municipalities the enacted statute names and leaves Kennebec’s other eighteen in the northern district [S5]. Population comes out as close to exactly even as Maine’s arithmetic permits, and Kennebec County is divided between two members of Congress (see Appendix C).

The Hancock Line — the southern district takes all of Hancock County instead. No county in Maine is divided, and the district line runs along county borders for its entire length. The two districts do not come out even; one runs over the ideal and one runs under (see Appendices C and D).

Margo scores plans on county-line crossings: the number of Maine county borders the district line runs along, plus a stated weight for each county the line runs through (see Appendix B for the border list, Appendix H MA1, MA2 and MA5 for the weight and the two counting conventions). Staff practice — not statute — keeps those five southern counties together in every shortlisted plan, to hold down the number of Mainers who change representatives, and picks the survivor on population rather than on crossings (see Appendix H, MA3). The commission’s screening tool claims to have examined every whole-county plan there is; Appendix F is the count that claim rests on, and it is only as good as the proof behind it.

Every figure in this case is sourced in Appendix G or stated as an explicit assumption in Appendix H. The vocabulary and the “how to…” skills this case asks of you are listed in Appendix I — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

This case uses population and geography only. It uses no election results and no party registration data, and neither plan is a partisan position.

Challenges

  1. Research and find an online resource that answers one of the "how to…" questions from I. Post a link to the resource as your submission.
  2. Post 250+ "excellent" words pitching a firm stance on which order you'd choose and why. Your pitch must use the resource from Challenge 1 and include at least one original visual display of the data.
  3. A pitch that includes:
    1. D1. A map-space count: using the Fundamental Counting Principle, the total number of ways Maine’s counties could be assigned to two districts if nothing but the assignment itself mattered — followed by a size-breakdown table giving the number of assignments that put exactly j counties in the southern district, for every j, as a binomial coefficient. Compute at least four of those coefficients by hand, showing the factorial arithmetic, and show that your whole row adds up to the Fundamental Counting Principle total you started with.
    2. D2. A recursion sheet with three parts: (a) a recursive formula for the number of assignments when the state has k counties instead of Maine’s actual number, with its base case stated separately; (b) a recursive formula for the entries of the size-breakdown row in D1, written so that each entry is built from two entries of the previous row, with its base cases stated separately; and (c) the conversion of the formula in (a) into an explicit closed formula, checked against your D1 total.
    3. D3. An exhaustiveness certificate: a proof by mathematical induction that the size-breakdown row of D1 adds up to the D2(c) total for every k, not just for Maine’s number of counties. Write the base case and the inductive step as separately labeled parts, and name the exact line of the inductive step where the D2(b) recursion is used. This is the document that lets the commission write “we examined every whole-county plan” in the record.
    4. D4. A pigeonhole brief: three claims proved by the Pigeonhole Principle, with no plan enumerated and no map drawn — (i) that at least one of Maine’s two districts must hold at least as many people as the ideal, and that exact equality between the two districts is therefore impossible for Maine no matter which plan is chosen; (ii) that under any whole-county plan at least one district must contain at least half of Maine’s counties, rounded up; and (iii) that at least one district must contain at least two of Maine’s four largest counties, with the population consequence of that fact stated in people as the guaranteed minimum: Pigeonhole proves that some two of the four largest counties share a district, but it does not tell you which two, so the only population figure the principle actually guarantees is the smallest possible sum over any two of those four counties — not the largest, and not whichever pair looks most dramatic on the page. State that minimum pairing’s sum and show the arithmetic that makes it the floor, not just a pairing.
    5. D5. A scorecard for The Kennebec Split against The Hancock Line: county-line crossings for each plan with the crossed borders listed by name; each plan’s larger district, smaller district, and two deviation figures — the largest single district’s distance from the ideal, and the maximum deviation, meaning larger district minus smaller district — each in people and converted to a percent of the ideal; the number of Mainers living in a divided county under each plan; and your recommendation set against the court standard in Appendix E. Appendix E says which of your two figures its percentages are; say in one sentence why the other one is not comparable to them.
  4. An appendix showing evidence of your original thinking — pictures, screenshots, notes, work. Doesn't count toward your word count.
  5. Participate twice or more in the case study discussion. If absent, post 500 "excellent" words on the questions above instead.

Appendix E — What the Courts Have Said About Population Equality

The standard for congressional districts is not a fixed percentage, and reasonable lawyers disagree about where the line sits. Two Supreme Court decisions are usually cited against each other, and this case does not pretend they point the same way:

How both percentages below are computed, so that yours can be compared to them. Each is a maximum deviation: the largest district minus the smallest district, divided by the average (ideal) district. It is not the distance from one district to the ideal, which for a two-district state is exactly half as large. Karcher’s 3,674 is 527,472 − 523,798, and dividing that by the average New Jersey district is the 0.6984% below. Score your plans the same way or the comparison means nothing — and say which of the two figures you are quoting every time you quote one.

CaseDeviationPercentHolding
Karcher v. Daggett (1983) [S8]3,674 people (527,472 vs. 523,798)0.6984%Struck down — “there are no de minimis population variations, which could practicably be avoided, but which nonetheless meet the standard… without justification.”
Tennant v. Jefferson County Commission (2012) [S9]4,871 people0.79%Upheld — “avoiding contests between incumbents and not splitting political subdivisions are valid, neutral state districting policies.” West Virginia had never divided a county between congressional districts.

Karcher says a small deviation still has to be justified. Tennant says keeping counties whole is the kind of thing that can justify one. Which case governs a given plan is exactly the argument Margo’s memo has to win — and how far past 0.79% a court would still go is not settled by either opinion.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — D1: The map-space count

    Use the Fundamental Counting Principle on Maine's 16 counties (each county goes to one of two districts) to find the raw assignment total, then drop the two assignments that leave a district empty. Then compute one binomial coefficient from the size-breakdown row by hand: C(16, 2).

    Step 2 — D2: Recursion sheet

    Part (a): if f(k) is the number of ways to assign k counties to two districts, f(k) = 2·f(k−1) with base case f(0) = 1. Use that recursion to compute f(3). Part (b): Pascal's rule says C(n, j) = C(n−1, j−1) + C(n−1, j). Use it to build C(5, 2) from two entries of row 4.

    Step 3 — D3: Exhaustiveness certificate (spot-check)

    This calculator can't grade a written induction proof — that's your deliverable, with a labeled base case and inductive step. What it CAN check: the small-case pattern your induction is built on. For a state with just k = 4 counties, compute the full size-breakdown row's sum, and compare it to 2^4.

    Step 4 — D4: Pigeonhole brief

    Three guaranteed-minimum claims, no plan enumerated. (i) Since Maine's resident population is odd, some district must hold at least how many people? (ii) Splitting 16 counties into 2 districts forces at least how many counties into one of them? (iii) Among Maine's four largest counties, some two must share a district — find the population of the SMALLEST possible such pair (the only sum Pigeonhole actually guarantees) from Appendix A.

    Step 5 — D5: The scorecard

    County-line crossings for each plan (Appendix B borders-along + Appendix H MA1's severance weight for a divided county), each plan's maximum deviation in people and as a percent of the ideal, and how many Mainers sit in a divided county. If you've revealed and applied the Twist below, this checks against the Twist's substitute plan instead.

    This checker confirms your arithmetic. It does not choose a recommendation — that, and the one-sentence comparability note Appendix E asks for, is still yours to write.

    Reveal the Twist

    A commissioner moves from the floor to strike the staff shortlist rule (Appendix H MA3) and substitute Oxford County for Hancock as the whole-county add-on. Appendix D already has the population split for this substitute plan — you don't need a new source for it, just a recomputation.

    Recompute what you just argued, using the Working Notebook above: how many county-line crossings does the Oxford substitute run along, using the same Appendix B borders and Appendix H MA1 rule you already used for The Hancock Line? How does its maximum deviation, in people and as a percent of the ideal, compare to Appendix E's two benchmarks?

    You have sixty seconds. Does that change your recommendation, or only your reason for it?
