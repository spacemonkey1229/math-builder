Overview

On the left monitor: the configuration console for Foothill Outfitters' refund-handling agent, the automated system that decides — thousands of times a month — whether a customer's refund request gets approved on the spot or kicked to a human. The office is quiet except for the low whir of a laptop fan working too hard and a half-eaten protein bar wedged against the keyboard. Two configuration files sit open side by side, one already staged for deployment. Priya's cursor hovers over the console's "Publish" button, one config away from changing how every refund request at Foothill Outfitters gets decided starting tomorrow morning.

One gate. One config. One number to defend when Legal asks why.

Background

Priya Nandakumar is the AI Operations Lead at Foothill Outfitters, a mid-size online outdoor-apparel retailer. Priya owns the approval-gate logic inside the company's customer-service AI agent — software built on the same kind of agentic workflow platform used across the retail and enterprise-software industry (real, named systems in this category include Salesforce Agentforce, ServiceNow AI Agents, and Microsoft Copilot Studio's multistage approval flows) — that decides in real time whether an incoming refund request is auto-approved or escalated to a human reviewer (SEE APPENDIX A).

The agent's decision is built from three yes/no conditions checked on every refund request, defined precisely in (SEE APPENDIX A): whether the requested amount is under a set dollar threshold, whether the requesting customer's account tenure is over a set length of time, and whether the request is a repeat request from the same customer within a recent window. Compliance's original rule — never disputed, and not up for debate in this case — auto-approves a request only when all three safety conditions hold at once. The disagreement is entirely about how to correctly write down every other case: the two designs on the table encode that "everything else" condition differently (SEE APPENDIX B), and the two designs do not send the same requests to the same place.

Both designs must operate inside the same fixed monthly volume of refund requests the agent actually sees, broken down by which of the three conditions each request satisfies (SEE APPENDIX C). Every escalation costs the company a human reviewer's time; every request auto-approved that should not have been carries a real, documented risk of loss (SEE APPENDIX D). Priya has to pick one design, defend the total cost math behind it, and publish the config before the day is out.

The two options are named on Priya's whiteboard: The Compound Gate, which escalates a request whenever any one of the three safety conditions fails, or The Streamlined Gate — the VP's proposed rewrite — which escalates a request only when all three unsafe conditions hold at once. The vocabulary and "how to…" procedures needed to build and compare these designs are listed in (SEE APPENDIX E), and every sourced or assumed figure in this case is documented in (SEE APPENDIX F).

Appendices

Appendix A — The Approval Gate: Conditions and Thresholds

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D1, D2, D3). Every refund request the agent sees is checked against exactly three conditions.

ConditionDefinitionThreshold used in this case
pThe requested refund amount is under Foothill Outfitters' auto-approval dollar thresholdUnder $60 (MA2)
qThe requesting customer's account tenure is over Foothill Outfitters' trust thresholdOver 1 year (MA2)
rThis is a repeat refund request from the same customer within the lookback window2nd or more refund request within a trailing 90-day window (MA2)

What "trailing 90-day window" means: not a fixed calendar quarter. The window is measured backward from the date of today's request — the 90 days immediately before it — and moves forward one day at a time as new requests come in, the same way a "rolling 90-day average" would.

Compliance's rule, not in dispute: auto-approve a request only when p ∧ q ∧ ¬r — the amount is under threshold, the customer is trusted, and this is not a repeat request. What every other combination of p, q, and r should do — the "everything else" case — is exactly what Options A and B (Appendix B) disagree about.

Appendix B — Two Proposed Gate Logic Designs

Referenced in Background and Dilemma. Both designs share Appendix A's three conditions and Compliance's shared approve rule (p ∧ q ∧ ¬r). They differ only in how "everything else" is written down as an escalation rule.

DesignEscalation rule (Escalate when TRUE)Proposed by
Option A — The Compound Gate¬p ∨ ¬q ∨ rPriya's own design, applying De Morgan's Laws directly to the negation of Compliance's approve rule
Option B — The Streamlined Gate¬p ∧ ¬q ∧ rThe VP of Customer Operations, pitched as "the same policy, just cleaner"

Real enterprise agentic-workflow platforms structure exactly this kind of automated gate as a set of explicit, human-authored logical conditions rather than a black-box judgment call — for example, Microsoft's own Copilot Studio documentation for its multistage approval feature instructs builders to write approval logic as an explicit list of conditions joined by AND/OR, worded as "Reject … if ANY of the following conditions are true" [S1] — the same disjunction-of-failure-conditions structure Option A's escalation rule uses. Salesforce's own architecture guidance for agentic workflows likewise states that "agentic workflows operating at high density should incorporate explicit human approval or escalation gates for actions with irreversible consequences, such as financial transactions" [S3], consistent with Foothill Outfitters treating an automated refund decision as exactly this kind of gated action.

Appendix D — Cost Inputs

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D4).

ItemValue
Cost per escalated review (a human reviewer's time to manually decide one refund request)$9.00 (MA3)
Rate at which an undetected, incorrectly auto-approved risky refund request turns out to be fraudulent or otherwise a real loss15.14% [S2]
Average dollar value at risk per incorrectly auto-approved, risky refund request$66.00 (MA4)

What "expected false-approval loss cost" means here: not every risky refund request that gets auto-approved by mistake turns out to be a real loss — most are perfectly legitimate customers who simply didn't meet one threshold. Multiplying the return-fraud rate by the average dollar value at risk turns "how many risky requests got waved through" into a real dollar figure for the expected loss, the same way an insurer prices a policy without knowing which individual claim will occur.

Why an industry-wide rate is used here: [S2]'s 15.14% figure measures fraud across all retail merchandise returns generally, not specifically "refund requests a company's own AI agent auto-approved by mistake." No company publishes a fraud rate that narrow. This case applies the broader, real, sourced industry rate to Foothill Outfitters' own risky-request category as the best available proxy — a reasonable stand-in, not a perfect match, and a legitimate point for a Hero to challenge in discussion.
