Overview

“Ms. Buckley, Research sent over the final concept boards,” says her assistant Paul, sliding two thick folders across the desk. Rebecca Buckley sets down her coffee, still smelling faintly of the studio commissary, and glances at the wall of glossy one-sheets from her last run of green-lights — the ones that kept this office open. “Production wants an answer before tomorrow's two o'clock,” Paul adds. Rebecca's phone buzzes face-down on the desk before she can respond: a text from the head of Marketing. Numbers are in. Call me.

She flips open the first folder. A screenplay concept, a genre, a production budget circled twice in red pen. Behind it, a stapled packet of test-audience scores — a scatter of dots she hasn't had time to read yet. She flips to the second folder. Same shape, bigger numbers, a different circle of red ink.

Over a decade of picking winners has taught Rebecca one thing: the folder that feels bigger isn't always the one that pays off. She squares both folders side by side on the desk and reaches for a legal pad. Somewhere down the hall, the marketing team is still waiting for her to call back.

Background

Rebecca Buckley has spent over a decade as a studio production and acquisitions executive, with a reputation for picking scripts that turn into hits (see Appendix C for her prior slate). Production has narrowed the studio's next wide-release slot to two competing screenplay concepts and wants her final call before tomorrow's meeting.

The first option, Waves In The Tales, is the studio's smaller, cheaper concept. Before it reached Rebecca's desk, the studio's research team tested it with a phone survey of customers drawn from a nationwide subscriber list, using stratified random sampling — age group as the strata, then simple random selection within each stratum — a slower, more expensive-per-respondent, more methodologically rigorous process that reached a comparatively small number of people (see Appendix A).

The second option, The Flower's Ice, is the studio's biggest and most expensive concept on the table. Its concept was tested with a convenience sample gathered in person at movie theaters in six different states — fast, cheap to run against a large pool of theater-goers, but not drawn at random from the full population of potential ticket-buyers, and skewed toward people who were already inside a movie theater that day (see Appendix B).

Both surveys asked the same two questions: how likely, on a 1-10 scale, is this test-audience member to see the film in a theater, and how much, in whole dollars from $1 to $10, would they pay for a ticket. Rebecca's job is to turn those survey scores into a projected opening-weekend box office number for each film, and weigh that projection against what each film actually costs to make — not against what the other film costs.

Marketing has one more real constraint Rebecca has to work against: the studio's board judges every green-lit film against its own production budget, not against its rival's. A cheaper film that performs solidly can beat an expensive film that performs only slightly better. Real theatrical-market pricing and marketing-spend context that Rebecca's team relies on is in Appendix D. Whichever film she chooses, Rebecca also has to recommend how much to spend marketing it (see Appendix D) — and every projection in this case depends on one number nobody in the building actually knows for certain: how many people nationwide will realistically consider seeing a wide release in this genre on opening weekend (see Appendix E).

The vocabulary and the “how to…” skills this case will ask of you are listed in Appendix F — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

Appendix D — Market & Pricing Context (Learn More)

Real, live resources and figures as of 2026-08-20:

  • Average U.S. movie ticket price. The National Association of Theatre Owners (now Cinema United) reported an average U.S. ticket price of $10.53 in its 2022 annual data, up 15% from $9.16 in 2019 — the most recent figure the trade group had released at time of publication. [S1]
  • Marketing (P&A) budget guidance. For a wide theatrical release, prints-and-advertising (P&A) spend commonly equals or exceeds the film's production budget — [S2] documents named examples where P&A ran from roughly 4x production cost (a $15,000-budget film with $18M in marketing) down to about 1.1x–1.6x (multi-million-dollar productions), and states a general rule of thumb that a wide release needs to earn roughly twice its production budget just to cover production plus marketing/distribution before profit. This case's own 20%–30% marketing-budget-as-share-of-production guidance (used in Challenge D6) is a separate, author-stated planning estimate — not a figure taken directly from [S2] — chosen to keep the marketing-budget question tractable within the film's own production budget rather than requiring a full box-office breakeven model.
  • Cinema United — formerly NATO: the U.S. theater owners' trade association behind the ticket-price figure above.
  • Thoolie — P&A Explained: The Film Budget Bigger Than Production: a plain-language walkthrough of how marketing/distribution budgets are set relative to production cost.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Correlation & line of best fit (D1–D2)

    Plot each film's survey data yourself (paper, Sheets, Desmos — your call), then check your estimated slope, y-intercept, and correlation coefficient against this notebook's own calculation from the exact same Appendix A/B data.

    Tolerance is generous (±0.05 on slope/intercept, ±0.03 on r) — small differences from rounding or a slightly different regression method are fine.

    Step 2 — Definite buyers & mean price (D4)

    From your own Appendix A/B data, find the share of respondents who scored 9 or 10 on likelihood-to-see (the “definite buyers”), and their mean willingness-to-pay price.

    Step 3 — Projected opening-weekend revenue (D5)

    Use Appendix E's addressable-audience assumption (8,000,000) with your Step 2 numbers to project each film's opening-weekend revenue.

    Step 4 — Revenue per production dollar (D6)

    Divide each film's projected revenue by its own production budget. This notebook checks each film's own ratio — it will not tell you which one is bigger. That comparison, and your recommendation, is Challenge 3's D6 and Challenge 2's pitch.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    Marketing just forwarded something else. A national research group — Pew Research Center — studied exactly this problem: when you survey people who chose to be somewhere (like a convenience sample gathered inside a movie theater) instead of picking them at random, how much can you fix that bias afterward with statistics? Their answer, across 24 real benchmark tests: the best correction techniques recovered only about 30% of the bias. Roughly 70% of the gap between a convenience sample and a true random sample doesn't wash out, no matter how you weight the numbers afterward. [S3]

    You now have 60 seconds. If only 30% of The Flower's Ice's extra enthusiasm over Waves' definite-buyer rate is trustworthy signal — and the rest is just who happened to be standing in that theater lobby that day — does that change your position? Whose plan is most fragile to this?
