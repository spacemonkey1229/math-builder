Overview

Dr. Amara Kondwani has the budget spreadsheet open on one half of her laptop screen and the district immunity map on the other. Motorbike engines idle outside the district health office, and a field coordinator leans in the doorway holding two cooler boxes and a clipboard, waiting on her sign-off before the vials go on ice. The rains are coming — after that, half these roads flood out for the season, and the sampling window closes with them. On the map, one district glows in an unbroken block of color where no measles case has touched a school-age child in years. Nobody on her team actually knows whether that means the population there is protected, or just never checked. Her serosurvey is the only real evidence the vaccination campaign will have before doses get allocated district by district.

The two draft designs open in her other tab spend the same survey money completely differently. One pours the entire allotment into the villages her team is already staffed, permitted, and present in, buying the tightest possible read on exactly those communities. The other pulls back inside those same villages and reroutes what's left into territory the map has never covered at all. Her supervisor wants a number by end of day. The coordinator is still standing in the doorway, waiting.

She minimizes the spreadsheet, opens the tab where both budgets sit side by side, and starts reading.

Background

Dr. Amara Kondwani is the field lead for a Ministry of Health measles-and-rubella serosurvey ahead of a supplemental immunization campaign in a rural health district. Her job is to draw a cluster sample of children across a fixed set of already-established survey clusters, test each sample for immunity, and report a seroprevalence estimate precise enough to tell campaign planners which age cohorts, in which clusters, are under-immunized and need targeted follow-up doses. She reports to the campaign's national planning office and is accountable for spending the survey's entire allocated budget before results are due — there is no mechanism to request more money mid-survey.

The survey answers to one hard limit: a fixed total budget of $68,558 (SEE APPENDIX A). Every dollar spent testing participants or opening new clusters comes out of that one number, and nothing is added to it later. Running the existing cluster infrastructure — staff, cold-chain transport, lab processing capacity — costs a fixed amount before a single blood sample is ever drawn; each additional participant tested inside a cluster that is already open costs a real, published marginal amount on top of that fixed cost (SEE APPENDIX A); and each entirely new cluster opened in a currently unsurveyed district costs a separate, larger marginal amount (SEE APPENDIX C). All figures in this case are sourced from a real published costing study of a measles-and-rubella serosurvey (SEE APPENDIX E); the vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in APPENDIX F.

Kondwani's team has narrowed the plan to two named designs. Both fit inside the same $68,558 ceiling, and both start from the same 16 already-open clusters:

  • Full Precision tests as many participants as the entire budget can buy inside the existing clusters, maximizing statistical precision on the seroprevalence estimate for the district her team already has access to.
  • Reserve And Expand tests only the smaller sample size the source study itself certifies as statistically adequate for the same clusters (SEE APPENDIX A), and reroutes whatever budget that frees into opening brand-new clusters in a currently unsurveyed district instead.

Both plans spend the same total money. Neither is free of a trade-off: Full Precision buys the tightest possible read on the district already mapped; Reserve And Expand buys wider geographic reach at the cost of a looser read on that original district (SEE APPENDICES A, B AND C).

Precision itself is not a vague feeling here — it is a real, published, computable quantity. The worldwide standard for how many children a cluster survey must sample to hit a given precision traces back to a single classic paper (SEE APPENDIX D): for a 95% confidence interval no wider than a stated percentage, published survey methodology gives a direct formula linking sample size to half-width, using an assumed "design effect" that accounts for testing children in clusters rather than one at a time across the whole district. Applying that real formula to Kondwani's own real sample sizes is what finally lets Full Precision and Reserve And Expand be judged on the very same ruler — not dollars per cluster, but the actual precision, in percentage points, that each plan's data delivers wherever it collects data at all (SEE APPENDIX D).

Appendix D — The Real Precision-vs-Sample-Size Relationship

Statistical precision for a cluster survey is a published, formula-driven relationship between how many children get sampled and how tight the resulting 95% confidence interval is. The classic reference is Henderson RH, Sundaresan T, "Cluster sampling to assess immunization coverage: a review of experience with a simplified sampling method," Bulletin of the World Health Organization 60(2): 253–260 (1982) [S7] — the paper that established the classic "30 clusters × 7 participants" (n = 210) design used worldwide, built to hit a two-sided 95% confidence interval no wider than ±10 percentage points, assuming a design effect (deff) of 2.

A 2026 peer-reviewed review of that same methodology [S8] states the exact derivation: an unclustered effective sample size of 96 (at p = 0.5, the maximum-variance assumption used whenever the true rate is unknown) is inflated by the deff of 2 to an actual required sample size of 192 — then rounded up slightly to the familiar 30×7 = 210 design.

d = z · √(deff · p(1−p) / n)

where d is the 95% CI half-width (as a decimal), z = 1.96 (95% confidence), deff = 2, p = 0.5, and n is the real sample size actually tested. Applying this real formula to Kondwani's own real, Carcelen-et-al.-sourced sample sizes (Appendix A) is what produces the CI half-width figures in Appendices A and C — no invented conversion factor, only a real formula applied to real n's.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    The ±14.1% new-district figure depends entirely on a disclosed Modeling Assumption: that new clusters get tested at the same 12-participants/cluster density as the certified-adequate existing design (8 clusters × 12 = 96 total new-district participants). Reserve And Expand's whole argument for opening 8 new clusters instead of fewer, bigger ones assumed this density.

    What if the freed budget were instead spent on fewer new clusters, tested proportionally deeper, so the total new-district n stayed exactly 96? Use the slider below to pick a different number of new clusters (from 1 to 8) at the same total n = 96 spread across them, and watch what happens to the new district's CI half-width.

    Notice the CI half-width never changes as you move the slider — the formula only depends on total n, not on how it's split across clusters. But deff = 2 already prices in the extra variability from testing fewer, bigger clusters, so in reality splitting the same total n across fewer clusters would violate the same deff assumption the whole formula relies on. The real lever is total n, not cluster count — and total n is capped by the same $1,620/cluster marginal cost either way.
