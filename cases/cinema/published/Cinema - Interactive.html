Overview

The smell of butter and hot kernels drifts out past the box office as Emily Carlin threads through the opening-night crowd, nodding at a regular, catching a stray popcorn bucket before it hits the floor. “Another great day, good work,” she tells one of her concession kids, then slips into her office behind the counter and closes the door on the noise.

Her desk is a mess of payroll printouts, distributor paperwork, and a half-eaten container of takeout. She opens her laptop. The email from the distributor has been sitting there since this morning, subject line in bold: two major releases, same weekend. She already knows what it says — she's read it twice. An animated adventure everyone's kid has been begging to see, and the next chapter of a franchise everyone's been arguing about online for a year. Both open the same day. Both are going to be huge. Majesty Cinemas has exactly one theater big enough to make either of them feel like an event.

Emily opens a new tab. The website's showtime grid is blank, waiting. She has to publish it before she leaves tonight.

She pulls up her notes, cracks her knuckles, and stares at the two folders sitting side by side on her desk.

Background

Emily Carlin has worked at Majesty Cinemas, a privately owned movie theater, for nearly four years — the last year as its manager. As manager, she owns payroll, food ordering, distributor relationships, and the movie schedule; her mandate from the owners is simple: keep the cinema running smoothly, and keep it profitable. Majesty Cinemas' theater complex, hours, and seating are fixed and cannot be changed for this weekend (SEE APPENDIX C).

Emily's distributor has just confirmed two major releases opening the same weekend: an animated family adventure and the next chapter of a blockbuster action franchise, alongside four other films Majesty Cinemas is already contracted to keep showing (SEE APPENDIX A). Every one of the six films carries its own contract — a minimum number of daily showings, a per-showing cost to Majesty Cinemas, and, for the two majors, a maximum allowed gap between showtimes across the whole complex. Majesty Cinemas has only one theater large enough to make a movie feel like the weekend's headline act; the other seven are smaller. Whichever movie Emily hands that one big room to, the other has to make do with the smaller theaters alone.

Two competing plans are on Emily's desk for the opening weekend (Thursday through Sunday):

  • The Unicorn House — the large theater runs nothing but the animated family adventure all weekend; the action sequel is scheduled only in the smaller theaters.
  • The Revengers House — the large theater runs nothing but the action sequel all weekend; the animated adventure is scheduled only in the smaller theaters.

Every showing has a real cost regardless of turnout, and every showing fills the way Majesty Cinemas' own attendance-history data says it fills, by time of day (SEE APPENDIX B). Ticket price, attendance patterns, concession habits, and the physical time it takes to clear and reopen a theater between showings are all recorded there — this is what Emily actually has to weigh before the website goes live tonight. (Want more on how real theaters make these calls? SEE APPENDIX F for further reading.)

Appendix C — The Theater Complex

Majesty Cinemas has 8 theaters: one large theater seating 150, and seven smaller theaters seating 85 each. The earliest possible showtime start, in any theater, is 10:30 AM; every theater must be fully emptied and cleaned by 1:45 AM.

Appendix D — Modeling Assumptions

D-1. Five candidate showtimes per day, for revenue estimation. Appendix B's attendance data is organized into exactly five named windows per day. For D3–D5's revenue estimate, this case treats each window as one candidate showtime slot per day for a movie in a given theater — a simplification separate from the minute-by-minute physical schedule D1 asks you to actually build.

D-2. Matinee vs. standard pricing. A showing in a window that starts before 4:00 PM uses the $7.75 matinee price; a showing starting at or after 4:00 PM uses the $11.50 standard price.

D-3. Attendance capacity is the same for every movie. Appendix B's capacity-by-showtime-start table is not specific to any one film; this case uses it as the modeled fill rate for whichever movie occupies that slot, in either theater size.

D-4. Concession spending uses a representative dollar figure per group. This case uses the midpoint of each dollar-range group ($37.50, $21.00, $8.00) as that group's representative spend. The top group ("more than $40.00") has no stated upper bound, so this case assumes a representative value of $50.00 for that group — an estimate, not a given figure.

D-5. Showings run at the contractual minimum, not more. This case models each major release running exactly its required minimum number of daily showings in its assigned theater(s) — never fewer, never more.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 · One Slot's Ticket Revenue (D3 groundwork)

    Appendix B, Saturday, the 6:30–8:30 PM window: 97% capacity, standard ($11.50) pricing. Compute the ticket revenue for ONE showing of that slot in the large 150-seat theater. Revenue = seats × capacity% × price.

    Now try the same slot in an 85-seat theater — you'll need both sizes for D3.

    Step 2 · Expected Concession Revenue Per Ticket (D4)

    Using Appendix B's concession bands and Appendix D's assumption (midpoints, with the open-ended top band estimated at $50.00): compute the weighted-average (mean) concession spend per ticket holder. Adjust the top-band estimate slider to see how sensitive this number is.
    $50

    Weighted average: multiply each group's share by its representative spend, then add every group's contribution.

    Step 3 · One Movie's Full Weekend Total Revenue (D3–D4)

    Pick one movie and one theater size below, then compute that movie's full opening-weekend (Thu–Sun) TOTAL revenue (ticket + concessions), using Appendix D-1's five-slot model and D-5 (run only the contractual minimum daily showings, dropping the weakest slot(s) first if the minimum is under 5).

    This is one entry in your own D3/D4 table — you'll need all four movie×theater combinations to build the full comparison Emily needs.

    Step 4 · That Same Combination's Net Profit (D5)

    Using your Step 3 total revenue for the same movie/theater combination, subtract the weekend's total exhibition cost (per-showing cost × number of showings that weekend) to find net profit.

    This notebook checks one combination at a time on purpose — assembling all four into Emily's Option A vs. Option B recommendation is the pitch you're writing, not something this calculator will do for you.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    One more thing. A follow-up email just came in from the distributor. Turns out the contract for both major releases includes a “flagship placement” marketing co-op clause: whichever release gets the big 150-seat room satisfies that clause automatically. Whichever one gets confined to the smaller theaters instead — Majesty Cinemas owes the distributor a $50 co-op fee on every one of that movie's small-theater showings, all weekend.

    This fee lands on whichever movie is confined to small theaters — it isn't built to favor one plan over the other. But The Revengers needs 5 daily showings against Lizzie's 4, so it's exposed to more fee-bearing showings, all weekend, if it's the one confined.
