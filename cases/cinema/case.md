## Background

**Emily Carlin** has worked at **Majesty Cinemas**, a privately owned movie theater, for nearly four years — the last year as its manager. As manager, she owns payroll, food ordering, distributor relationships, and the movie schedule; her mandate from the owners is simple: keep the cinema running smoothly, and keep it profitable. Majesty Cinemas' theater complex, hours, and seating are fixed and cannot be changed for this weekend (**SEE APPENDIX C**).

Emily's distributor has just confirmed two major releases opening the same weekend: an animated family adventure and the next chapter of a blockbuster action franchise, alongside four other films Majesty Cinemas is already contracted to keep showing (**SEE APPENDIX A**). Every one of the six films carries its own contract — a minimum number of daily showings, a per-showing cost to Majesty Cinemas, and, for the two majors, a maximum allowed gap between showtimes across the whole complex. Majesty Cinemas has only one theater large enough to make a movie feel like the weekend's headline act; the other seven are smaller. Whichever movie Emily hands that one big room to, the other has to make do with the smaller theaters alone.

Two competing plans are on Emily's desk for the opening weekend (Thursday through Sunday):

- **The Unicorn House** — the large theater runs nothing but the animated family adventure all weekend; the action sequel is scheduled only in the smaller theaters.
- **The Revengers House** — the large theater runs nothing but the action sequel all weekend; the animated adventure is scheduled only in the smaller theaters.

Every showing has a real cost regardless of turnout, and every showing fills the way Majesty Cinemas' own attendance-history data says it fills, by time of day (**SEE APPENDIX B**). Ticket price, attendance patterns, concession habits, and the physical time it takes to clear and reopen a theater between showings are all recorded there — this is what Emily actually has to weigh before the website goes live tonight. (Want to see how real theater chains make these same kinds of calls? **SEE APPENDIX F** for further reading.)

The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in **Appendix G** — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

## Appendix D — Modeling Assumptions

**D-1. Five candidate showtimes per day, for revenue estimation.** Appendix B's attendance data is organized into exactly five named windows per day. For D3–D5's revenue estimate, this case treats each window as one candidate showtime slot per day for a movie in a given theater — a simplification made to keep the financial comparison tractable, separate from the minute-by-minute physical schedule D1 asks you to actually build (which has to account for cleaning time, the pre-open buffer, and whether a movie's required showings can really all fit in one theater — they may not always, which is exactly the kind of thing D1 is for).

**D-2. Matinee vs. standard pricing.** A showing in a window that starts before 4:00 PM uses the $7.75 matinee price; a showing starting at or after 4:00 PM uses the $11.50 standard price — Appendix B's own stated cutoff.

**D-3. Attendance capacity is the same for every movie.** Appendix B's capacity-by-showtime-start table is not specific to any one film; this case uses it as the modeled fill rate for whichever movie occupies that slot, in either theater size.

**D-4. Concession spending uses a representative dollar figure per group.** Four of Appendix B's five concession groups are given as a dollar range; this case uses the midpoint of each range as that group's representative spend ($37.50, $21.00, $8.00). The top group ("more than $40.00") has no stated upper bound, so this case assumes a representative value of **$50.00** for that group — an estimate, not a given figure, made because the source data leaves that group open-ended.

**D-5. Showings run at the contractual minimum, not more.** This case models each major release running exactly its required minimum number of daily showings (Appendix A) in its assigned theater(s) — never fewer (that would violate the distribution contract) and never more (Majesty Cinemas' data doesn't give any reason, film-by-film, to expect a showing beyond the minimum to outsell the pattern in Appendix B).

Curious how real movie theaters make the kinds of calls Emily is facing? These are optional, real-world resources — none of them are required for the Challenges, and none of them change any number in Appendices A–D.

## Balance Argument (facilitator reference — do not share with students before discussion)

**Argument for A (The Unicorn House):** Lizzie And The Last Unicorn only needs 4 daily showings against The Revengers' 5 — meaning Lizzie never has to run the single worst-attendance slot of the day (the 10:30 AM opening slot, every day of the weekend), while The Revengers, needing 5, is contractually forced to run that weak slot every single day, everywhere it plays. Lizzie is also cheaper to be *wrong* about: at $325 a showing it costs more per showing than Revengers' $220, but it runs one fewer showing a day, every day, all weekend.

**Argument for B (The Revengers House):** The Revengers costs Majesty Cinemas $220 per showing versus Lizzie's $325 — a real gap, on every single showing, all weekend, in whichever theater it's in. The large theater's 150 seats multiply whatever a showing earns; putting the cheaper-to-run film there, at scale, compounds that cost advantage across every day of the weekend instead of just some of it.

**The margin:** the two plans land close enough on projected weekend net profit that this is a genuinely defensible call either way — see FACILITATOR.md for the worked number and the sensitivity check behind it. Neither argument above is a knockout on its own; both are real, quantifiable positions from Appendix A and B alone.

**The trap:** a Hero who only compares each film's *review score* (Appendix A) or Revengers' longer runtime concludes the flashier, longer, higher-rated movie obviously "deserves" the big room — without ever running the actual cost-per-showing and slot-count arithmetic that Appendix B's attendance-capacity data forces. Review score never enters Majesty Cinemas' own attendance data at all (Modeling Assumption D-3) — it's real, printed, and irrelevant to this decision, which is exactly why it's tempting to lean on. D1/D2's showing-count discovery (Revengers structurally can't fit all 5 of its required daily showings inside one theater, of either size) is the second half of the trap: a Hero who doesn't check the turnaround math in Appendix B will build an impossible schedule and not know it.
