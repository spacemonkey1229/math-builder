Overview

The Boise conference room smells like burnt coffee and dry-erase marker. Commissioner Dana Whitfield spreads two printouts across the table, smoothing each one flat with the side of her hand — two Idahos, drawn in three colors instead of two.

It's 11:40 p.m. The filing deadline for the Commission for Reapportionment's public draft is midnight tomorrow. Her phone, face-down by her elbow, has buzzed four times in the last twenty minutes — a reporter, twice, and two of the five other commissioners, wanting to know which map she's bringing to the 9:00 a.m. vote.

On the left printout, the Treasure Valley sits whole — one solid district, its border tracing the outer edge of Ada and Canyon counties like a single held breath. On the right, that same valley is cut clean through, its population divided between two of the state's three new districts, the line running wherever the population numbers said it should.

Both maps satisfy the letter of the law, or so the commission's outside counsel keeps telling her. Both maps, her gut tells her, will get accused of gerrymandering the moment either one becomes public — just from opposite directions.

She picks up her red pen. She doesn't uncap it yet.

Whichever line she recommends at tomorrow morning's vote, six commissioners — three Republicans, three Democrats, evenly split by law — will have to look at the same two maps.

Background

Idaho currently elects two members to the U.S. House of Representatives from two congressional districts. Following the 2020 U.S. Census and the population growth confirmed in subsequent Census Bureau estimates, Idaho is on track to gain a third congressional seat — the state's first change in district count since the 1990s. Idaho law requires the Secretary of State to convene a bipartisan, six-member Commission for Reapportionment (three commissioners appointed by Republican officeholders, three by Democratic officeholders) whenever a new seat must be drawn (see Appendix A). That commission has now been instructed to redraw Idaho's map from two districts into three.

The commission does not have a blank slate. The U.S. Constitution's Equal Protection Clause and decades of Idaho and federal case law require every congressional district in a state to be drawn as close to exactly equal in population as is practically achievable — a far stricter standard than applies to state legislative districts (see Appendix C). Beyond population equality, Idaho's own redistricting statute and the commission's operating rules bind the map to several additional, simultaneous criteria: each district must be reasonably compact and contiguous; counties should remain whole and undivided wherever population math allows it; and each district must be broadly representative of the state's overall population, race, age composition, and political-party balance — no district may be engineered to concentrate (“pack”) or dilute (“crack”) a particular group's voting strength relative to the others (see Appendix A).

Those criteria pull against each other the moment a third district enters the picture. Ada and Canyon counties together — the Treasure Valley, anchored by Boise — hold roughly 40% of Idaho's entire population, a share that has only grown since 2020, and Ada County alone outweighs all ten counties of the Idaho Panhandle combined (see Appendix B). Divided three ways, Idaho's population does not split cleanly along its existing county lines: however the commission draws District 3, the fast-growing Treasure Valley has to either anchor a district by itself or be split across more than one.

Redistricting lawyers have blunt names for the ways a map can go wrong. Packing crams a disfavored group into as few districts as possible, so it wins those seats by a landslide but has no voice anywhere else. Cracking does the opposite — it spreads that same group thin across several districts so it never adds up to a real majority in any of them. Courts and researchers also track wasted votes: every ballot that didn't actually change who won, whether it was cast for the loser or piled on top of a landslide winner. And when a single county's population gets divided between two or more districts instead of staying in one piece, that's a county split — something Idaho's own statute says should be avoided whenever the population math allows it (see Appendix A).

Two complete draft maps have reached the commission's floor for tomorrow's vote:

The Whole Valley Plan

Draws District 3 as a single, compact, intact Ada + Canyon county district — the Treasure Valley kept entirely together, with Districts 1 and 2 covering the rest of the state's 42 counties between them, unchanged from how they're assigned under either plan (see Appendix F).

The Balanced Split Plan

Divides the combined population of Ada and Canyon counties across two of the three districts, pairing part of the Treasure Valley with the northern counties and part with the southern and eastern counties, so each of Idaho's three districts carries a mix of urban and rural population (see Appendix F).

Appendices

Appendix A — Gerrymandering, Idaho's Commission, and the Legal Standard
  • Gerrymandering, packing, and cracking, defined: “Gerrymandering is the intentional drawing of a district map or plan to advantage or disadvantage a specific group.” Cracking splits a group of voters across multiple districts so their voting strength is diluted everywhere. Packing concentrates a group into as few districts as possible, so it wins those districts by overwhelming margins but has no influence anywhere else. [S1]
  • Idaho's Commission for Reapportionment was created by a 1994 constitutional amendment (Idaho Const. art. III, §2; Idaho Code §72-1501). It has six members: the President Pro Tempore and Speaker of the Legislature's majority party each appoint one, the House and Senate minority leaders each appoint one, and the chairs of the state Republican and Democratic parties each appoint one — six seats, split three and three by design. [S2]
  • Idaho's stated redistricting criteria (Idaho Code §72-1506): districts must be substantially equal in population; the plan should avoid oddly-shaped (“uncompact”) districts to the maximum extent possible; county divisions should be avoided, and minimized when unavoidable, and counties may never be divided to protect a political party or an incumbent; districts must preserve traditional neighborhoods and communities of interest to the maximum extent possible; and districts must be contiguous. [S3]
  • A stricter population-equality standard for Congress specifically: the U.S. Supreme Court has held that congressional districts must be made “as nearly of equal population as is practicable,” and struck down a congressional map with a population deviation of well under 1% for failing to justify even that small a gap with a legitimate state objective. Karcher v. Daggett, 462 U.S. 725 (1983). [S4]

Note on this case's county regions. For tractability, this case treats Idaho's 44 counties as three population blocks: the Treasure Valley (Ada + Canyon), a North & West-Central region, and a South-Central & East region. This is a case-construction choice, not a claim about how Idaho's actual future 3-district map will be drawn — the population, land-area, and county-list figures for each region are all real, sourced 2020 Census figures.

Appendix C — Redistricting Legal Standards

Idaho's own 2021 Commission for Reapportionment Final Report states the population-equality standard this way, discussing the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause:

“A person's right to vote… is unconstitutionally impaired when the weight of that person's vote is ‘in a substantial fashion diluted’ compared to the votes of citizens in other parts of the state… A state, in creating districts, may ‘legitimately desire to maintain the integrity of various political subdivisions’ and take into account other valid considerations, such as ‘natural or historical boundary lines.’ … [But] maintaining county or other political subdivision boundaries ‘can justify small deviations, it cannot be allowed to negate the fundamental principle of one person, one vote.’” [S16]

The commission's own operating rule for how much deviation is allowed: in practice, Idaho's 2021 Commission agreed that “no district should deviate more than five percent, either over or under, from the ideal district size, unless there was a compelling reason for such deviation.” [S16] That standard was applied to legislative districts in 2021 — the congressional rule is even tighter.

Congressional districts face a stricter standard. The U.S. Supreme Court has held congressional districts must be made “as nearly of equal population as is practicable,” and in Karcher v. Daggett, 462 U.S. 725 (1983), struck down a New Jersey congressional map with an overall population deviation of well under one percent because the state could not prove even that small a deviation served a legitimate objective. No amount of congressional population deviation is automatically acceptable. [S4]

Appendix E — How to Calculate an Efficiency Gap

The efficiency gap is a political-science measure of how evenly a district map turns votes into seats, developed by Nicholas Stephanopoulos and Eric McGhee and used by courts and researchers to evaluate partisan fairness. [S11]

The method, step by step:

  1. For each district, find each side's “wasted votes.” A losing vote is any vote cast for a candidate who did not win that district. A surplus vote is a vote for the winning candidate beyond the 50%-plus-one needed to win.
  2. Add up all of Side A's wasted votes across every district, and all of Side B's wasted votes across every district.
  3. Efficiency gap = (Side B's total wasted votes − Side A's total wasted votes) ÷ total votes cast statewide.

A result near zero means both sides waste votes at roughly the same rate — a “fair” map by this measure. A large positive or negative result means one side's votes are being wasted at a substantially higher rate than the other's. [S11]

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Population balance (D1)

    Enter your chosen plan's three district populations. The calculator finds the statewide ideal (mean) district population and each district's percent deviation from it — your job is to interpret which district deviates most and whether that's within Appendix C's stated tolerances.

    Step 2 — Compactness (D2)

    Enter a shape's land area (from Appendix F) and your own measured perimeter (from the map's mile scale bar). The calculator computes the area of a circle with that same perimeter, then the Polsby-Popper score (land area ÷ circle area). A score near 1.0 is highly compact; the closer to 0, the more elongated/irregular.

    Step 3 — Efficiency gap (D3)

    Enter each district's total votes and the winning candidate's votes (Appendix D) to find that district's wasted votes for each side. Do this once for the current two-district baseline, and again — using your own apportioned totals — for your chosen plan's three districts.

    Repeat this for every district in your baseline and your chosen plan, then combine: efficiency gap = (Side B total wasted − Side A total wasted) ÷ total votes cast statewide (Appendix E).

    Step 4 — Demographic check (D4)

    Enter two regions' populations and their % under 18 (Appendix B) to find the population-weighted % under 18 for their combination — then compare it to Idaho's statewide 23.4%.

    Reveal the Twist

    Whichever plan you picked, go back and check your D3 efficiency-gap number against a neighbor who picked the other plan. Compare them side by side. What do you notice?

    Here's the thing Modeling Assumption MA2 sets up, whether or not you spotted it while computing D3: because every region's two-party vote share is assumed identical (the uniform statewide split), the projected efficiency gap doesn't actually depend on which population ends up in which district — only on vote share, which MA2 already fixed as the same everywhere. If your arithmetic is right, your D3 number should come out the same no matter which plan you ran it on.

    If that's true, what does it mean for your recommendation? The metric Appendix E asked you to compute turns out not to distinguish the two plans at all under this case's disclosed assumption — so whichever plan you're arguing for has to stand on D1 (population balance) and D2 (compactness), not on D3. Neither plan wins on every axis. That's the real dilemma.
