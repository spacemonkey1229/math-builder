Overview

The comic strip on Dr. Okafor's door always gets a laugh out of Deondra Lamont on her walk past — today's is a doctor holding a chart, patient in a paper gown, some punchline about waiting rooms. Lamont, CEO of Benedictus Hospital Group, usually keeps walking. This time she stops.

Something about it doesn't sit right. She thinks about it through two meetings and a lunch she doesn't finish, and by mid-afternoon she's asked her assistant for every patient complaint on file about how the group's physicians treat overweight patients. The number that comes back is bigger than she expected.

She picks up the phone and dials the hospital's biostatistician.

"Ingrid, it's Deondra. I need you to look into something for me." A pause. "Do our physicians treat overweight and obese patients differently than everyone else? I don't want a guess. I want the numbers — and I want to know what, if anything, we should do about it."

Ingrid Robertson has run three years of surveys and studies for this hospital group — lab staffing models, admission-length analyses, the kind of work nobody outside her office reads closely. This one is different. She already knows what data exists to answer it, and she already knows the CEO will want a firm recommendation, not a shrug.

She opens a blank document and starts typing.

Background

Benedictus Hospital Group operates 5 large hospitals and more than 30 smaller clinics across the Dallas–Fort Worth metro area, employing 1,852 physicians — 1,228 of them primary care physicians (Appendix C). The group has averaged a thin 1.4% profit margin over the last ten years; its most recent six years of profit are on record (Appendix C).

Patient complaints specifically alleging that a physician treated them differently because of their weight have been climbing: 15 of 435 total complaints last quarter, and 82 of 1,458 total complaints in the current year (Appendix C). Lamont wants to know whether that trend reflects a real pattern in how physicians allocate their time and attention — and, if it does, whether it is large enough to justify spending real money to fix it.

The one tool available to answer the first half of that question is a real, published research study Ingrid has on file: a controlled experiment in which 122 primary care physicians affiliated with the hospital group each reviewed one of six versions of an identical patient medical chart — a patient presenting with an uncomplicated migraine headache, otherwise healthy — with only the patient's weight (given as average, overweight, or obese, by Body Mass Index), gender, and age varied across versions. Each physician was asked how much time they expected to spend with that patient during the visit. 109 of the 122 physicians' time estimates were recorded (Appendix B); the rest of the study's design and its methodology are documented in Appendix A.

If Ingrid recommends it, the hospital group's leadership has a specific fix ready to deploy: an outside firm's bias-awareness training program, at a fixed cost of $265 per physician trained, covering the physical and social-bias tools the training firm says reduce differential treatment over time (Appendix C). Nobody has committed to who receives the training or what it would cost in total — that arithmetic, and the modeling choices it depends on, are recorded openly (Appendix D) rather than decided quietly.

Real-world context for why this question matters at all — beyond Benedictus's own numbers — sits in Appendix E; every externally sourced claim in this case is documented with its source in Appendix F.

The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in Appendix G — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

Appendices

Appendix A — The Study

This case is presented as fiction: Benedictus Hospital Group, Deondra Lamont, and Ingrid Robertson are invented, and the hospital's own patient-complaint and physician-count figures (Appendix C) are this case's own fictional-but-fixed scenario data.

Appendix B's dataset is different — it reproduces a real, published academic study. Hebl, M., & Xu, J. (2001), "Weighing the care: physicians' reactions to the size of a patient," International Journal of Obesity, 25, 1246–1252, ran this experiment: physicians at hospitals in the Texas Medical Center of Houston (not Dallas — this case relocates the setting) were mailed a mock medical chart depicting a migraine-headache patient. The chart's patient weight was manipulated across three BMI levels — average (23), overweight (30), obese (36) — alongside gender and age, giving six chart versions. 122 physicians were randomly assigned one version each. A public teaching reproduction of part of the same study independently confirms the 122-physician count and Texas Medical Center Houston design, but reproduces only a simplified 2-condition slice for classroom use, noting the full study is larger — consistent with this case's fuller 3-condition dataset. The exact match of every individual value to the original 2001 journal article could not be independently re-verified, since the full article sits behind a publisher paywall (see Appendix F, S1/S2).

Real-world context, current as of this case's 2026 write-up: recent CDC data puts U.S. adult obesity prevalence at 40.3% and overweight-including-obesity at 72.4% (2021–2023 survey cycle) — real numbers, reported for context only; neither is load-bearing to any calculation in this case (Appendix F, S3).

Appendix D — Modeling Assumptions

These figures are not sourced — they are stated scenario choices made explicit so a Hero can challenge them in discussion.

MA1 — which physicians the training covers: this case reads "provide training to its physicians" as all 1,852 physicians, not only the 1,228 primary care physicians the Appendix A study surveyed. A narrower reading is equally defensible and changes the total dollar cost substantially.

MA2 — how many profit years count as "typical": this case averages all six given years equally. Weighting only the most recent years is an equally defensible reading and produces a different denominator.

MA3 — how to treat the 13 missing responses: this case treats the 109 recorded responses as missing-at-random and analyzes them as the full working sample. Whether this missingness is random is an open question the data itself cannot settle — a Hero who raises it is reasoning correctly, not making an error.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 · Group Means (D1/D4 groundwork)

    From Appendix B, compute the mean time spent with the average-weight group (n=33), and the mean time spent with the overweight and obese groups pooled together (n=76). Round each to 1 decimal place.

    Use the Download CSV button on Appendix B to pull all 109 rows into a spreadsheet if you'd rather compute there.

    Step 2 · The Percentage Gap

    Using your Step 1 means, find the percent difference: (average mean − heavier mean) ÷ average mean × 100. Round to 1 decimal place.

    This is one of the two numbers Ingrid weighs side by side (D3/D4's foundation). Step 4 builds the other one.

    Step 3 · Percent-Above-Median (D3)

    From Appendix B: what percentage of average-weight patients (n=33) had a time strictly greater than the overweight group's median (22.5 min)? And strictly greater than the obese group's median (20 min)? Round to 1 decimal place.

    Look closely at your two answers once you've checked them — is that a coincidence, or does it tell you something about the data?

    Step 4 · Explore the Cost Side (Appendix D, MA1/MA2)

    There's no single "correct" answer here — MA1 and MA2 are open assumptions. Drag the sliders to see how the training's total cost, as a percent of a typical year's profit, changes with each choice. Compare it to your Step 2 answer.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    122 physicians were mailed a chart. Only 109 answers are on record — 13 missing, about 1 in 9. Here's the breakdown by which chart they got: if the 122 physicians split evenly three ways across weight condition, you'd expect about 41 responses per group. The overweight and obese groups came in close to that (38 each — short about 3 apiece). The average-weight group came in at 33 — short about 8, roughly double either other group's shortfall, and more than half of everything missing across the whole study.

    The physicians who got a completely unremarkable, average-weight chart were the ones most likely to never send back a time estimate at all.

    GroupExpected (of 122÷3)Actual recordedShortfall

    This doesn't hand you an answer — it hands you a harder question. If "boring chart, skip the survey" explains the missing average-weight responses, does that make your Step 2 gap too big, too small, or is there no way to tell from this data alone? Write out your reasoning; that's the point.
