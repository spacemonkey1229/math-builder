## Overview

The comic strip on Dr. Okafor's door always gets a laugh out of Deondra Lamont on her walk past — today's is a doctor holding a chart, patient in a paper gown, some punchline about waiting rooms. Lamont, CEO of Benedictus Hospital Group, usually keeps walking. This time she stops.

Something about it doesn't sit right. She thinks about it through two meetings and a lunch she doesn't finish, and by mid-afternoon she's asked her assistant for every patient complaint on file about how the group's physicians treat overweight patients. The number that comes back is bigger than she expected.

She picks up the phone and dials the hospital's biostatistician.

"Ingrid, it's Deondra. I need you to look into something for me." A pause. "Do our physicians treat overweight and obese patients differently than everyone else? I don't want a guess. I want the numbers — and I want to know what, if anything, we should do about it."

Ingrid Robertson has run three years of surveys and studies for this hospital group — lab staffing models, admission-length analyses, the kind of work nobody outside her office reads closely. This one is different. She already knows what data exists to answer it, and she already knows the CEO will want a firm recommendation, not a shrug.

She opens a blank document and starts typing.

## Background

Benedictus Hospital Group operates 5 large hospitals and more than 30 smaller clinics across the Dallas–Fort Worth metro area, employing 1,852 physicians — 1,228 of them primary care physicians (**SEE APPENDIX C**). The group has averaged a thin 1.4% profit margin over the last ten years; its most recent six years of profit are on record (**SEE APPENDIX C**).

Patient complaints specifically alleging that a physician treated them differently because of their weight have been climbing: 15 of 435 total complaints last quarter, and 82 of 1,458 total complaints in the current year (**SEE APPENDIX C**). Lamont wants to know whether that trend reflects a real pattern in how physicians allocate their time and attention — and, if it does, whether it is large enough to justify spending real money to fix it.

The one tool available to answer the first half of that question is a real, published research study Ingrid has on file: a controlled experiment in which 122 primary care physicians affiliated with the hospital group each reviewed one of six versions of an identical patient medical chart — a patient presenting with an uncomplicated migraine headache, otherwise healthy — with only the patient's weight (given as **average**, **overweight**, or **obese**, by Body Mass Index), gender, and age varied across versions. Each physician was asked how much time they expected to spend with that patient during the visit. 109 of the 122 physicians' time estimates were recorded (**SEE APPENDIX B**); the rest of the study's design and its methodology are documented in **APPENDIX A**.

If Ingrid recommends it, the hospital group's leadership has a specific fix ready to deploy: an outside firm's bias-awareness training program, at a fixed cost of $265 per physician trained, covering the physical and social-bias tools the training firm says reduce differential treatment over time (**SEE APPENDIX C**). Nobody has committed to who receives the training or what it would cost in total — that arithmetic, and the modeling choices it depends on, are recorded openly (**SEE APPENDIX D**) rather than decided quietly.

Real-world context for why this question matters at all — beyond Benedictus's own numbers — sits in **Appendix E**; every externally sourced claim in this case, including the real study behind Appendix B, is documented with its source in **Appendix F**. Curious readers who want to go beyond the case itself and read more about the real study and real-world weight bias in healthcare should **SEE APPENDIX G**.

The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in **Appendix H** — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

## Appendix A — The Study

This case is presented as fiction: Benedictus Hospital Group, Deondra Lamont, and Ingrid Robertson are invented, and the hospital's own patient-complaint and physician-count figures (Appendix C) are this case's own fictional-but-fixed scenario data — the same way an invented company's price list or headcount doesn't need an outside citation.

**The time-spent dataset in Appendix B is different — it reproduces a real, published academic study.** Hebl, M., & Xu, J. (2001), "Weighing the care: physicians' reactions to the size of a patient," *International Journal of Obesity*, 25, 1246–1252 (**SEE APPENDIX F, S1**), ran exactly this experiment: physicians at hospitals in the Texas Medical Center of Houston (not Dallas — this case relocates the setting) were mailed a packet containing a mock medical chart depicting a patient with an uncomplicated migraine headache. The chart's patient weight was manipulated across three levels by Body Mass Index — average (BMI = 23), overweight (BMI = 30), or obese (BMI = 36) — alongside patient gender and age, giving six chart versions. 122 physicians were randomly assigned one version each and asked to estimate how much time they expected to spend with that patient. A public teaching reproduction of part of this same study (**SEE APPENDIX F, S2**) independently confirms the 122-physician count, the Texas Medical Center Houston hospitals, and the migraine-chart design — but only reproduces a simplified 2-condition (average/overweight) slice of the full study for classroom use, noting explicitly that "the method and data described here are only a small part of a larger study." This case's Appendix B dataset is the fuller, 3-condition version consistent with the original study's actual design; the exact match of every individual value to the original 2001 journal article could not be independently re-verified against that article's own published tables, since the full article sits behind a publisher paywall this case-writing pass could not access — flagged honestly rather than presented as byte-verified (see `case.json.sources` confidence grades).

Real-world context, current as of this case's 2026 write-up: recent CDC data (**SEE APPENDIX F, S3**) puts U.S. adult obesity prevalence at 40.3% and overweight-including-obesity at 72.4% (2021–2023 survey cycle) — both real numbers, reported here for context only; neither is load-bearing to any calculation in this case.

## Appendix D — Modeling Assumptions

> These figures are **not sourced** — they are stated scenario choices made explicit here so a Hero can challenge them in discussion. That is a legitimate move.

| # | Assumption | Value used | Basis | Sensitivity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MA1 | Which physicians the training program would cover, if recommended | All 1,852 physicians | The hospital group's own language ("provide training to its physicians") does not restrict the program to the 1,228 primary care physicians the Appendix A study actually surveyed — patient-weight-related interactions are not limited to primary care encounters. A narrower reading (primary care physicians only) is equally defensible and changes the total dollar cost substantially. | The answer is sensitive to this choice — a narrower training cohort changes the total cost meaningfully. |
| MA2 | How many years of the profit history (Appendix C) to average when judging whether the training's cost is "affordable" relative to a normal year | Most recent 6 years, equally weighted | The hospital group provided six years of profit data with no guidance on whether older or more recent years should carry more weight in judging a "typical" year; profit nearly doubled from 2015 to 2016 and fell back from 2017 to 2018, so the choice of window matters. | The answer is sensitive to this choice — weighting only the most recent years produces a different "typical profit" figure than averaging all six. |
| MA3 | How to treat the 13 of 122 physicians (Appendix B) whose time estimate was never recorded | Treated as missing at random; the 109 recorded responses are analyzed as the full working sample | The case does not know, and the original study's public materials do not say, why those 13 responses are missing. If physicians who found the "average weight" chart unremarkable were less likely to bother finishing the survey than physicians who reacted to an overweight or obese chart, the missing responses would not be missing at random — and the size of the time gap this case measures could be different from the true gap in the full 122-physician sample. This is a real, disclosed limitation of the dataset, not resolved by this case. | Whether this missingness is random is an open question the data itself cannot settle — a Hero who raises it is reasoning correctly, not making an error. |

## Balance Argument (facilitator reference — do not share with students before discussion)

**Argument for A (recommend the training):** Across the real dataset (Appendix B), average-weight patients' physicians expected to spend a mean of 31.4 minutes with them, versus 23.0 minutes for overweight-and-obese patients combined — a real, disclosed 26.6% gap. That gap is bigger, in percentage terms, than the training program's own total cost measured against a typical year's profit (13.4% of the 6-year average profit, Appendix C) — the size of the problem the data shows outweighs the size of the ask. The training is also cheap per physician ($265) relative to the hospital group's cost of a single serious discrimination complaint reaching a human-rights tribunal (Appendix E).

**Argument for B (do not recommend the training):** A half-million-dollar program ($490,780 at the case's own default reading of "its physicians" = all 1,852) is a real, meaningful expense for an operator averaging a 1.4% profit margin — not a rounding error, whichever profit-averaging window (Appendix D, MA2) is used to judge it. The underlying evidence is also one single-scenario experiment (a migraine visit) from one research study, with 13 of 122 physicians' responses missing and no confirmation the missingness is random (Appendix D, MA3) — spending real money on a hospital-wide mandate based on one incomplete dataset, before replicating the finding across other visit types, is a defensible position too.

**The margin:** The gap this case is built to keep genuinely close is between the size of the measured disparity (as a percentage) and the size of the training cost (as a percentage of a typical year's profit) — close enough that a careful Hero has to do real arithmetic, not eyeball it, to see which is bigger. See `case.json.balance` for the full computed figures; do not print them here in the student-facing document.

**The trap:** A Hero sees the 15-of-435 and 82-of-1,458 quarterly complaint numbers in Background and treats the *rising complaint rate* itself as the proof of bias, without ever touching the actual time-spent dataset in Appendix B — the complaint counts are context for why Lamont asked the question, not evidence of anything on their own (they are not adjusted for total patient volume by weight status, are not broken out by physician, and have no comparison baseline). A confident recommendation built only on the complaint counts, without doing D1–D5's actual work on Appendix B, is exactly the trap FACILITATOR.md's Socratic questions are built to expose.
