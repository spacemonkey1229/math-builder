Overview

Talia Brooks has two trajectory files open side by side, and the cursor has been sitting on the same blinking line for eleven minutes.

Meridian Launch Systems' mission control room is quiet this late — just the low hum of the environmental system and, somewhere down the hall, someone's keyboard going in short bursts. Talia is the trajectory analyst who signs the transfer-orbit design that goes into the customer's contract, and right now there are two designs, not one. The first plot on her left monitor climbs steeply and gets there fast — a hard, direct burn that puts the payload on orbit in days, if the propellant budget holds. The second plot, on the right, drifts out in a long, patient spiral that sips propellant instead of gulping it — and takes weeks longer to arrive.

Her inbox has a message from the payload customer's program manager, still unread, subject line starting with "RE: delivery date —". She hasn't opened it. She already knows roughly what it says, because she asked for exactly this kind of pressure when she requested the meeting that produced it.

What she doesn't know yet, not with numbers she'd sign her name to, is which trajectory actually delivers more usable payload for every day the customer has to wait. The steep burn eats into the mass budget before the payload ever separates from the kick stage. The long drift protects that mass — but every extra day in transit is a day the payload spends soaking in the belts.

Talia pulls both plots onto one screen, opens a blank sheet between them, and reaches for her calculator.

Background

Talia Brooks is the trajectory analyst at Meridian Launch Systems, a small commercial launch company that flies rideshare payloads to geostationary orbit using an expendable kick stage. She is responsible for recommending the transfer-orbit design that the mission uses once the payload separates from the launch vehicle's upper stage in low Earth parking orbit — and once she signs off, that design is what gets written into the customer's delivery contract (see Appendix A).

Two kick-stage trajectory designs are on the table for this mission, and both start from the same parking orbit and are aimed at the same target geostationary orbit radius (see Appendix A).

The Direct Burn

A high-thrust, direct parametric transfer trajectory: the kick stage's chemical engine fires hard early in the transfer, putting the payload on an aggressive, shorter-period transfer ellipse that reaches the target orbit radius in far less time. That thrust isn't free — a higher fraction of the kick stage's total launch mass has to be propellant, which is mass that does not get delivered as payload (see Appendices B and C).

The Patient Drift

A low-thrust, extended-coast parametric transfer trajectory: the kick stage's electric propulsion system fires gently over a much longer transfer, tracing a larger, longer-period transfer ellipse to the same target orbit radius. Because electric propulsion is far more propellant-efficient than the chemical burn, a smaller fraction of launch mass has to be propellant, and more of it survives as deliverable payload — but the payload spends far more days in transit, and a meaningful share of that transit crosses Earth's Van Allen radiation belts (see Appendices B and D).

The payload customer's contract fixes a maximum acceptable delivery window and a maximum acceptable cumulative radiation dose the payload's electronics are rated to survive in transit (see Appendix A) — either option must fall inside both limits to be flyable at all. Meridian's kick stage has a fixed total wet mass and a fixed dry (structure-plus-engine) mass regardless of which trajectory is flown; only the propellant load and the resulting deliverable payload mass change between the two designs (see Appendix C). Where a figure below is not a matter of public record, it is stated as an explicit modeling assumption (see Appendix E) rather than guessed, and every sourced figure is listed in Appendix F. The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case asks you to research are listed in Appendix G — where Challenge 1 sends you.

Both trajectories begin at Appendix A's parking-orbit radius and are designed to reach Appendix A's target orbit radius at transfer-ellipse apoapsis — D2 asks you to verify this directly from each option's own rectangular equation rather than take it on faith. Because both options start and end at the same two real orbital radii, the two-body transfer ellipse connecting those radii is geometrically identical for both — what differs between the Direct Burn and the Patient Drift is not the shape of the ellipse but how it is flown: a near-instantaneous chemical burn versus a continuous low-thrust spiral. A real continuous low-thrust transfer does not actually trace a single Keplerian ellipse at all; treating the Patient Drift as flying this same ellipse, only far more slowly, is a stated simplification (MA2, see Appendix E), not a literal description of low-thrust orbital dynamics.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which trajectory to recommend; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 · Parametric position setup (D1)

    Both options share the same transfer ellipse (Appendix B, MA2): semi-major axis a and eccentricity e. Using the eccentric anomaly E as the parameter (E ranges from 0 to 2π over one full transfer), the parametric position equations are x(E) = a(cos E − e) and y(E) = a√(1−e²) sin E, with Earth's center at the origin. At E = 0 the trajectory is at its closest point to Earth (periapsis, distance a(1−e)); at E = π it is at its farthest point (apoapsis, distance a(1+e)).

    Your value

    Worked example: a = 24,521 km, e = 0.7195 (Appendix B). Periapsis = a(1−e); apoapsis = a(1+e).

    Step 2 · Rectangular equation and intercept check (D2)

    Eliminate E from Step 1's parametric equations: solve x = a(cos E − e) for cos E, solve y = a√(1−e²) sin E for sin E, then use cos²E + sin²E = 1 to get the rectangular equation (x + ae)²/a² + y²/(a²(1−e²)) = 1. Use it to check: does each trajectory's apoapsis distance from Step 1 actually equal Appendix A's target orbit radius, and its periapsis distance equal Appendix A's parking orbit radius?

    Your check

    If your Step 1 apoapsis/periapsis don't line up with Appendix A's stated orbit radii within a reasonable rounding tolerance, re-check your a and e before moving on — D2 asks you to show this check explicitly, not assume it.

    Step 3 · Propellant mass fraction and deliverable payload (D3)

    Rocket equation: propellant mass = wet mass × (1 − e−Δv / (Isp · g₀)), where g₀ = 9.80665×10⁻³ km/s². Deliverable payload = wet mass − dry mass − propellant mass. Propellant-mass-fraction percent = 100 × propellant mass ÷ wet mass.

    Step 6 · What would have to change (D6)

    Holding every other figure fixed, how many days would the Patient Drift's transit time need to shrink to (from its stated 180 days) for its Step 4 kg/day figure to exactly equal the Direct Burn's?     Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    “Someone on the team says: ‘Wait — the Patient Drift protects noticeably more payload mass, and electric propulsion is famous for being way more efficient than chemical. Shouldn't that count for more? Why isn't this close?’ The primary metric isn't payload mass plus some efficiency credit — it's payload mass divided by transit time. Chemical thrust is measured in newtons to kilonewtons and converts nearly all its delta-v in minutes; Hall-effect electric thrust is measured in millinewtons, orders of magnitude weaker, so even though it needs more total delta-v for the same transfer, it can only deliver that thrust gradually, over months. A modest advantage in the numerator and a huge disadvantage in the denominator don't average out or partially cancel — they compound.”
