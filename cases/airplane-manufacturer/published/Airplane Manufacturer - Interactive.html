Overview

The laser cutter isn't even bolted to the floor yet, and Drew Larson is already staring at two spec sheets. Sparks from the neighboring bay throw orange light across the shop as a tech runs cable to the new machine — eight thousand watts of fiber laser, sitting in its packaging like a racehorse still in the trailer. Drew's phone buzzes on the workbench, face down, for the third time in ten minutes. Boeing again, probably. Or the CFO, who has asked twice this week when the new line starts paying for itself.

Drew turns back to the two folders spread across a steel table still tacky with cutting oil. One holds a spec sheet for a turbine disk — flat, toothed, unglamorous. The other holds a spec sheet for an inlet O-ring — the polished band that caps a jet engine's nacelle, the part passengers actually see through the window. Both folders end in the same number Drew keeps circling: how many of these, cut from the same sheets of aircraft aluminum, on the same machine, can this company actually produce.

The tech gives a thumbs up — the machine is live. Drew picks up both folders, walks toward the office where the pitch has to be written by Friday, and doesn't put the phone away.

Background

Stout Aviation is a privately owned company in Arizona that manufactures components for large jet engines, specializing in sheet metal fabrication — cutting and shaping large flat sheets of aluminum alloy into finished engine parts. The airline manufacturer Boeing is Stout Aviation's largest client.

As the airline industry has grown, demand for new planes and parts has pushed Stout Aviation's production up nearly 20% year over year for the past five years. To keep pace, Stout Aviation just approved the purchase of an additional fiber CNC laser cutting machine (see Appendix A), and has tasked the company's Chief Strategy Officer, Drew Larson, with deciding which new engine component the company should begin producing with it (see Appendix B and Appendix C).

Stout Aviation plans to run the new machine at most 20 hours per day, 5 days a week, with at least 2 hourly employees running the machine and loading sheet metal at all times. Each employee is paid an average of $45.00 per hour.

Through months of research and contacts inside Boeing, Drew has identified rising demand for two possible components: inlet O-rings and turbine disks. Both are cut from sheets of 7 meter by 5 meter, 2024-T3 aluminum alloy, which cost Stout Aviation $76.80 per square meter to purchase. Stout Aviation resells all excess scrap aluminum back to the distributor at $23.45 per square meter.

Every net dimension, cost, and modeling convention this case depends on is fixed in Appendix B, Appendix C, and the Modeling Assumptions (Appendix D) — use those conventions, or your numbers won't match anyone else's.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Cut-path length & cut time (D2, D3)

    Compute the total distance the laser must trace for ONE of each part (every edge and hole), then the time to cut it at 3 m/minute.

    Step 2 — Net material area & percent of sheet (D4)

    Compute the finished part's own footprint (not its bounding box) for each component, in square meters.

    Step 3 — Maximum weekly production (D5)

    Using load time + cut time per sheet, and a 100-hour operating week, how many of your chosen component can Stout Aviation produce in one week?

    Step 4 — Profit margin & breakeven (D6)

    Using your Step 3 component, compute profit margin as a percent of sale price, and how many weeks to break even on the $136,500 machine.
