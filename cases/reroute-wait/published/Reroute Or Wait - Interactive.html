Overview

Nadia Ferreiro has the whole east wall of the planning room covered in butcher paper, and the marker in her hand has gone dry. The line she keeps redrawing is Line Twenty — the bus that runs from the far west suburbs, straight down Burnside, over the river, and out to the far east end of the county. On the paper it is one continuous stroke. Soon it will not be, because the bridge it crosses is coming down to be replaced, and it is not coming back for years.

Her manager stops in the doorway with a coat over one arm and does not sit down. Riders' committee is Thursday. Somebody is going to stand up at that meeting and ask what happens to their bus, and whatever Nadia says will be the answer they hold her to. Two service plans are pinned side by side behind her: one keeps the line whole and sends every bus the long way around; the other cuts the line in half at the water and lets riders make the crossing themselves. She has a printout of the timetable, a printout of last spring's ridership counts, and the sinking feeling that both plans are defensible and that she is the one who has to pick.

Her manager says the word "Thursday" again, more gently, and leaves. Nadia caps the dead marker, looks at the two sheets, and reaches for the one on the left.

Background

Nadia Ferreiro is a service planner at TriMet, the transit agency for the Portland, Oregon metro region. She is responsible for writing the service plan that will run one bus line — Line 20-Burnside/Stark — for the duration of a multi-year bridge closure. She does not control the construction schedule and she does not get more buses; she controls only how the buses she already has are routed and how often they run. Her recommendation goes to the riders' committee and then to the agency's service planning board.

The closure is real and already recommended. Multnomah County's Earthquake Ready Burnside Bridge project will fully close the Burnside Bridge and detour traffic to neighbouring bridges while the existing bridge is demolished and replaced [S6] (see Appendix F). Line 20 crosses the Willamette River on that bridge, on the segment between its downtown timepoint and its first east-side timepoint (see Appendix A, Appendix B). Every other segment of Line 20 is untouched.

Two service plans are on the wall.

The Long Way Round — keep Line 20 as one through line and detour every bus around the closure over the next bridge north. Nobody transfers. But the detour lengthens the round trip, and with a fixed bus assignment a longer round trip buys a wider headway: under this plan Line 20 runs every twenty minutes instead of every fifteen, for everybody, all day, for the whole closure (see Appendix E, Appendix G MA1).
The Clean Break — split Line 20 into a west line and an east line that each stop at their own side of the river. Neither half's running time changes and both halves keep the fifteen-minute headway they have today [S2]. Riders who never cross the river do not lose a minute. Riders who do cross must get themselves across on the light rail line and pick up the other half of Line 20 on the far side (see Appendix C, Appendix E).

Nadia's metric is total added rider-minutes per weekday — the sum, across every Line 20 rider, of the minutes the plan costs them. That total is what she has to defend on Thursday. Computing it means treating the corridor as a network of stops joined by timed segments and asking, for each trip, what the fastest way through that network actually is under each plan (see Appendix A, Appendix B, Appendix C).

The ridership counts she has are real published agency counts, and they are richer than this decision needs (see Appendix D). Part of the work is deciding which of them bear on the question. Every figure in this case is either sourced in Appendix F or stated as an explicit modeling assumption in Appendix G. The vocabulary and the "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in Appendix I — that is where Challenge 1 sends you. (See Appendix H for further reading on the real bridge project and the earthquake risk behind it.)

Appendix E — The Two Service Plans, And How To Count Rider-Minutes

Option A — The Long Way Round. One through line. Every bus detours around the closed edge. Every rider whose fastest trip used the closed edge now rides the detour instead, at the extra in-vehicle cost stated in Appendix G, MA2. Because the round trip is longer and the bus assignment is fixed, the headway widens for every Line 20 rider whether they cross the river or not: from the published headway in Appendix B to the wider headway stated in Appendix G, MA1. Added waiting time per rider is half the increase in headway.

Option B — The Clean Break. Two lines, each keeping today's running times and today's headway [S2]. A rider who does not cross the closed edge is charged nothing. A rider who does cross rides the east half to Rose Quarter Transit Center (Appendix G, MA4), waits for a train, rides across on the light rail segment in Appendix B, walks from Pioneer Square North to W Burnside & SW 5th (Appendix G, MA3), waits for the west half, and no longer spends the minutes the direct Burnside Bridge segment used to take.

Average waiting time. For a service running on a fixed headway, this case charges a rider an average wait of half that headway. Where two light rail lines serve the same pair of stations on the same published headway [S3, S4], they are treated as one service at half that headway.

Counting the riders who cross. The published counts in Appendix D give total boardings and average trip length for the whole line, not an origin-and-destination table. This case therefore estimates the share of Line 20 trips that cross any one interior point of the route as the average trip length divided by the route's one-way length — the standard result for trips scattered uniformly along a line. The route's one-way length is not published directly and must be derived from Appendix D and Appendix B:

revenue hours  = boarding rides / rides per revenue hour
revenue miles  = passenger miles / passenger miles per revenue mile
average speed  = revenue miles / revenue hours
route length   = average speed x one-way running time from the timetable

The limits of this estimate are stated in Appendix G, MA6.

The ledger. Added rider-minutes per weekday under each option is the sum, over every rider group, of the number of riders in that group times the minutes that option costs each of them. Report waiting minutes and in-vehicle minutes on separate lines before totalling.

    Step 2 · Cycle-And-Cut Inventory (D2)

    Trace the graph. Exactly one cycle exists in this network — find it, then count how many edges lie on no cycle at all (these are the ones whose removal disconnects the network into two pieces).

    Hint: the loop drawn in Appendix A's diagram (W5–E12–RQ–PSN–W5) is the only place two different routes connect the same pair of vertices. Every edge outside that loop is a cut edge — which is exactly why Option A is possible for the closed W5–E12 edge (there's an alternate way around) and would not be possible for, say, BTC–STC (there isn't).

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    “A letter arrives from the county. The bridge the detour was going to use is having its own deck rehabilitated in the same window. Option A's buses have to go around again — one more detour, four more minutes for every rider who crosses. Option B is untouched: its river crossing is the light rail, which is on a different structure and a different programme.”

    “You have 60 seconds. Are you changing your recommendation — or not?”
