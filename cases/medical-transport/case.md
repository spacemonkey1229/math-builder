## Background

Inland Transport has operated in the Oregon area for more than 30 years, specializing in the transport of high-value and time-critical items, including sensitive documents, medical supplies, and human organs and tissue for transplant. Its healthcare logistics division runs a fleet of five 2018 Ford Focus sedans, working continuously to carry items from Portland International Airport (PDX) to regional hospitals in Portland, Hillsboro, Eugene, and Astoria (SEE APPENDIX A).

Josie Tramble has worked at Inland for three years since graduating from the University of Washington with a degree in Supply Chain Management. This is her first project as the newly promoted Healthcare Logistics Manager, and her first assignment is a policy recommendation on driving speed.

Inland runs two driving shifts of different drivers each day: 4:00 a.m.–12:00 p.m. and 12:00 p.m.–8:00 p.m. Cars are picked up from Inland's lot at the start of each shift and returned to the same lot by the end of it. By state health-code regulation, each driver can transport only one item at a time — packages are picked up curbside from an airport courier at PDX (which stores time-sensitive items in secure cold storage, even overnight, until a driver is dispatched) and delivered directly to the receiving hospital. Drivers are required by law to take a 30-minute break sometime between their 4th and 6th working hour, during which the car is out of service (SEE APPENDIX C).

Inland is paid $3.54 per mile driven on each delivery. Averaged across a full day, the fleet drives 352 freeway miles, 142 highway miles, and 110 city miles (SEE APPENDIX C). Every car must be returned with a full tank of gas; Inland fuels at a commercial cardlock station and reports company-wide fuel spend as a single line item (SEE APPENDIX B). Each vehicle carries its own insurance policy, and Inland covers up to 4 speeding-ticket violations per vehicle per year before further consequences apply (SEE APPENDIX E).

In addition to her $105,000 annual salary, Josie earns a $7,500 quarterly bonus if the fleet averages at least 5 long-distance deliveries (round trips of 180+ miles) and at least 18 total deliveries per day (SEE APPENDIX A). Her presentation to the board must include a firm, defended recommendation for the speed her drivers should drive in the city, on the highway, and on the freeway — Oregon's posted limits for each are given in Appendix D, along with what Inland's own insurer and Oregon's courts actually charge when a driver is caught over them. Every figure Josie's presentation relies on is cited in Appendix G, and the standards she'll need to research to build it are listed in Appendix H.

## Dilemma

You are Josie Tramble.

A.) Pitch a policy that Inland's drivers never exceed the posted speed limit, in the city, on the highway, or on the freeway OR
B.) Pitch a policy that Inland's drivers routinely drive 10 mph over the posted speed limit, in the city, on the highway, and on the freeway

## Appendix A — Map and Delivery Data

**Route:** Portland International Airport (PDX) → regional hospital → Inland's lot (5741 NE 87th Ave, Portland, OR 97220). Distances are one-way, PDX to hospital [S9].

| Hospital | City | One-way distance from PDX | Round trip | Average deliveries/day |
|---|---|---|---|
| Randall Children's Hospital at Legacy Emanuel | Portland | 7.8 mi | 15.6 mi | 8.4 |
| Hillsboro Medical Center | Hillsboro | 34 mi | 68 mi | 7.6 |
| PeaceHealth Sacred Heart Medical Center | Eugene | 122 mi | 244 mi | 2.7 |
| Columbia Memorial Hospital | Astoria | 97 mi | 194 mi | 1.8 |

"Long-distance" per Josie's bonus criteria is defined as a round trip of 180+ miles — under this table, that is Eugene (244 mi) and Astoria (194 mi) runs only.

**Delivery-capacity check (see Appendix C):** the deliveries/day column above is the fleet's real average daily volume, not a drive-time ceiling. At 7.5 hours of available driving per shift (8-hour shift minus the required 30-minute break), the fleet's total capacity is 5 vehicles x 2 shifts x 7.5 hr = **75 vehicle-hours/day**. Driving this table's average mix consumes only about 31.7 of those hours under Option A (42% utilized) and about 26.4 under Option B (35% utilized) — either way, well under capacity. That means total and long-distance delivery *volume* is bound by real hospital demand, not by how fast the fleet drives, and stays the same under both options: speed does not manufacture more patients. The one place speed genuinely changes what a *single* vehicle can mechanically do: dedicated only to the Eugene route, one vehicle clears just 1 round trip per 7.5-hour shift at Option A's 3.91-hour trip time (2/day total) — short of Eugene's own 2.7/day average, so Option A structurally needs a second, partially-dedicated vehicle to cover Eugene. At Option B's 3.36-hour trip time, that same single vehicle clears 2 round trips per shift (4/day), comfortably covering Eugene's demand alone. That is a real, quantified scheduling-efficiency advantage for Option B — it just doesn't move the fleet's average long-distance count past Josie's 5/day bonus threshold.

**Effect of speed on fuel economy:** the U.S. Department of Energy's fueleconomy.gov states that gas mileage "usually decreases rapidly at speeds above 50 mph," and that "each 5 mph you drive over 50 mph is like paying an additional $0.29 per gallon for gas" [S2]. This case models that effect as a 7% mpg reduction for each 5 mph driven over 50 mph, compounding per increment (e.g., driving 15 mph over 50 applies the 7% reduction three times) [S2, derived].

## Appendix D — Oregon Speed Limits and Speeding Fines

| Road type | Posted limit | Option A speed | Option B speed |
|---|---|---|---|
| City (Portland-area arterial) | 35 mph [S3] | 35 mph | 45 mph |
| Highway | 55 mph [S4] | 55 mph | 65 mph |
| Freeway / Interstate | 65 mph [S4] | 65 mph | 75 mph |

Oregon classifies a speeding violation by how many mph over the posted limit the driver was clocked, and each class carries a fixed presumptive fine [S5, S6]:

| MPH over limit | Violation class | Presumptive fine |
|---|---|---|
| 1–10 | Class D | $115 |
| 11–20 | Class C | $165 |
| 21–30 | Class B | $265 |
| 31+ | Class A | $440 |

**Exception:** on a road posted 65 mph or faster, exceeding the limit by 10 mph or less is charged one class higher — Class C instead of Class D [S5]. Under Option B, the freeway violation (65→75, posted limit is 65) is therefore a **Class C, $165** violation, not a Class D $115 violation like the city and highway 10-over violations.

## Appendix E — Inland's Insurance Policy

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Base premium | $1,453.00 per vehicle per year |
| Surcharge per speeding-ticket violation | $164.95 per vehicle per year |
| Tickets Inland covers before other consequences apply | up to 4 per vehicle per year |

Independent market research on U.S. auto insurance confirms this magnitude: one speeding ticket raises a driver's annual premium by roughly 24% on average nationally [S7] — on Inland's $1,453 base premium, that is a $349 increase, consistent in magnitude with Inland's own stated $164.95 surcharge for a single violation. That same research shows mph-over-limit severity barely moves the number (its own rate table varies by less than 1.5% across brackets from "under 5 over" to "21–25 over") — what actually drives a bigger increase is the *count* of tickets on record: a second ticket on record raises the premium by roughly 45%, and a third by roughly 60% [S7].

## Appendix F — Crash Risk Data

The Nilsson/Elvik "Power Model," used by U.S. and international transportation-safety researchers to relate travel speed to crash outcomes, models the relationship as: for a given percent increase in average travel speed, crashes involving injury increase by roughly that percent squared (relative), serious-injury crashes by roughly that percent cubed (relative), and fatal crashes by roughly that percent to the fourth power (relative) [S8]. In practical terms across the range of speeds this case covers, researchers report that fatal-crash risk rises fastest, followed by serious-injury risk, then all-injury-crash risk [S8].

Oregon recorded 539 traffic deaths in 2024 (587 in 2023), and speed remains one of the top contributing factors named in the state's own crash reporting [S10].
