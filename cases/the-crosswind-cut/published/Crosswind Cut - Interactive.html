Overview

The corridor advisory refreshes on Jordan Reyes's second monitor and the number in the crosswind field does not move. Behind the glass, Drone 114 sits on its pad with its rotors folded, waiting on a configuration Jordan has not yet locked in. The prelaunch checklist has one field still blank: motor/prop config.

Two toolboxes are open on the bench. One holds the high-KV motors and the small, fast propellers — the pair Jordan's team calls the sprint rig. The other holds the low-KV motors and the wide, slow-turning propellers — the cruise rig. Once ground crew bolts one pair on, that is what Drone 114 flies today. There is no swapping mid-route.

Dispatch pings again: the FAA waiver that lets Swiftwing fly this corridor beyond visual line of sight is conditioned on staying inside the mapped lateral boundary, published to the meter. Drift outside it and the flight is a reportable violation, not just a bad delivery. Drop below the mandatory battery reserve before touchdown and the drone is required to set down wherever it is, waiver corridor or not.

Jordan pulls up both spec sheets side by side — thrust curves, current draw, the works — and starts doing the arithmetic that neither toolbox label tells you: how much of each rig's thrust budget actually points down the corridor once the wind gets paid first.

The cursor blinks over the checklist's empty configuration field.

Background

Jordan Reyes is a flight-operations engineer at Swiftwing Regional Logistics, a last-mile drone delivery operator. Jordan is the person on shift responsible for locking in each drone's motor/propeller configuration before it launches, and for signing off that the choice keeps the flight inside the operating limits Swiftwing's FAA beyond-visual-line-of-sight waiver requires. Today's route runs Drone 114 down a mapped delivery corridor that a published crosswind advisory crosses at close to a right angle to the direction of travel (see Appendix B).

Two configurations are available for this airframe, and both are drawn from the same manufacturer's published multirotor propeller/motor performance data rather than a single marketing number (see Appendix A):

Configuration Sparrow

The high-KV motor paired with the smaller-diameter propeller. It spins faster and has more raw thrust on tap, at the cost of drawing more current for every pound of thrust it produces.

Configuration Albatross

The low-KV motor paired with the larger-diameter propeller. In calm air it produces more thrust for every amp the battery gives up, but it has a smaller margin of surplus thrust left over once some of that thrust has to be spent holding the drone on course against a crosswind.

Both configurations are bolted to the same airframe, carry the same payload, and fly the same corridor at the same commanded groundspeed (see Appendix A) — so the two options differ in exactly two things: how much total thrust each is capable of producing, and how much current it costs to produce it.

Two hard constraints apply to either choice. First, the corridor's lateral boundary is fixed by the waiver: the drone must hold its programmed heading down the corridor centerline, which means whichever configuration flies today has to devote part of its thrust budget to cancelling the crosswind's push off that heading before any thrust is "useful" (see Appendix B). Second, Swiftwing's flight rules set a mandatory battery reserve that must remain in the pack at touchdown, computed from the configuration's current draw over the route's flight time (see Appendix C). Jordan has to check both configurations against that reserve rule before locking one in.

Where a figure in this case is not a matter of public record, it is stated as an explicit modeling assumption (see Appendix D) rather than guessed, and every sourced figure is listed in Appendix E. The vocabulary and the "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in Appendix G — that is where Challenge 1 sends you. How the wind is resolved. The crosswind is treated as a single vector, constant along the whole corridor, with a component perpendicular to the corridor heading (the part that pushes the drone off centerline) and a component along the corridor heading (the part that helps or hurts groundspeed directly). Cancelling the perpendicular component is what consumes part of each configuration's thrust budget before any thrust counts as forward progress.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which configuration to bolt on; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 · Thrust-and-current totals (D1)

    Sum each configuration's per-motor thrust and current draw (Appendix A) across the airframe's 4 motors (Appendix D, MA3) at the route's 90% throttle setting.
    Sparrow — total thrust (g)Sparrow — total current (A)Albatross — total thrust (g)Albatross — total current (A)
    Your total

    Worked example: Sparrow's per-motor thrust is 2,782 g (Appendix A). Multiply by the 4-motor airframe (Appendix D, MA3) to get total thrust. Do the same for current, then repeat both for Albatross.

    Step 2 · Crosswind-cancellation vector (D2)

    The crosswind's component perpendicular to the corridor heading is crosswind × sin(crossing angle) (Appendix B, D MA1/MA2). The wind-correction angle θ is the angle the thrust vector must crab away from the heading so its own perpendicular component exactly cancels that crosswind push: sin(θ) = (crosswind × sin(crossing angle)) ÷ groundspeed (Appendix D, MA4). This θ — and cos(θ) — is common to both configurations, since MA1/MA2/MA4 don't depend on which rig is bolted on.

    This is the vector-subtraction step: each configuration's total thrust vector (Step 1) is aimed θ degrees off the corridor heading so that its perpendicular component exactly cancels the crosswind's push. What's left pointing down the corridor is what Step 3 dot-products out.

    Step 3 · Net forward-progress thrust (D3)

    Net forward-progress thrust is the dot product of the total thrust vector (Step 1) with the delivery-heading unit vector, once the thrust vector is aimed at the wind-correction angle θ from Step 2: net forward thrust = total thrust × cos(θ).

    Step 4 · Thrust-per-amp comparison (D4)

    For each configuration, compare the manufacturer's advertised calm-air thrust-per-amp figure (Appendix A) against the net-forward-progress-thrust-per-amp figure: net forward thrust (Step 3) ÷ total current (Step 1).

    Step 5 · Battery-reserve check (D5)

    Flight time = route length (Appendix B, D MA5) ÷ groundspeed (Appendix D, MA4). Battery used = total current (Step 1) × flight time. Percent of the mandatory reserve remaining = 100 × (pack capacity − battery used) ÷ pack capacity (Appendix C).
