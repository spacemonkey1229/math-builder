## Overview

The klaxon cuts through the War Room before the analyst even finishes standing up. "General — all enemy missile sites just lit up. Launch sequences are initiating across the board." General Marcus T. Reyes doesn't look up from the screen yet. "Time to launch?"

"Twelve minutes, sir. Maybe less."

Reyes is already moving. "Get the birds at Camp Dragonbreath in the air. Now." Across the room, Lieutenant General Reina Harper is pulling two folders off her desk before he's even finished the sentence — she's been running these numbers for a week, hoping she'd never have to hand them over for real. "Grey Dawn and Desert Sparrow, sir. Both ready. You have four minutes before the jets are airborne, and then it's fixed — no changing the plan once they're off the runway."

Reyes takes both folders. Two F-16s are already taxiing on the tarmac feed behind her, engines spooling. He has four minutes to read what's inside, and then the choice is made for however long it takes those jets to fly.

He opens the first folder.

## Background

General Marcus T. Reyes commands the joint strike cell responsible for Camp Dragonbreath, a forward airbase sitting just across the border from a hostile state whose ballistic missile program has been under close watch for months. When the base's early-warning radar confirms that every tracked missile site in the region has begun launch preparations, Reyes has to choose how to use the only aircraft he has in the air in time to matter: two F-16 fighters, armed and airborne within four minutes of the order, leaving exactly **12 minutes** of flight time before the missile sites finish their launch sequences (**SEE APPENDIX A**).

Lieutenant General Harper's team has prepared two pre-analyzed strike plans:

**Operation Grey Dawn** — order both F-16s to attack and destroy the long-range **ICBM** sites first; only after every reachable ICBM site is destroyed do the jets use any remaining flight time on short-range **SRBM** sites.

**Operation Desert Sparrow** — order both F-16s to attack and destroy as many missile sites as possible along the fastest available route, with no priority given to ICBM sites over SRBM sites.

The Defense Intelligence Agency's site survey lists nine ICBM sites and six SRBM sites, each carrying one of five warhead classes, plotted on a coordinate grid centered on Camp Dragonbreath (**SEE APPENDIX A**). Each warhead class carries a real population-at-risk figure if it reaches a major urban center (**SEE APPENDIX B**). The two missile types are not equally dangerous to the same people: each ICBM site carries a single long-range missile whose class can reach the entire continental United States and is not stopped by the U.S. missile defense system now in place (**SEE APPENDIX C**). Each SRBM site carries a shorter-range missile that threatens regional allied cities and nearby U.S. military installations, and unlike the ICBM threat, an allied missile-defense network has a real, if partial, chance of shooting an SRBM down after launch (**SEE APPENDIX C**).

Once airborne, every F-16 in this case flies at the same top speed, and cannot fire on a target until it closes to within a fixed standoff distance of it (**SEE APPENDIX C**). Every missile site destroyed before it launches is a population fully protected from that site's warhead; every site still standing when the 12-minute window closes is a population still at risk, discounted for SRBM sites by the intercept chance in Appendix C — how each site's yield translates to a population-at-risk figure, and every other modeling convention this case fixes, is in **APPENDIX D**. Every cited figure in Appendix C is fully sourced in **APPENDIX E**.

The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in **APPENDIX F** — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

## Balance Argument (facilitator reference — not part of the student-facing case)

**Argument for A (Operation Grey Dawn):** With only two F-16s and a 12-minute window, a nearest-neighbor ICBM-first route destroys 8 of the 9 ICBM sites (88.9% — missing only ICBM-Golf, the most distant site from Camp Dragonbreath) plus one SRBM site reached with the small amount of time left over — 9 sites total. Desert Sparrow's own route only reaches 7 of 9 ICBM sites (77.8%), because two legs it would need for ICBM-Hotel and ICBM-Golf get spent on closer SRBM sites instead: on ICBM-neutralization rate specifically, Grey Dawn beats Desert Sparrow by 11.1 percentage points — a 14.3% relative margin, close to Desert Sparrow's own headline 13.6% population margin, just measured on the undefended threat class instead of raw population. That distinction is real, not rhetorical: this case's own model applies no intercept discount to ICBM sites at all (MA2 is SRBM-only), while every SRBM site either operation leaves standing at least faces a real, sourced 5.6% intercept chance (Appendix C, S2). Grey Dawn leaves just one ICBM site live (352,364 people, undiscounted — Appendix B's derived 1 Mt figure) with zero backstop, trading a smaller population-protected total for eliminating nearly all of the one threat class this region has no real defense against.

**Argument for B (Operation Desert Sparrow):** Ignoring the ICBM/SRBM distinction and simply routing to the nearest live target every time reaches 11 of the 15 sites total (7 ICBM + 4 SRBM) — two more sites than Grey Dawn destroys, because it never "wastes" a leg flying past a closer SRBM site to reach a farther ICBM site. Total expected population protected comes out **13.6% higher** than Grey Dawn's total. The cost is that 2 ICBM sites remain live instead of 1 — a real, if partial, increase in the number of over-the-continental-US threats still standing.

**Margin basis:** Primary metric is total expected population protected (population fully protected at destroyed sites, using Appendix B's derived table with Appendix D's MA2 intercept discount applied to SRBM sites), computed with each operation's fastest 2-jet nearest-neighbor route under the 12-minute window. Grey Dawn protects 4,844,636 of 7,830,282 people at risk (61.9%); Desert Sparrow protects 5,501,882 (70.3%) — a 13.6% relative margin in B's favor. See `sensitivity_model.py` for the full computation and `case.json.derived`.

**The twist (see FACILITATOR.md):** a third F-16 becomes available, but only after a 2-minute delay (a 10-minute window instead of 12). Recomputing both operations' routes with 3 jets: Grey Dawn now completes **all 9** ICBM sites plus 3 SRBM sites (12 of 15, protecting 6,247,319), while Desert Sparrow reaches 13 of 15 (protecting 6,561,439) — the margin **narrows from 13.6% to 5.0%**, without flipping which option wins. This is the trap: a Hero who assumed Grey Dawn's ICBM sites were already "spent" capacity discovers the third jet lets Grey Dawn close almost the entire gap by finally finishing what it started.

**Assumption-sensitivity disclosure (MA3, re-derived 2026-08-13):** Appendix B's population figures are now computed from two real sources (Glasstone & Dolan's cube-root blast-radius scaling, S4; 2020 Census average urban density, S5), not guessed — but the underlying real-world quantity (exactly how dense a specific, unnamed "major urban center" is) is still genuinely uncertain, the same way a future price or a medical prognosis is: no single number is "the" answer. A Tier 2 flex check confirms this real uncertainty still moves the verdict: flexing the 5.2 Mt figure -50% (a less-dense city) flips the winner to Grey Dawn; flexing the 3.1 Mt figure +50% (a denser one) does too. This is disclosed here and in FACILITATOR.md's Lessons Learned rather than eliminated, because eliminating it would mean picking a single density and hiding the real range of plausible major cities behind it — the case's own point is that a Hero must be ready to defend which density they assumed and show how their recommendation would change under a denser or sparser target city.
