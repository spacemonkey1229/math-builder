Overview

The klaxon cuts through the War Room before the analyst even finishes standing up. "General — all enemy missile sites just lit up. Launch sequences are initiating across the board." General Marcus T. Reyes doesn't look up from the screen yet. "Time to launch?"

"Twelve minutes, sir. Maybe less."

Reyes is already moving. "Get the birds at Camp Dragonbreath in the air. Now." Across the room, Lieutenant General Reina Harper is pulling two folders off her desk before he's even finished the sentence — she's been running these numbers for a week, hoping she'd never have to hand them over for real. "Grey Dawn and Desert Sparrow, sir. Both ready. You have four minutes before the jets are airborne, and then it's fixed — no changing the plan once they're off the runway."

Reyes takes both folders. Two F-16s are already taxiing on the tarmac feed behind her, engines spooling. He has four minutes to read what's inside, and then the choice is made for however long it takes those jets to fly.

He opens the first folder.

Background

General Marcus T. Reyes commands the joint strike cell responsible for Camp Dragonbreath, a forward airbase sitting just across the border from a hostile state whose ballistic missile program has been under close watch for months. When the base's early-warning radar confirms that every tracked missile site in the region has begun launch preparations, Reyes has to choose how to use the only aircraft he has in the air in time to matter: two F-16 fighters, armed and airborne within four minutes of the order, leaving exactly 12 minutes of flight time before the missile sites finish their launch sequences (SEE APPENDIX A).

Lieutenant General Harper's team has prepared two pre-analyzed strike plans:

Operation Grey Dawn — order both F-16s to attack and destroy the long-range ICBM sites first; only after every reachable ICBM site is destroyed do the jets use any remaining flight time on short-range SRBM sites.

Operation Desert Sparrow — order both F-16s to attack and destroy as many missile sites as possible along the fastest available route, with no priority given to ICBM sites over SRBM sites.

The Defense Intelligence Agency's site survey lists nine ICBM sites and six SRBM sites, each carrying one of five warhead classes, plotted on a coordinate grid centered on Camp Dragonbreath (SEE APPENDIX A). The two missile types are not equally dangerous to the same people: each ICBM site carries a single long-range missile whose class can reach the entire continental United States and is not stopped by the U.S. missile defense system now in place (SEE APPENDIX C). Each SRBM site carries a shorter-range missile that threatens regional allied cities and nearby U.S. military installations, and unlike the ICBM threat, an allied missile-defense network has a real, if partial, chance of shooting an SRBM down after launch (SEE APPENDIX C).

Once airborne, every F-16 in this case flies at the same top speed, and cannot fire on a target until it closes to within a fixed standoff distance of it (SEE APPENDIX C). Every missile site destroyed before it launches is a population fully protected from that site's warhead; every site still standing when the 12-minute window closes is a population still at risk, discounted for SRBM sites by the intercept chance in Appendix C — how each site's yield translates to a population-at-risk figure, and every other modeling convention this case fixes, is in APPENDIX D.

The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in APPENDIX F — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.
