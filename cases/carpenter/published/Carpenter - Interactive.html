Overview

Sawdust hangs in the light from the bare living-room window. Newt Harrison runs a thumbnail along the seam where the old linoleum used to be, now just bare subfloor and a faint rectangle of glue residue. Behind him, a text thread is still open on his phone, propped against a paint can: Whatever you recommend, Newt — we trust you. Just tell us what and why.

Ten years of remodels in this city, and he's never had a client hand him the whole call like that.

He walks the flat again — bedroom, bedroom, bedroom, the wide living room where the light comes in low in the afternoon, the kitchen already gutted down to studs on one wall. Three sample boards lean against the fireplace: a carpet swatch soft enough to sink a heel into, a plank of hardwood with a grain like a fingerprint, and a stack of tile samples still rubber-banded together from the supplier.

His notebook is open on the counter, half a page of numbers in his own handwriting, the rest blank. A price list. A wear chart. A currency he still has to convert every time, because the lumber yard prices everything ten minutes south of the border, in a different money than the one that'll actually pay for it.

Newt caps his pencil and looks at the three boards one more time.

Somebody has to close this out by Friday.

Background

Spencer and Michelle Berisel bought a flat in downtown Montreal, Quebec, that they plan to run as a short-term rental (see Appendix A). They live outside the city in Granby and won't be on-site day to day. The flat turns over tenants at least weekly, with heavy foot traffic between bookings — the ordinary wear of a busy short-term rental, not a quiet family home.

The Berisels have told Newt Harrison, the carpenter finishing the rest of the remodel, that "comfort and luxury" are what will make this listing stand out in a crowded market — but with every other line item in the renovation already spoken for, they've also set a soft budget cap of CD$17,900 (Canadian dollars) for flooring alone: initial materials, delivery, and installation, all in. It's a target they'd like to hit, not a hard wall Newt is forbidden to cross — going over it costs him no penalty and breaks no contract, it just means the pitch has to explain, in plain numbers, why it was worth it — and they've asked him to show his work either way.

Because building-material prices vary from one side of a border to another the same way gas or groceries do, flooring materials happen to run cheaper sourced from the U.S. than bought locally in Quebec. So Newt buys everything in Burlington, Vermont — a short drive south — and has it delivered north. The supplier prices every material in U.S. dollars; the Berisels pay Newt in Canadian dollars, so every material cost has to cross that conversion at least once (see Appendix D). The supplier also charges a delivery fee of a flat 2.6% rate on the total material cost for the cross-border haul — "flat" meaning that same percentage applies no matter which flooring is ordered or how big the order is, not a fixed dollar amount.

Two rooms of the flat are locked in no matter what: the kitchen and all bathrooms will be tiled, full stop — nobody is proposing carpet or hardwood in a bathroom. What's still open is the bedrooms and the living room, and Newt has boiled it down to two real options for that space (see Appendix A for the floor plan Newt is pricing against, and Appendix B for what each material actually costs and how long it lasts). Every figure Newt is working from carries some kind of caveat or judgment call, spelled out plainly in Appendix F, rather than buried quietly in a table. Two articles Newt has already bookmarked while researching this pitch are in Appendix E.

Appendix C — Maintenance & Refinishing Costs Over Time

Flooring isn't a one-time purchase. Each material has its own ongoing cost to keep it rentable between guests, and — for shorter-lived materials — its own replacement cycle. (Grout is porous, so it soaks up moisture and grime between tiles over time; resealing it periodically is what keeps that from happening. A hardwood recoat buffs and refreshes only the thin top finish layer, without cutting into the wood itself — cheaper and faster than a full sand-and-refinish, which sands down through that worn finish, and sometimes into the wood's surface, before applying new coats; a floor only needs the bigger job once the top layer has worn through, not on every service.)

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Area takeoff

    Convert the floor plan's total square meters (Appendix A) into square feet for each zone, using 1 m² = 10.764 ft². Use the rounded totals from the table.

    Step 2 — Materials-and-delivery worksheet, per option

    For each option: (zone material cost + tile material cost) × 2.6% delivery fee, plus all installation labor, converted USD→CAD at 1.3785 (Appendix D). Enter each option's total initial cost in CAD.

    Step 3 — Budget check

    Compare your picked option's initial cost (Step 2) against the Berisels' CD$17,900 soft cap. Positive = over the cap; negative = under it.

    Step 4 — 15-year total cost of ownership

    Add initial cost (Step 2) to all maintenance, cleaning, and any full replacement/refinishing over 15 years (Appendix C), for the option you picked. Read Appendix B/C's figures at face value for now — don't apply the Twist yet unless you've checked the box below.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    "Newt makes one more call before he sends the pitch — to his flooring wholesaler, who's fitted plenty of short-term rentals. The wholesaler tells him something the spec sheet doesn't say: under nightly-turnover, heavy-traffic use — exactly what the Berisels are running — carpet's real-world replacement clock runs at roughly half the normal residential figure. Not 8 to 10 years. More like 4 to 5. Hardwood, being a hard, dense material, doesn't see nearly the same effect — it might need a touch more frequent recoating, but it doesn't need replacing on a different clock."

    Instead of one full carpet replacement landing inside the 15-year horizon, two now do — at year 5 and year 10. Recheck Step 4 for Option A with the box below checked, and recheck Step 5. Does this change your recommendation — or not? And more importantly: what just broke in your original model? Were you wrong about the price of flooring, or were you just missing what kind of building this actually is?
