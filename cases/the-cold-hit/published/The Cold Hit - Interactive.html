Overview

"It's a name and a number, Dana. Tell me if that's enough."

Her supervisor said it from the doorway an hour ago and hasn't come back for the answer yet. Dana Osei has had the printout face-down on her desk ever since — she doesn't want to look at it again until she's decided what to write.

Six months ago, a degraded blood swab from a decade-old assault case went back through the lab for the third time — new chemistry, better recovery, same brittle old sample. It came back with a partial genetic profile, thin but real. Dana ran it the way she runs every cold case: search the state database first. Nothing. Escalate to the national one. A name came back.

No fingerprint. No confession. No witness. Just a name, a profile, and a number Dana has spent her whole career explaining to juries who want it to mean something simpler than it does.

The DA's office has a backlog of its own and wants a yes or a no, not a lecture on probability. Dana's unit has a written policy for exactly this moment, and today is the day she has to decide which version of it she's actually going to follow.

She turns the printout back over.

Background

Dana Osei is the forensic statistician for a state crime laboratory's DNA unit. Her lab is not a separate outside agency — it operates as part of the state's Bureau of Forensic Services, the parent state agency that "the lab" and "the state's crime lab" both refer to throughout this case; when this case says "the lab," it means the DNA unit inside the Bureau of Forensic Services. She doesn't investigate cases and she doesn't decide guilt — her job is to tell the department, in writing, exactly what a DNA hit does and does not prove, before it becomes the centerpiece of a referral.

A DNA genetic profile is just a list of results at specific spots on the genome (a genome is the complete set of DNA instructions in a cell) called loci (one spot is a locus) — picture a locus as one labeled slot in a form, and the result recorded there as whatever got written in that slot. A full CODIS profile has 20 such slots. Two people's profiles "match" at a locus when the entries in that slot are identical; a full match means every slot recorded agrees. A degraded sample — old, broken down, partially destroyed — often can't produce a reliable result at every slot, which is why this case's evidence sample only has a usable entry at 8 of the 20 (see loci and Appendix C).

How a database search actually works

"Running a search" is a mechanical, five-step process, not a black box:

  1. Enter the profile. Lab staff type the evidence sample's locus-by-locus results into CODIS — software maintained by the FBI that every participating crime lab connects to.
  2. The software compares. CODIS automatically checks that list, slot by slot, against every profile already stored in the database being searched, looking for an exact match at every recovered locus.
  3. State first, then national. The search runs against the state's own data bank first. If nothing turns up there, it escalates to the FBI's National DNA Index System, which pools participating states' data banks together (see LDIS / SDIS / NDIS and Appendix A).
  4. A candidate hit. If the software finds an exact match, that's a "hit" — but only a candidate. Nothing is released to investigators yet.
  5. Manual confirmation, then release. Lab staff re-check the candidate match by hand against the original physical evidence sample. Only after that human confirmation does a name get released to investigators — and even then, it becomes a lead, not automatically a referral.

The search Dana runs is a fixed, mechanical sequence, not a single black-box lookup: state database first, national only on a miss, and a computer match is never released to investigators until a human re-checks it by hand.

The lab's cold-case unit has exactly two policies on the table for handling a database "cold hit" — a match with no other evidence yet attached — and department leadership has asked Dana to model both before the unit commits for the year. Refer on Match sends the referral memo to the district attorney's office as soon as a search returns a hit, with no further lab-side delay. Hold for Corroboration keeps every cold-hit case in the unit for one additional step — a documented, independent, non-DNA cross-check, such as confirming the person's known location or associations line up with the crime — before it is referred, which costs investigator time on every single case but is designed to catch the cases where a hit was coincidence, not culprit. The cost of getting this wrong is not just wasted time: a referral that later has to be withdrawn carries discovery obligations and real reputational and liberty consequences for whoever was wrongly named.

The core quantity separating the two policies is not the offender's guilt or innocence — it is the probability that a given hit is a coincidental match: two different people who happen to share a genetic profile at every locus the lab could recover, not because they're related, but by chance. That probability depends on how many loci were usable (a degraded sample yields fewer) and, separately, on how many other profiles the search compared against — a hit found by comparing against millions of records is not equivalent to a one-to-one comparison, a distinction with real statistical and legal history (SEE APPENDIX C).

Appendix A — CODIS Architecture: The Three-Tier Search and the 20 Core Loci

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D2, D4). The Combined DNA Index System (CODIS) operates at three levels: the Local DNA Index System (LDIS), where a profile is first developed; the State DNA Index System (SDIS), which lets laboratories within one state compare against each other; and the National DNA Index System (NDIS), the top tier, which compares across every participating state [S4]. A profile that finds no match at the state level is eligible to be searched at the national level — this is exactly the two-stage search Dana's unit runs on every cold case.

Since January 1, 2017, every laboratory participating in NDIS has been required to type a profile at a minimum of 20 CODIS core loci, up from the original 13 used since the 1990s. The FBI's own stated reason: as CODIS grew larger, expanding the core loci became "imperative... in order to reduce the possibility of fictitious matches," alongside gains in discriminating power and compatibility with international DNA databases [S5]. The 13 original core loci are CSF1PO, D3S1358, D5S818, D7S820, D8S1179, D13S317, D16S539, D18S51, D21S11, FGA, TH01, TPOX, and vWA; the 7 loci added in 2017 are D1S1656, D2S441, D2S1338, D10S1248, D12S391, D19S433, and D22S1045 [S5]. This case's evidence sample is too degraded to recover all 20 — only 8 loci produced a usable result (SEE APPENDIX C).

Those locus codes (CSF1PO, D3S1358, TH01, vWA, and so on) are just the standardized names for each of the 20 tested locations on the genome — think of them like street names on a map. Nothing in this case depends on knowing what makes any individual locus different from another; they only matter here as a count (8 usable out of 20 total).

Appendix C — Match Probability Figures

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D1, D2).

Per-locus match probability. Real, published per-locus random match probabilities vary by locus and by population group — there is no single true value. A commonly cited representative figure, drawn from courtroom testimony reported in the press, is that two unrelated people have roughly a 7.5% (0.075) chance of matching by coincidence at any single locus [S3]. This case uses that figure as a stated representative average across loci.

This case's usable loci. The evidence sample in this case produced a usable result at 8 of the 20 CODIS core loci — this specific count (8) is a scenario construction for this case, chosen to be realistic for a degraded decade-old sample.

The database match probability (DMP) rule. A 1996 National Research Council report (part of the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, a body chartered by Congress in 1863 to give the U.S. independent scientific advice) on forensic DNA evidence addressed exactly Dana's situation — a match found by searching a database, not by comparing one suspect to one piece of evidence. Its recommendation: "When the suspect is found by a search of DNA databases, the random-match probability should be multiplied by N, the number of persons in the database," producing what is called the database match probability [S6]. In symbols: DMP = N × RMP, where N is the number of profiles searched and RMP is the random match probability for the profile recovered.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which policy to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Random match probability, 8 usable loci (D1)

    Appendix C gives a per-locus match probability and says this case's evidence sample has a usable result at 8 loci. Multiply the per-locus probability by itself once for each usable locus — this only works because a match at one locus is an independent event from a match at any other locus. Enter your RMP as a decimal (it will be a very small number — scientific notation like d.ddde-d is fine).

    Step 2 — Database match probability, both search stages (D2)

    Using Appendix B's two database sizes and your Step 1 RMP, apply DMP = N × RMP at each search stage.

    Step 3 — Cases closed per detective-and-lab hour (D3)

    Using Appendix D's caseload, Appendix E's per-case time costs, and your Step 2 national DMP, compute total hours and the rate for each policy, then the percent margin between them. Getting Policy A's total hours right takes two explicit multiplication steps before you add anything up:
    1. How many of this year's hits are partial-profile hits? partial-profile hit count = (Appendix D's 60 cases this year) × (Appendix D's 60% partial-profile share).
    2. Of those, how many are expected to be false (coincidental) matches? expected false referrals = (that partial-profile hit count) × (your Step 2 national DMP — the probability any one partial-profile hit is a coincidence). This is a fractional expected value, not rounded to a whole case count, because it's an average over many possible cases, not a prediction about any one of them.
    Then: Policy A total hours = (60 cases × 6 base-review hours) + (expected false referrals × 200 rework hours). Policy B total hours = (60 cases × 6 base-review hours) + (60 cases × 3 corroboration hours) — Policy B has no rework term because its corroboration step is what catches a coincidental match before it's referred.

    Step 4 — Combination vs. permutation, 10 of 20 loci (D4)

    CODIS's core panel has 20 loci (Appendix A). Calculate how many different 10-locus subsets could be drawn (order doesn't matter — a combination), and separately how many ordered selections of 10 loci from 20 are possible (order matters — a permutation).

    Step 5 — Written explanation (D5)

    Using Population, Sample, and Convenience Sampling, explain in a short paragraph why the database Dana searches (built only from people previously arrested or convicted) is not a random sample of the general population, and whether — and how — that distinction should change how confident anyone is in a "cold hit" by itself.     Reveal the Real-World Basis

    Dana Osei and her lab are fictional. The numbers problem she's facing is not — it happened, in a real courtroom, with a real conviction on the line.

    In 2008, John Puckett was convicted of a 1972 California murder on a DNA "cold hit" produced by searching the state's database of 338,711 offender profiles. The prosecutor told the jury the match had a 1-in-1.1-million chance of being coincidental — the raw per-profile random match probability. What the jury was never told: once you account for the fact that the match came from searching 338,711 people rather than comparing one suspect to one profile, the odds of a coincidental match turning up somewhere in that search were closer to 1 in 3. That's the exact database-size correction this case makes you compute in D2 — it's not a classroom simplification. It's Recommendation 5.1 of the National Research Council's 1996 DNA forensic science report (the same S6 source behind this case's Appendix C formula), left out of a real trial.

    Puckett's conviction was upheld on appeal. The case remains a standard reference point in the ongoing legal and statistical debate over how — and whether — cold-hit probabilities should be explained to a jury. The math you just did isn't hypothetical: a jury got the 1-in-1.1-million number and not the 1-in-3 number, and it mattered.
