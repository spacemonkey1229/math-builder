Overview

The map is still warm from the printer when Five slaps both palms on the table. "Ah, there she is — welcome, Four." He spreads his arms like he's introducing a headliner. "Good. Now that we're all here, let's get down to business."

One doesn't look up from the laptop balanced on his knee, three fingers moving fast across the trackpad. Two leans against the wall by the door, arms crossed, already dressed for driving — gloves on, jacket zipped, the look of someone who has sat in a lot of parked cars waiting for other people to finish talking.

"You're all here because you're the best of the best," Five says. "This score sets us up for life. Small city. Limited security. Lots of exits." He taps the map. "We should be in and out fast."

Every eye in the room turns to Four.

She unrolls a second map over the first — this one hers, covered in her own handwriting, two routes traced in different colors from the same starting point, each one ending at a different door.

"Four," Five says. "What's the plan?"
She doesn't answer right away. Her pen hovers between the two colored lines.

Background

For over four years, the Gang Of Five has eluded every authority chasing them across the Western United States, pulling off more than $3 million in heists without ever injuring a soul, always in broad daylight, always on a weekday, never leaving a trace. Despite the name, the crew is four people — three is considered unlucky in the trade, so nobody claims the number. Each member goes by a number instead of a name:

One — Hedley "Three Fingers," a hacker savant despite having only three fingers on his left hand.
Two — "Two Face" Randy, burned in a childhood fire and rebuilt with a partial face transplant; an expert safecracker and, as a part-time race car driver, the crew's getaway wheelman.
Four — Felicity "The Viper," the gang's master schemer. She plans every heist and every getaway.
Five — leader of the crew, an expert fence with contacts across the globe. No one, not even his own crew, knows his real name.

This time the target is the real Zions Bank Idaho headquarters, at 800 W Main St in downtown Boise, Idaho — a building that also houses a server farm holding the bank's client logins, passwords, and tens of millions of dollars in cryptocurrency (see Appendix A).

The clock is unforgiving. The gang has 9 minutes total inside the building before the vault's own alarm silently signals police, and that signal fires the instant the hard drives are pulled from the server racks — which Four has scheduled for the 8-minute-30-second mark. That leaves exactly 30 seconds to get out of the building and reach the getaway car before the police can plausibly arrive. Four has scouted two ways out: a street-level exit and an underground parking-garage exit, and she has already calculated, for each one, how long it takes to reach the car and the odds the crew clears the building without being made (see Appendix A). Neither route is instant, and neither is free of risk — that tradeoff is the whole decision in front of her.

Getting out of the building is only the first half of the problem. Security cameras on nearby buildings will almost certainly let police identify the crew's car — a white 2018 Ford Focus — within minutes of the alarm going off, and once that happens, a police helicopter will be in the air hunting for it. The plan has always been the same: drive the Focus from Zions Bank to a parking lot at Heroes Academy, a real school on Boise's northwest side, and swap into a second, clean vehicle before driving out of state (see Appendix A). Every mile spent in the Focus before that swap is a mile of exposure — and Four has her own detailed model, built from years in the trade, of exactly what raises and lowers that exposure along the way: the kind of road, how many stoplights and stop signs, how many minutes the crew spends in the car, and how close the route passes to an actual police facility (see Appendix B and Appendix C). None of these figures are pulled from a public crime database — nobody publishes "odds of getting caught per stoplight" — so Four's whole risk model is disclosed openly as her own professional judgment call, not presented as if it were externally verified (see Appendix C). Every real, verifiable fact this case relies on — the building's address, the school's address, the real route, and the real police facility location — is documented with its source (see Appendix D).

Whichever exit the crew takes, they take the same car to the same swap lot afterward — so the getaway drive itself doesn't change with the choice. What changes is only how the crew leaves the building, and by how much that changes their overall odds of getting all the way to Heroes Academy clean. The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in Appendix E — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — The route table (D1)

    Enter the three road-type segment distances from Appendix A, then the route's total drive time.

    Step 2 — Window check and average speed (D2)

    Four has a 30-second post-alarm window. Check each option's leftover slack, then find the drive's average speed from Step 1's totals.

    Step 3 — Compound probability, each option (D3)

    Convert every Appendix C risk factor into a survival probability, multiply them into one combined route-survival percentage, then multiply that by each option's own building-exit chance. Show your intermediate numbers in your pitch — this box only checks your final figures.

    Step 4 — Police-proximity check (D4)

    Appendix C's MA6 penalizes routes that pass within 1 mile (or ½ mile) of a police station. Appendix B gives the real distance from Zions Bank to Boise PD headquarters — but that's a straight-line "as the crow flies" fact about the bank, not necessarily about the getaway route itself. Reason it out using the two different road corridors named in Appendix A and B, then note your conclusion below.

    Step 5 — Two original visual displays (D5)

    This case's data workbook has the raw Appendix A/C figures ready to drop into a spreadsheet chart tool. Build (1) a bar graph comparing your own Option A and Option B final percentages from Step 3, and (2) a pie chart or histogram showing which risk factor from Appendix C removes the largest share of the route-survival probability. This notebook doesn't auto-grade a chart — that part goes in your pitch appendix.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    On your way out of the building, you see a meter maid ticketing your Ford Focus — the parking meter never got paid. There's no time to argue and no time to wait: you have to abandon the car. Two wants the corvette in the next row. Five says take the minivan instead. Everyone looks at you. Make the call, Four.

    Whichever vehicle you pick, stealing it and hot-wiring it without keys costs real time before you're back on the road — time that has to come out of your getaway window somewhere. There are no case-provided numbers for the corvette-vs-minivan choice itself; that part is a judgment call under missing information, not a hidden calculation.
