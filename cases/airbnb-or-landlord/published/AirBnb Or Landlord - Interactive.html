Overview

“Congratulations, you two — we’re scheduled to close in thirty days!” The realtor’s voice crackles through the phone, then goes quiet. Wendy Dalton lowers the phone from her ear and looks across the kitchen at Dwayne, who already has a roll of packing tape in one hand and a half-flattened box in the other.

Thirty days. That’s a paint touch-up, a dumpster rental, six years of school photos to sort through — and, somewhere in there, a decision about the house they’re about to leave behind.

Dwayne sets the tape down and turns his laptop around so Wendy can see it. Two browser tabs sit open side by side. One is a host dashboard, all red and green calendar squares and a nightly rate ticking in the corner. The other is a single plain number, repeated twelve times down a spreadsheet column.

“So which one are we actually doing?” Wendy asks, leaning over his shoulder. Down the hall, one of the kids drags a moving box across the hardwood like a sled, and nobody tells them to stop.

Dwayne’s cursor hovers between the two tabs, waiting for one of them to make the choice obvious.

It doesn’t.

Background

Dwayne and Wendy Dalton bought their current home years ago and have kept it up ever since — new appliances, a new water heater, a new roof. It’s fully remodeled and furnished, eight minutes from the airport, and close to shopping, restaurants, and a movie theater. Now that the family is moving into a bigger home nearby, the Daltons have already decided the old house becomes an income property — they are not selling it. They intend to rent it out for at least the next ten years. What they haven’t decided is how (see Appendix A for the home’s full specs and costs).

Option 1 — Airbnb. Boise’s short-term rental market is real but still developing compared to larger metro areas (see Appendix B). Cleaning happens after every single stay, and the Daltons plan to do it themselves: about two hours per turnover, with real cleaning materials costing $25 per cleaning. They plan to charge guests a separate $65 cleaning fee on top of the nightly rate for each stay.

Option 2 — Traditional rental. Boise’s long-term rental market is in high demand (see Appendix C). A traditional rental means signing one renter to a 1- or 2-year lease: no nightly vacancy risk, and — because the home comes fully furnished — the Daltons can charge a real market premium over an unfurnished comparable (see Appendix D).

Whichever option the Daltons choose, the house itself costs the same to hold either way: a $1,092 monthly mortgage payment and $490 per year in additional maintenance regardless of who’s renting it or how (see Appendix A). Utilities are the one cost that splits by option — Airbnb guests expect utilities included in the nightly rate, while a traditional 12-month tenant pays their own utility bills.

The move to the new house isn’t free, either. Starting next month, the Daltons take on a new second mortgage payment of $2,300 per month on top of everything else. Dwayne and Wendy both work full-time locally and together bring home $8,200 per month, against $4,000 per month in other household expenses. Whatever this rental property earns has to fit into that picture (see Appendix E). Every dollar figure in this case that isn’t a direct citation is a disclosed, stated Modeling Assumption (see Appendix F) — and every cited figure is sourced (see Appendix G).

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — D1: Airbnb annual profit breakdown

    Gross nightly revenue + cleaning-fee revenue, minus the host fee, cleaning materials, utilities, mortgage, and maintenance.

    Step 2 — D2: Traditional-rental annual profit breakdown

    Apply the furnished premium to the unfurnished baseline, times 12, minus mortgage and maintenance.

    Step 3 — D3: Monthly-equivalent comparison

    Convert D1/D2 to monthly figures, find the percent margin, and check the $2,300 second mortgage.

    Step 4 — D4: Two original visual displays

    Build a bar graph (Option A vs. Option B annual profit) and a pie chart (your chosen option’s revenue/cost breakdown) in a spreadsheet or on paper.

    Step 5 — D5: Monthly household cash-flow total

    Combined income minus other expenses minus the new second mortgage, plus your chosen option’s monthly profit.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    “Everyone’s Airbnb profit number so far only counts dollars. But the Daltons plan to clean the place themselves — no cleaning service, no line item for their own time. At 3 nights per average booking and 174 occupied nights a year, that’s a real number of bookings — and at 2 hours of cleaning per turnover, that adds up to a real number of hours of Dwayne and Wendy’s own unpaid labor every single year, spent turning over a house they don’t even live in anymore.”

    Recompute your own bookings-per-year and labor-hours-per-year using Appendix F’s stated stay length and the Background’s stated cleaning time. Then ask yourself: does a dollar total that ignores your own unpaid time still tell the whole story? What would Option A’s real profit look like if the Daltons instead paid someone else to do the cleaning?
