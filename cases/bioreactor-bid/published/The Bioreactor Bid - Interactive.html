Overview

The incubator room hums at 37 degrees, and Wei Zhang has been staring at the same two vendor folders for twenty minutes without opening either one. Through the observation window, the pilot suite sits dark — whichever vessel he recommends this week is the one that gets installed there, scaled up, and run for a client whose entire production budget for this program lives inside his line item. His manager's message is still open on his phone: procurement needs your number by end of day.

Wei turns first to the sales sheet for the tank he already knows, the one every operator on his team could run half-asleep — a straight steel cylinder, predictable, no surprises. Then he opens the second folder, the one with the glossy cutaway diagram of a flexible bag rocking on its cradle, its liquid surface curving and flattening with every tilt. He remembers the client's kickoff call, the words “we need density, not just volume.” He also remembers the number his director circled in red on the wave system's quote last quarter, the one that made her wince.

He pulls a legal pad toward him and uncaps a marker. Somewhere in these two folders is a vessel that actually earns its keep on this run — not the one that sounds better in the sales pitch, but the one that pencils out. He squares the two folders side by side on the bench, picks up the first spec sheet, and starts reading the fine print on curvature.

Background

Wei Zhang is a Bioprocess Engineer at a contract manufacturing organization (CRO) that scales up client cell-culture processes for production runs. He is responsible for recommending the production bioreactor for an upcoming batch and accountable to both his director and the client's program manager for defending that recommendation with numbers, not intuition (see Appendix E). Two vessel options are under consideration for this run:

The Column Bid

A standard cylindrical stirred-tank bioreactor (STR). Its geometry is simple and its working volume at any fill level is well characterized by the ordinary cylinder-volume formula, but its mixing efficiency caps the cell density the batch can reach (see Appendix B).

The Wave Bid

A wave/rocking bioreactor whose flexible bag forms a curved, non-cylindrical profile as it rocks and fills. Its rocking motion mixes more efficiently and supports a higher achievable cell density, but because the bag's cross-section is not a simple cylinder, its true working volume at the batch's target fill level must be found by treating the filled bag as a solid of revolution generated by its cross-sectional profile, not by assuming the vessel's full nominal capacity is usable (see Appendix C).

Both vessels would run the same client cell line under the same feed protocol. The client's process team has supplied standard growth-kinetics data describing how fast nutrient is fed into the batch and how fast the culture takes it up over the run (see Appendix A) — the batch's net cumulative yield is the surplus that accumulates between those two rates over the full run.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which vessel to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 · Net cumulative yield (D1)

    Find the area between Appendix A's feed-rate and uptake-rate curves (day 0–5), then apply the 0.05 L/g conversion factor. Report liters of viable product per liter of working volume.

    A trapezoidal sum of (feed − uptake) across the six day-marks works well here — this is a Riemann-sum-style approximation of the area between two curves.

    Step 2 · Working volume for each vessel (D2)

    Column Bid: apply the cylinder-volume formula to Appendix B's dimensions at the 75% fill level. Wave Bid: apply the disk method to Appendix C's r(h) profile — integrate πr(h)² over its full height range.

    Step 3 · Total-yield table (D3)

    Multiply Step 1's per-liter net yield by each vessel's Step 2 working volume to get total liters of viable product.
