Overview

Harlan Voss reads the same line of the spreadsheet for the third time, because the third time it still doesn't smooth out the way it's supposed to.

It's 6:50 on a Tuesday evening and the office is empty except for the hum of the server closet down the hall. Highline Life & Annuity files its new longevity-annuity reserve basis with the state insurance department in eleven days, and Harlan is the analyst whose initials go at the bottom of the actuarial memorandum. Next to a cold cup of coffee sits a printout with a single circled number: the reserve rate the model assigns to a policyholder turning exactly one hundred years old this filing year.

Two spreadsheets are open side by side. The left one holds the formula his mentor taught him three years ago — a clean, smooth curve that grinds through every attained age in seconds and has never once caused a filing to bounce. The right one holds the actual published mortality table, age by age, exactly as printed, with a jump near the top that the smooth formula quietly glides past.

Both spreadsheets are supposed to produce the same thing: the dollars of reserve required per thousand dollars of promised benefit. They don't agree — not by a little, and not everywhere, but very sharply in exactly one place.

Harlan minimizes his email, opens a blank sheet of scratch paper, and writes both formulas at the top of the page. He has eleven days to decide which one goes in the filing.

Background

Harlan Voss is a senior actuarial analyst at Highline Life & Annuity Company, a regional insurer. He is one of two analysts responsible for the reserve basis behind Highline's newest product, the Horizon Series — a single-premium immediate annuity that pays monthly income for as long as the policyholder lives, including the small but real number of policyholders who live past age 100. Harlan's manager signs the filing, but the supporting math carries Harlan's initials, and it is Harlan who has to explain any of it if a regulator's own actuary asks a question during review.

Highline must submit its statutory reserve basis for the Horizon Series to the state insurance department in 11 days (see Appendix A). The basis is a single, filed methodology — Highline cannot submit two competing calculations and let the regulator pick one, and it cannot swap methods later without a full refiling.

Two methods are on the table.

The Smooth Curve. Highline's actuaries have a closed-form formula they have used for years: one smooth equation that estimates the dollar reserve required per $1,000 of promised benefit at any attained age, built to track the real published mortality data closely and to keep climbing gradually toward its long-run ceiling as age increases (see Appendix B). It is one line in a spreadsheet, it has never triggered a regulatory objection on any other Highline product, and it never has a break in it — it is a single smooth curve for every age from 0 to 120.

The Full Table. The alternative is to price every attained age directly off the actual published mortality table, exactly as printed — including the table's own closing convention at its oldest recorded age, where every survivor still living is treated as certain to die within that final interval (see Appendix C). It is slower to compute by hand, and it produces a number that jumps sharply at that boundary — which happens to be the exact age band where Highline's own block of Horizon Series contracts still has real survivors on the books this filing year (see Appendix D).

Both methods are legitimate starting points; nothing in Highline's filing rules requires one over the other. What differs is how each one treats a single attained age, and how much of Highline's in-force block sits exactly there. Background on both the industry's standard mortality tables and the mathematical model behind “smooth toward an asymptote” is in Appendix E. Neither the Smooth Curve's exact formula nor the size of Highline's in-force block is drawn from a public source — both are stated Modeling Assumptions, disclosed in full in Appendix F. Every external source cited anywhere in this case is collected in Appendix G. The vocabulary and “how to…” skills this case asks you to research are listed in Appendix H — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

Appendix B — The Smooth Curve — Formula and Derivation

Highline's Smooth Curve formula estimates the dollars of reserve required per $1,000 of face value, CA(x), at attained age x:

CA(x) = 1000 − 851.8963 × (0.973722)(x−90)

Rounded to four and six decimal places respectively so the equation is easy to copy into a calculator; using these rounded coefficients reproduces every value in the table below to the penny.

How this formula was built (Appendix F, MA1): the formula is an exponential-approach-to-an-asymptote curve, calibrated so that it passes through the two real published data points at x=90 and x=99 from Appendix C, and set to approach a long-run asymptote of $1,000 per $1,000 of face value as x → ∞ — the same ultimate number the Full Table converges to eventually, since every insured individual eventually dies with certainty. The formula assumes that ultimate value is approached smoothly, rather than reached abruptly at any single age.

The last row is not a typo and it is not a rounding artifact — it is exactly how the source table defines it. The published table does not continue age-by-age past 99. Instead, everyone still alive at exact age 100 is grouped into a single open-ended interval, “100 and over,” and by the definition of the table, the probability that a person in that open interval eventually dies within that interval is exactly 1 — because there is no higher age band left for them to be counted in. This is a standard, real convention for closing out the top of a published life table, not something specific to this case [S1].

Appendix E — The Real Industry Table and the Real Asymptotic Model

Two real, published facts sit behind this case's dilemma.

The industry's own standard annuity mortality table has the same shape of problem. U.S. life insurers pricing annuities generally use the Society of Actuaries' 2012 Individual Annuity Mortality (2012 IAM) Basic Table — a published table covering ages 0 through 120 (SOA Table Identity 2581 for males, 2582 for females), publicly viewable at the SOA's own mortality table library [S3]. It was built by blending several different graduation methods across different age ranges rather than one formula for the whole table — which is exactly the kind of construction that can create a seam at the boundary between methods. This case uses the CDC's population life table (Appendix C) instead, because this case's author could not independently verify specific digit-level values from the SOA table's interactive lookup tool (see Appendix F, MA3).

The “smooth asymptotic” idea in the Smooth Curve formula is a real, named actuarial and demographic technique. When statisticians need to model mortality at the very oldest ages — where raw death counts get too small to trust — the standard method is the Kannisto model, a logistic-curve formula for the force of mortality built to approach a fixed asymptote smoothly as age increases. It is the method the Human Mortality Database itself uses to close out life tables at the oldest ages, fit using data from age 80 onward, with smoothed rates applied starting from an age the database sets per-population (constrained to fall between 80 and 95) [S4]. Highline's Smooth Curve formula in Appendix B is this same style of model, applied here as a stated, illustrative version rather than a full statistical fit (see Appendix F, MA1).

The Society of Actuaries has also published real methodology guidance specifically on annuity reserving practice: the “2012 Individual Annuity Reserving Report & Table” (SOA Payout Annuity Table Team, September 28, 2011), prepared at the request of the NAIC's Life Actuarial Task Force to guide how the 2012 IAM table should be used for statutory annuity reserves [S2]. It establishes that the choice of how to apply a mortality table to reserving — not just which table — is a real, filed, professional decision.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    Go back to Step 5. You found a portfolio-wide blended margin between the two methods — and on its own, a blended margin this small is easy to read as “these two methods barely disagree.”

    Now go back to Step 4. That single-cohort comparison at exactly age 100 told a very different story — a much larger relative gap, at exactly the one age where Highline has real survivors on the books this year.

    Ask yourself: does a small number in aggregate tell the whole story, when nearly all of the real dollar exposure might be sitting in one narrow slice of the portfolio? Statutory reserve adequacy is generally checked cohort by cohort, not only in aggregate — a company can be technically deficient at one attained age even while its overall block reserve looks comfortable.
