# Guide Notes: House Plans

**Case:** House Plans · **Tier:** T2 · **Band:** MS · **Studio:** Heroes Academy
**Standards:** see `case.json.standards[]` for the exact ids (8 skills — area, decimal
multiplication/subtraction, percent-of-a-whole, and comparison)

Legacy conversion, 2026-08-01, reformatted from the original 2020 Guide Notes
(`source/Y1/Session 2/House Plans/Guide Notes.docx`) onto the current 11-anchor ladder. The
original had only one matched anchor heading and no twist recorded; this version builds a full
ladder and adds a real, computable twist (a stated lumber-cost increase).

## Intro

Frame the format: this is a real family budgeting decision with a real bank ceiling behind it, not
a worksheet. Name the opener — "Two floor plans, one loan, and a family that has to live with
whichever number they get wrong."

## Launch

Show both floor plans' total square footage with no cost attached yet — 2,912 vs. 4,494. Ask:
"Just from the size difference, which one do you think ends up more expensive, and by how much?"
Do not confirm or correct answers here.

## Anchor 1 — Take a stand and vote

**TAKE A STAND AND VOTE. No reasoning yet, just commit.**

"Who builds the Farmhouse? Who builds the Ranch Estate?" Show of hands, no discussion. Record the
count — Close will ask them to vote again.

## Anchor 2 — What is the problem we're solving?

**"Is this the right question?" · "Is $495,000 actually the number we're working with?"**

Let confusion grow here, deliberately. Push on Appendix C's loan condition: "If the loan is only
90% of the total cost, is $495,000 really the ceiling, or is it something else?" If the room
realizes on its own that they need to find a bigger number before they can compare anything, that's
the case working as designed.

## Anchor 3 — What is the process?

**"If we go through this process and find the data, how will we decide?"**

Once the room has the true ceiling, ask them to name the actual steps: find each plan's most
upgraded affordable tier, spend what's left on amenities, compare the two final totals against the
same ceiling. Re-ask: "Do we agree on that process?"

## Anchor 4 — What don't we know?

**"Do we need to make assumptions? Which ones are critical?"**

Surface Appendix C's two real conventions: the 90%-loan condition sets the true ceiling, and every
dollar (build and amenities alike) counts against the same number. Ask what happens to the
comparison if either assumption were wrong.

## Data Deep Dive

Now, and only now, the numbers. Walk D2's ceiling calculation and D3's tier-affordability check
together as a room, cold-calling for the "find the whole" step specifically — this is where a Hero
who treated $495,000 as the real ceiling will get every downstream number wrong.

## Twist — drop this at ~15 minutes into the discussion

Deliver slowly, with pauses:

"The contractor calls. Lumber prices have jumped 6% since the quote was written — the build cost
for whichever plan you picked just went up."

**Facilitator-only: what the numbers actually do (`case.json.derived`).** The Farmhouse's Luxury
build rises from $432,635.84 to $458,593.99 (+$25,958.15); the Ranch's Standard build rises from
$454,792.80 to $482,080.37 (+$27,287.57). **The twist does not change which tier either house can
afford — both still fit under the $550,000 ceiling — but it meaningfully shrinks the amenity budget
for both.** A Hero who picked amenities right up to the old limit now has to cut something. Ask:
"What's the first amenity you'd drop, and why that one and not another?"

Do not tell a Hero which amenity is "correct" to cut — that's a real values question (a pool vs. a
sport court vs. landscaping), not a math answer.

## Immediate recommit

Re-vote immediately. Ask by name: "Did the 6% increase change your recommendation, or just your
amenity list?"

## Deep conflict — three threads (20-30 min)

1. **Space vs. finish.** The Ranch gives 54% more living space at a lower finish tier; the
   Farmhouse gives less space but the best finishes money can buy under the ceiling. Is there a
   principled way to weigh square footage against finish quality, or is it purely a family's own
   taste?
2. **The 90% rule.** Why would a lender require a family to cover 10% themselves instead of loaning
   the full amount? What risk is the bank actually managing?
3. **What the twist really tested.** If lumber prices can move 6% before the family even breaks
   ground, how much of this whole budget exercise is actually solid, and how much is a best guess
   the family should build a cushion around?

| 10:00–15:00 | Pressure-test the models — *"Is $495,000 really the number you're building to?"* |

## The trap

A Hero treats $495,000 as the real ceiling and never finds the true $550,000 figure — every
downstream number (affordable tier, amenity budget) is then wrong in the same direction. The case's
real first step is arithmetic, not opinion: find the actual number before arguing about it. Watch
for a pitch that never mentions the 90% condition at all.

## Socratic questions

1. The Ranch Estate is 54% bigger than the Farmhouse but only affords the Standard tier — is "cost per square foot of quality" a fair way to compare two genuinely different-sized houses?
2. Both floor plans land within 0.11% of the same $550,000 ceiling once optimized — is that closeness a coincidence, or does picking amenities to fill a budget almost always end up close to full, no matter the house?
3. If the lender's 90% condition were 80% instead, how would that change the true ceiling — and would it change which tier either house can afford?
4. You had to choose which amenity to drop after the twist — what's your reasoning, and would a different family reasonably choose differently?
5. If lumber prices could move 6% before ground is even broken, should the family build in a cushion below the $550,000 ceiling on purpose? How much?

## Close

**VOTE AGAIN.** "Did we ask the right question at the start?" "What math was new to you tonight, and
how did you actually attack it?" Compare the closing vote to Anchor 1's — a room that didn't move at
all after the twist is worth asking about directly: did the lumber increase not feel real, or did
the room already expect some kind of cost creep?
