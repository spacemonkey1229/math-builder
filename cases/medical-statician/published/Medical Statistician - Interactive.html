Overview

The boardroom smells like dry-erase marker and the coffee nobody's finished. Whiteboards line two walls, still covered in last quarter's charts. Tanya Teeples sits with her laptop open and a fresh legal pad, pen uncapped, at the foot of a table too long for the two people using it.

At the head of it, James Miller laces his fingers together and doesn't smile. “We have a new drug in the pipeline. Paxovir — a treatment for diabetes in dogs. I want you leading the study that proves it works.”

Tanya nods once, already turning half a sentence over in her head before she says it out loud. “We'll need a controlled trial. A real one — a group that gets Paxovir, a group that doesn't, and numbers at the end that mean something.”

“This is the flagship product, Tanya.” Miller leans forward. “The FDA will take this apart line by line. I need this bulletproof. Where do you even start finding dogs for a study like this?”

That's the question she was already circling. There are two honest answers, and they don't lead to the same place. One means reaching into the full, messy population of American dog owners — most of whom have no idea their dog could be part of anything. The other means picking up the phone to clinics that already have exactly the patients she needs, sitting in exam rooms right now.

Miller is still watching her, waiting. Behind him, a whiteboard chart from some other project shows a bell curve nobody's erased.

Tanya looks down at the blank first line of her legal pad.

Background

Tanya Teeples has worked as a medical statistician at Bansky Pharmaceuticals for over eight years, after three years freelancing as a medical statistician for private companies and government agencies. She holds a master's degree in Healthcare Statistics from USC. At Bansky, she designs the drug trials that determine whether a treatment Bansky's scientists have developed is effective enough to bring to market — and whether the U.S. Food and Drug Administration's Center for Veterinary Medicine (FDA CVM) will approve it for real patients (SEE APPENDIX F).

Paxovir is a new weekly-injection drug Bansky scientists designed to treat dogs with diabetes mellitus. Diabetes is a real and serious disease in dogs: it is estimated to affect about 1 in 300 dogs in the U.S., typically appearing between 4 and 14 years of age, with unspayed female dogs about twice as likely as males to develop it, and several breeds — including Cocker Spaniels, Dachshunds, Doberman Pinschers, German Shepherds, Pomeranians, Terriers, Toy Poodles, Miniature Schnauzers, Keeshonden, and Samoyeds — showing elevated risk (SEE APPENDIX B). Paxovir has already passed safety testing in laboratory rats and a small (100-dog) pilot sample, though a few pilot dogs saw no benefit, a few got worse, and a few died — expected in early-phase testing, but a reminder of what a badly designed larger trial risks (SEE APPENDIX A).

Quick facts
  • Fixed budget: $600,000.00 — $23,000 design, $84,000 analysis, $493,000 left for recruitment + dosing
  • Must launch within 1 month; conclude within 7 months
  • Drug cost: $15.00/dose/week × 26 weeks = $390.00 per subject

Tanya's central decision is how to build her sample. She could draw dogs at random from the full population of pet dogs in the U.S. — a method that produces the strongest, most generalizable evidence, but is expensive and slow, because most dogs contacted at random will not have diabetes at all (SEE APPENDIX B, D). Or she could recruit entirely through veterinary clinics Bansky already partners with, which are already treating at-risk-breed dogs and can hand her a list of diagnosed patients immediately — fast and cheap, but a sample that is not random, and federal regulators weigh non-random evidence differently than they weigh a randomized study (SEE APPENDIX F). Every dollar in this case that is not a direct citation to a real source is a disclosed, stated Modeling Assumption (SEE APPENDIX G) — none is invented quietly, and every citation is listed in full (SEE APPENDIX H).

Appendix C — Sampling Method Reference

Probability sampling (every member of the population has a known, nonzero chance of selection):

MethodDescription
Simple random samplingEvery individual has an equal, independent chance of selection — e.g., a computer-generated random draw from a national registry
Systematic samplingSelect every k-th individual from an ordered list after a random start
Stratified samplingDivide the population into subgroups (e.g., by breed or region), then randomly sample within each
Cluster samplingDivide the population into naturally occurring groups (e.g., by clinic or zip code), then randomly select whole clusters

Non-probability sampling (selection is not random; some members of the population have no chance of being chosen):

MethodDescription
Convenience samplingSelect whoever is easiest to reach — e.g., patients already at a partner clinic
Quota samplingFill fixed quotas for subgroups without random selection within them
Purposive samplingHand-pick individuals believed to be especially informative
Snowball (referral) samplingExisting subjects recruit further subjects from their own network

FindingValueSource

The wide spread above — a nearly 11-fold difference between the cheapest and most expensive site in the same real study — is itself the point: how you build a sample changes what it costs per subject, sometimes dramatically, even before considering how random the sample is.

Appendix E — Margin of Error: Formula and Calculator

At a 95% confidence level, the standard margin-of-error formula used for survey and sample-size problems is:

Margin of Error = z × √(p(1 − p) / n)

where z = 1.96 for 95% confidence [S6], p is the assumed proportion (use p = 0.5, the most conservative choice, when no other estimate is available), and n is the sample size.

An interactive calculator using this same formula is available at SurveyMonkey's Margin of Error Calculator [S6] — useful for checking your own hand calculation, but the formula above is reproduced here so the case does not depend on the link staying live.

Appendix F — FDA Regulatory Standard for Study Design

Before Paxovir can be sold to treat diabetic dogs, Bansky must submit a New Animal Drug Application (NADA) to the FDA's Center for Veterinary Medicine. Federal regulation requires “substantial evidence of effectiveness,” established through “adequate and well-controlled studies” (21 CFR § 514.117). The regulation's own stated purpose for those studies is to “distinguish the effect of the new animal drug from other influences, such as spontaneous change in the course of the disease... or biased observation,” and it specifically requires a design that permits “a valid comparison with one or more controls,” with subject assignment that “is random and minimizes bias” [S7].

Separately, FDA CVM has also published guidance (GFI #266, finalized October 2021) describing how the agency will evaluate real-world data — including registry and observational data, such as records drawn from a limited set of partner clinics — when it is submitted to help demonstrate effectiveness [S8]. Real-world data of that kind is not automatically disqualified, but the regulation's own definition of a well-controlled study is built around randomization minimizing bias — which is the real, cited reason Option A carries a regulatory advantage that Option B's dollar cost does not reflect.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    “Everyone arguing for Option A has been treating this as settled: 'non-random data just doesn't count for the FDA.' It's not that settled. In 2021, FDA's Center for Veterinary Medicine finalized real guidance — GFI #266 — that explicitly describes how the agency will evaluate real-world data, including registry and clinic-record data, when it's submitted to help demonstrate a drug works.”

    This does not flip the case to Option B being regulator-approved automatically — GFI #266 describes real-world data as something CVM will evaluate to help demonstrate effectiveness, not a replacement for the “adequate and well-controlled” randomized standard in 21 CFR 514.117 that Appendix F already quoted. What it breaks is the strong-form anti-B argument (“B can never work for FDA, so the cost savings don't matter”) — that position is now indefensible as stated. It does not make the reverse position (“so B is just as good”) defensible either. The twist narrows the gap between the two arguments; it does not close it.

    Good — re-check your D2 argument: does your write-up still claim Option B is automatically disqualified with the FDA, or does it now weigh the real-world-data pathway against the base randomization standard?
