# Case Study: The Fold Line

MS | Math | Case Study | 2026-27 | Adventure

---

## Overview

Priya Nakamura runs her thumbnail down the chalk line on the bolt of fabric and doesn't like what she sees. Behind her, the cutting room's twelve tables are already stacked with tomorrow's tickets, and the big rotary cutter at station three is still ticking as it cools. She's been staring at the same folded half-pattern for ten minutes, turning it over, laying it flat, folding it back.

Every bodice front the shop cuts starts the same way: a pattern piece, a bolt of fabric, and a choice her cutters make a hundred times a shift without thinking about it. Fold the fabric and trace one line, and the piece comes off symmetric, free — but the fold pins the piece in place, and there's no way to shift it to use the fabric better. Or open the fabric flat, trace the piece twice, flip the second copy, and nest the two mirror images together like puzzle pieces — tighter on the fabric, but twice the tracing, twice the cutting.

She's run the numbers on this before, in her head, on scraps, in the margins of yesterday's cutting tickets. She's never actually written them down side by side. The shop's margin is thin enough that this decision, repeated three hundred times a day, either pads it or eats it — and nobody has told her which.

Her supervisor is waiting by the door for her answer before the morning run starts. Priya picks up her tape measure and turns back to the table.

## Background

Priya Nakamura is the cutting-room lead at a small-batch garment manufacturer that runs twelve cutting tables on a single 44-inch bolt line. She signs off on the cutting plan every morning, and any fabric wasted beyond what a plan predicts comes directly out of the shop's own margin — there is no separate materials budget to absorb it.

The shop's highest-volume item is a fitted bodice front panel, cut fresh every day. Priya must choose, once, a standing rule for how every cutter on the floor cuts that panel from now on. Two methods are on the table:

**The Fold Cut** — fold the fabric in half along its length and lay the pattern's straight center-front edge exactly on the true fold. One tracing, one cutting pass through both layers, produces a complete, symmetric panel. Because the pattern's edge is locked to the fold, it cannot be rotated or shifted to avoid the curved waste at the armhole and side seam — a real, known industry effect called a marker's *efficiency*, the percentage of the fabric it uses that actually becomes garment rather than scrap (SEE APPENDIX D).

**The Open Cut** — lay the fabric flat, unfolded, and trace the *complete* panel as two separate mirror-image half-pieces. Because each half is a free piece, not locked to a fold, it can be flipped and nested edge-to-edge against its mirror, closing up more of the curved gaps that the Fold Cut leaves behind. It also means tracing and cutting the panel twice instead of once.

Every cutting decision has two costs, and they pull in opposite directions: the **fabric** consumed per panel (SEE APPENDIX A, APPENDIX B, and APPENDIX D) and the **cutting-room labor** spent marking and cutting it (SEE APPENDIX C). The shop's cutters are paid the same wage regardless of which method they use (SEE APPENDIX C), and the shop cuts the same 300 bodice fronts every day regardless of which method Priya picks. The panel's finished, sewn dimensions (SEE APPENDIX A) do not change between the two methods — only how much raw fabric and cutting time it takes to get there. None of the figures in this case are invented outright; every one is either cited to a real, live source or stated openly as a scenario assumption, with the basis for each assumption disclosed in **APPENDIX E**.

The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in **APPENDIX F** — that is where Challenge 1 sends you. Every sourced figure and stated assumption in this case is documented in **APPENDIX G**.

Priya's supervisor knocks on the door frame.

## Dilemma

You are Priya Nakamura.

Do you:

**A.)** Adopt the Fold Cut — one tracing and one cutting pass per panel, locked to the fabric's true fold **OR**

**B.)** Adopt the Open Cut — two tracings and two cutting passes per panel, free to nest against each other

## Appendix A — The Bodice Front Panel: Finished Dimensions

| Measurement | Value |
|---|---|
| Finished width, fold edge to side seam (bust level) | 8 3/8 in [MA1] |
| Finished length, shoulder to waist hem | 23 1/4 in [MA1] |
| Side/shoulder seam allowance (industry-standard) | 5/8 in [S1] |
| Waist hem allowance | 1 1/2 in [MA1] |

*The finished width and length above describe the sewn, worn panel — the same panel, sewn the same way, regardless of which cutting method produces it. Only the seam and hem allowances are added on top to get the panel's actual **cutting** dimensions, which is what Challenge D3 asks you to find. A 5/8-inch seam allowance is the near-universal U.S. home-sewing and light-manufacturing convention [S1] and applies at both the side seam and the shoulder seam; the case treats it as constant on every raw edge that needs one.*

*Note: the panel's real outline is curved at the armhole and side seam, not a rectangle. This case (and the marker-efficiency figures in Appendix D) treats the panel's **cutting bounding box** — the smallest rectangle the curved piece fits inside — as the fabric footprint the panel actually claims from the bolt. That simplification is standard in real marker-making arithmetic [S3] and is what makes Appendix D's efficiency percentages meaningful in the first place.*

## Appendix D — Marker Efficiency

| Cutting method | Marker efficiency | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Fold Cut | 78% [MA3] | The pattern's straight edge is locked to the true fold and cannot be rotated or flipped to close up the curved waste at the armhole/side seam. |
| Open Cut | 88% [MA3] | The two mirror-image half-pieces are free pieces and can be nested edge-to-edge against each other, closing most of that curved gap. |

**Marker efficiency** is a real, standard garment-industry measure: the percentage of the fabric drawn from the bolt that ends up as usable garment, versus scrap [S3]. It is defined as (total area of the pattern pieces) ÷ (total area of fabric the marker/layout actually uses) × 100 [S3]. A single, position-locked piece (like a piece pinned to a fold) typically runs in the 75–82% range; a piece free to be flipped and nested against a mirror copy typically runs in the 82–90% range [S6]. This case fixes one value from each real, cited range for the Fold Cut and the Open Cut respectively (MA3) — no public source publishes an efficiency figure specific to *this* panel shape at *this* bolt width, so citing a single exact number as fact would misstate what is actually known.

*To find how much fabric a panel actually requires from the bolt, divide the panel's cutting-bounding-box area (Appendix A, Challenge D3) by its method's efficiency, not the other way around — efficiency describes what fraction of the fabric drawn becomes panel, so the fabric drawn must be larger than the panel itself.*
