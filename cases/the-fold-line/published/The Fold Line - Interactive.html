Overview

Priya Nakamura runs her thumbnail down the chalk line on the boltA large roll of fabric as sold by the manufacturer, wound to a standard width. of fabric and doesn't like what she sees. Behind her, the cutting room's twelve tables are already stacked with tomorrow's tickets, and the big rotary cutter at station three is still ticking as it cools. She's been staring at the same folded half-pattern for ten minutes, turning it over, laying it flat, folding it back.

Every bodice front the shop cuts starts the same way: a pattern piece, a bolt of fabric, and a choice her cutters make a hundred times a shift without thinking about it. Fold the fabric and trace one line, and the piece comes off symmetric, free — but the fold pins the piece in place, and there's no way to shift it to use the fabric better. Or open the fabric flat, trace the piece twice, flip the second copy, and nest the two mirror images together like puzzle pieces — tighter on the fabric, but twice the tracing, twice the cutting.

She's run the numbers on this before, in her head, on scraps, in the margins of yesterday's cutting tickets. She's never actually written them down side by side. The shop's margin is thin enough that this decision, repeated three hundred times a day, either pads it or eats it — and nobody has told her which.

Her supervisor is waiting by the door for her answer before the morning run starts. Priya picks up her tape measure and turns back to the table.

Background

Priya Nakamura is the cutting-room lead at a small-batch garment manufacturer that runs twelve cutting tables on a single 44-inch bolt line. She signs off on the cutting plan every morning, and any fabric wasted beyond what a plan predicts comes directly out of the shop's own margin — there is no separate materials budget to absorb it.

The shop's highest-volume item is a fitted bodice front panel, cut fresh every day. Priya must choose, once, a standing rule for how every cutter on the floor cuts that panel from now on. Two methods are on the table:

The Fold Cut — fold the fabric in half along its length and lay the pattern's straight center-front edge exactly on the true fold. One tracing, one cutting pass through both layers, produces a complete, symmetric panel. Because the pattern's edge is locked to the fold, it cannot be rotated or shifted to avoid the curved waste at the armhole and side seam — a real, known industry effect called marker efficiencyThe percentage of fabric drawn from the bolt that actually becomes finished garment, rather than scrap., the percentage of the fabric it uses that actually becomes garment rather than scrap (see Appendix D).

The Open Cut — lay the fabric flat, unfolded, and trace the complete panel as two separate mirror-image half-pieces. Because each half is a free piece, not locked to a fold, a cutter can physically slide its paper pattern around on the flat fabric — the way you'd slide two puzzle pieces across a table until their edges touch — and nest it edge-to-edge against its mirror image, closing up more of the curved gaps that the Fold Cut leaves behind. It also means tracing and cutting the panel twice instead of once.

Every cutting decision has two costs, and they pull in opposite directions: the fabric consumed per panel (see Appendix A, Appendix B, and Appendix D) and the cutting-room labor spent marking and cutting it (see Appendix C). The shop's cutters are paid the same wage regardless of which method they use (see Appendix C), and the shop cuts the same 300 bodice fronts every day regardless of which method Priya picks. The panel's finished, sewn dimensions (see Appendix A) do not change between the two methods — only how much raw fabric and cutting time it takes to get there. None of the figures in this case are invented outright; every one is either cited to a real, live source or stated openly as a scenario assumption, with the basis for each assumption disclosed in Appendix E.

The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in Appendix F — that is where Challenge 1 sends you. Every sourced figure and stated assumption in this case is documented in Appendix G.

Side/shoulder seam allowanceThe extra fabric added beyond a garment's finished edge so two pieces can be sewn together without losing size. (industry-standard)5/8 in
Waist hem allowance1 1/2 in

The finished width and length describe the sewn, worn panel — the same panel, sewn the same way, regardless of which cutting method produces it. Only the seam and hem allowances are added on top to get the panel's actual cutting dimensions, which is what Challenge D3 asks you to find. A 5/8-inch seam allowance is the near-universal U.S. home-sewing and light-manufacturing convention and applies at both the side seam and the shoulder seam. Note: the panel's real outline is curved at the armhole and side seam, not a rectangle — picture tracing a curvy puzzle-piece shape on graph paper, then drawing the smallest possible straight-sided rectangle that still contains the whole curvy outline: that rectangle's area is what this case calls the cutting bounding box, and it treats that rectangle as the fabric footprint the panel actually claims from the bolt.

Appendix D — Marker Efficiency
Cutting methodMarker efficiencyWhat it means
Fold Cut78%The pattern's straight edge is locked to the true fold and cannot be rotated or flipped to close up the curved waste at the armhole/side seam.
Open Cut88%The two mirror-image half-pieces are free pieces and can be nested edge-to-edge against each other, closing most of that curved gap.

Marker efficiency is a real, standard garment-industry measure: the percentage of the fabric drawn from the bolt that ends up as usable garment, versus scrap. A "marker" is simply the traced layout — picture the chalk outlines of every pattern piece drawn directly onto a length of fabric before any cutting happens, arranged as tightly as the cutting method allows. Efficiency is defined as (total area of the pattern pieces) ÷ (total area of that traced-out layout) × 100. To find how much fabric a panel actually requires from the bolt, divide the panel's cutting-bounding-box area (Appendix A, Challenge D3) by its method's efficiency — efficiency describes what fraction of the fabric drawn becomes panel, so the fabric drawn must be larger than the panel itself.

    Step 1 — Cutting dimensions (mixed-number addition)

    Using Appendix A's finished width, finished length, seam allowance, and hem allowance, add the mixed numbers to find the panel's total cutting width and cutting length, in inches.

    Step 2 — Inches to yards

    Convert your Step 1 cutting length from inches to yards by multiplying by the fraction 1/36 (there are 36 inches in a yard).

    Step 3 — Cost per panel, each option

    Using the cutting bounding-box area, each method's marker efficiency (Appendix D) and the bolt width (Appendix B) to find fabric yardage, then price it (Appendix B) and add labor (minutes converted to hours, times the wage in Appendix C), find the total dollar cost per panel for each option.

    Step 4 — Daily-run projection

    Multiply each option's per-panel total from Step 3 by the shop's 300-panel day, then state the dollar gap between the two daily totals.
