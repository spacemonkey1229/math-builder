Case Study: The Special Order
MS | Math | Case Study | 2026 | Adventure

## Overview

Grace Liu holds the phone against her shoulder and doesn't hang up. On the shop tablet in front of her, the mill's order form is still open, cursor blinking in the quantity box. Behind her, the panel saw screams through another sheet of plywood, and somewhere past the dust curtain a nail gun fires twice. The movers are booked for the 14th. The client signed a fixed-bid contract. And the cut list taped above her bench calls for stile stock at a width the shelf downstairs doesn't carry — not close enough to fudge, not far enough to ignore.

Her lead carpenter leans in the doorway, sawdust in his eyebrows. "So — mill it ourselves off what's already sitting here, or you put in the special order?" He says it like it's obvious. It isn't. She's watched both ways go wrong before: a rush job where "we'll just plane it down" ate two days nobody had, and a special order that showed up perfect and three weeks late for a client who'd already called twice.

She flips the order form's units toggle out of habit — someone reset it to decimals again — and switches it back. Everyone on this crew reads a tape measure, not a calculator screen.

The cursor blinks. The saw screams. Her thumb hovers over the quantity field.

## Background

Grace Liu is the production manager at a five-person custom cabinet shop. She is responsible for keeping a fixed-bid kitchen job — twenty-four cabinet doors, on a client-facing install date the client has already scheduled movers around — inside its material budget and its schedule at the same time, and she personally signs off on every lumber order the shop places.

The cut list for this job's door frames calls for stile and rail stock at a **net finished width of 1-3/4 inch (7/4 inch)** (SEE APPENDIX A). Two ways exist to get there, and Grace names them the way her shop always has:

- **Standard Stock** — buy the hardwood the shop already stocks in standard nominal widths, and rip and joint it down to 1-3/4" in-house. It is on the shelf today, in whatever quantity she wants, with no minimum order. But turning a rough board into a clean 1-3/4" stile costs *real, fractional width* every single time: a straight edge has to be jointed off one side before it can be ripped safely, and the rip saw itself removes a kerf of material that becomes sawdust, not lumber (SEE APPENDIX B). Both allowances are stated in inches, as fractions, because that is how every spec sheet, blade box, and shop drawing in this trade actually states them — never as a decimal.
- **The Special Order** — the mill will pre-mill hardwood to the *exact* 1-3/4" width Grace needs, at a higher price per board foot, with none of Standard Stock's rip-and-joint waste. But the mill only runs a custom width on its own equipment in batches: an order below its stated minimum board footage is turned away outright, and even above that minimum, the lead time is long enough that Grace cannot place the order today and also make a change later (SEE APPENDIX C). Whether that minimum is worth committing to depends on how many doors' worth of stock she is ordering at once — the batch size the fixed-bid job actually needs. The minimum itself is not a published figure for this exact kind of order (SEE APPENDIX E).

A recurring headache on Grace's crew is that two rip-saw operators do not always write down the same offcut width the same way: one logs it as a fraction of eighths, the other simplifies it first, and the shop's own waste log has both forms sitting side by side for what is actually the identical measured width (SEE APPENDIX D). Before Grace can compare Standard Stock's true cost against The Special Order's, she has to be certain the waste figures she is comparing are actually equal widths and not two different ones that merely look different on paper.

Every fractional-width calculation in this case — the rough-width required per stile, the waste-log reconciliation, and the cost-per-door comparison — must be carried through in fraction and mixed-number form, matching how the shop's own cut sheets, blade specs, and mill order forms actually record these widths. The vocabulary and "how to…" procedures needed are listed in (SEE APPENDIX F). All priced and sourced figures are cited in (SEE APPENDIX G).

## Dilemma

You are Grace Liu.
Do you:
A.) Buy standard nominal hardwood stock today and rip/joint it down to width in-house — Standard Stock OR
B.) Place the mill's special order for pre-milled, exact-width stock — The Special Order

## Appendix A — Grace's Cut List (Door Stiles & Rails)

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D1). All widths are the shop's own net finished
specification for this job's cabinet doors — not an externally sourced figure.

| Component | Quantity needed (job total) | Net finished width | Length per piece |
|---|---|---|---|
| Door stiles | 48 (2 per door x 24 doors) | 1-3/4 in | 2 ft (24 in) |
| Door rails | 48 (2 per door x 24 doors) | 1-3/4 in | 2 ft (24 in) |
| Total stile/rail pieces | 96 | 1-3/4 in (7/4 in) | 2 ft (24 in) each |

Every piece in this job's door frames uses the identical 1-3/4" net width and 2 ft length — the
shop's own cut-list specification for this job, not an externally sourced figure. The case's
fraction arithmetic is deliberately built around one width used at scale, not many different
ones, so the comparison between sourcing options is apples-to-apples. Board footage per piece =
(width in inches x length in inches x thickness in inches) / 144, using a 1-inch nominal (4/4)
thickness for both options.

## Appendix D — The Rip Crew's Waste Log

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D3). Two rip-saw operators logged the offcut width
removed from the same batch of boards on the same afternoon. Both entries describe the jointing
allowance and kerf allowance from Appendix B — the point of this appendix is that they are the
*same* physical width, logged in two different fraction forms.

| Operator | Logged offcut width (as written in the shop log) |
|---|---|
| Operator 1 (measures in sixteenths, doesn't reduce before logging) | 6/16 in |
| Operator 2 (always simplifies before logging) | 3/8 in |
| Operator 1, a second board that afternoon (measures in sixteenths, doesn't reduce) | 5/16 in |

Two of these three entries describe the exact same physical offcut width; one describes a
genuinely different width from a board that was jointed less than the shop's usual allowance
(a real occurrence — not every board needs the full 1/4" taken off to true up its edge).
Challenge 3 (D3) asks which is which, and the arithmetic that proves it.
