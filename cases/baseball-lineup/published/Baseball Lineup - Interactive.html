Overview

Carly Steinbrenner watches an early spring practice through the open glass windows of her third-deck office behind third base. I love my job, she thinks, and for a moment she is eight years old again, playing catch with her father in this same outfield. A breeze moves the papers in her hand. The sound calls her back to work.

Her phone rings. "Hi Tony." She already knows what he's going to ask.

"Have you found my assistant yet?" says Tony DeLussa, the team's general manager.

"I have the final two applications in my hands right now," Carly says. "Any last input?"

"I know you'll figure it out. I trust you completely. But I need to know by the end of the day — Paul's replacement has to be ready to move fast."

Carly looks down at the two folders on her desk. Ash and Venicia are so evenly matched — same work ethic, same instincts, same three rounds of interviews — that she gave them one final, unscripted challenge that morning: build the best batting lineup you can from the team's spring roster. Their answers could not be more different.

She sets the folders side by side and opens the first one.

Background

Carly Steinbrenner is the Denver Dragons' Director of Personnel. She grew up around the organization — her father was a hitting coach here for over twenty years — and for three years she has advised the front office on every player and staff hire in the club. At only twenty-five, she is the youngest Director in the league, and her calls on personnel have been reliably good ones.

Tony's longtime executive assistant, Paul, is moving into a new role, and Carly must name his replacement today. Two finalists remain: Ash and Venicia. Both are outstanding, and side by side their résumés are nearly identical. Unable to separate them on paper, Carly gave both candidates the same challenge without warning: using the Dragons' spring training statistics, build the batting lineup you would put on the field (SEE APPENDIX A). Their two lineups are recorded in APPENDIX B.

Carly cannot hire both. She has to read what each lineup says about how Ash and Venicia think — and decide which way of thinking she wants running the front office with her.

The full spring training numbers for every player either candidate could have chosen are in APPENDIX A. The two lineups Ash and Venicia actually submitted are in APPENDIX B. APPENDIX C states exactly how this case defines an at-bat, a plate appearance, and every rounding rule — use it, or your numbers will not match anyone else's.

The vocabulary and the "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in Appendix E — that is where Challenge 1 sends you. Curious for more background beyond the required math? Appendix D is a short list of real outside reading and video links.

Appendix C — Modeling Assumptions

Assumption 1 — At-bats exclude walks. Real baseball convention: a walk is a plate appearance, but not an at-bat. At-bats (AB) = 1B + 2B + 3B + HR + Outs. Plate appearances (PA) = AB + Walks. Batting average is hits ÷ AB. The outcome-percentage breakdown (D2) uses PA, not AB — two different denominators for two different questions.

Assumption 2 — Rounding. Batting averages round to 3 decimal places; percentages round to the nearest tenth of a percent. A player's 6 outcome percentages may sum to slightly more or less than 100.0% because of rounding — expected, not an error.

Assumption 3 — Why order is set aside. The original Guide Notes ask whether batting order should matter to Carly's decision. D5 asks you to compute exactly how many distinct 9-player batting orders exist for a fixed lineup — that number is large enough on its own to explain why this case asks Carly to decide who to hire, not to also referee ordering.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you who to hire; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Batting average (D1)

    Pick any player from Appendix A. Compute hits ÷ at-bats, rounded to 3 decimal places.

    Step 2 — Plate-appearance outcome percentage (D2)

    Pick a player and one outcome. Compute that outcome's percentage of plate appearances (Appendix C, Assumption 1), to the nearest tenth of a percent.

    Step 3 — Lineup averages, two ways (D3)

    For each lineup: (i) combined average (total hits ÷ total at-bats across the 9 players) and (ii) the mean of the 9 players' individual averages.

    Step 4 — Power check (unlocks after the Twist)

    Locked. Reveal the Twist below and check "apply the twist" to unlock this step.
    Slugging percentage = total bases ÷ at-bats, where a single/double/triple/home run count as 1/2/3/4 bases. Compute each lineup's combined slugging percentage and total home runs.

    Step 5 — Batting orders (D5)

    Using the Fundamental Counting Principle, how many distinct batting orders are possible for a fixed 9-player lineup?
