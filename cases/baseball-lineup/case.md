## Background

Carly Steinbrenner is the Denver Dragons' Director of Personnel. She grew up around the organization — her father was a hitting coach here for over twenty years — and for three years she has advised the front office on every player and staff hire in the club. At only twenty-five, she is the youngest Director in the league, and her calls on personnel have been reliably good ones.

Tony's longtime executive assistant, Paul, is moving into a new role, and Carly must name his replacement today. Two finalists remain: **Ash** and **Venicia**. Both are outstanding, and side by side their résumés are nearly identical. Unable to separate them on paper, Carly gave both candidates the same challenge without warning: using the Dragons' spring training statistics, build the batting lineup you would put on the field (SEE APPENDIX A). Their two lineups are recorded in **APPENDIX B**.

Carly cannot hire both. She has to read what each lineup says about how Ash and Venicia think — and decide which way of thinking she wants running the front office with her.

The full spring training numbers for every player either candidate could have chosen are in **APPENDIX A**. The two lineups Ash and Venicia actually submitted are in **APPENDIX B**. **APPENDIX C** states exactly how this case defines an at-bat, a plate appearance, and every rounding rule — use it, or your numbers will not match anyone else's.

The vocabulary and the "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in **APPENDIX D** — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

## Appendix C: Modeling Assumptions

**Assumption 1 — At-bats exclude walks.** This case uses the real baseball convention: a walk is a plate appearance, but not an at-bat. **At-bats (AB) = 1B + 2B + 3B + HR + Outs.** **Plate appearances (PA) = AB + Walks.** Batting average is hits ÷ AB. The plate-appearance outcome breakdown in Challenge 3 (D2) uses PA as its denominator, not AB — these are two different, correct denominators for two different questions, and mixing them up is the single most common error in this case.

**Assumption 2 — Rounding.** Batting averages and slugging percentages are rounded to **three decimal places** (the real convention sportswriters use, e.g. `.514`). Percentages are rounded to the **nearest tenth of a percent**. Because of rounding, a player's six outcome percentages (D2) may sum to slightly more or less than 100.0% — that is expected, not an error, and you do not need to force it to sum exactly. Any "how much bigger" margin between two lineups' AVG or SLG (e.g. Ash's lead on combined average) is computed from the **already-rounded** three-decimal figures, not from the raw unrounded ratio — the two methods differ by about a tenth of a percentage point, and the rounded-figures version is the one this case's numbers use throughout.

**Assumption 3 — Why order is set aside.** The Guide Notes that inspired this case ask whether batting *order* — not just roster selection — should matter to Carly's decision. It's a fair question: Challenge 3 (D5) asks you to compute exactly how many distinct 9-player batting orders exist for a fixed lineup, and that number is large enough on its own to explain why this case asks Carly to decide *who* to hire based on *who* is in the lineup, not to also referee the ordering question inside each candidate's pick.
