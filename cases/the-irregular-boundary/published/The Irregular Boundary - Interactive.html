Overview

Renata Suh drags the last vertex into place and the polygon on her screen finally closes — a lopsided shape with a bite taken out of one corner and a notch pushed into another, laid over a satellite photo of dark, tilled soil. Her coffee's gone cold next to the mouse. Two other windows sit minimized along the bottom of the screen: one is the bank's boundary-certification form, blinking a due date at the top in red. The other is an email thread with a neighboring farm's manager, tone gone from friendly to clipped over the last three messages, about whose fence line a certain pivot's outer wheel track actually crosses.

She pulls up a second file — the same quarter-section, but drawn as a clean rectangle, corners left blank and unplanted, nowhere near the disputed strip. Two versions of the same field. Two different answers to the same question: where does the planted boundary actually go this year.

Her cursor hovers between the two file names. Outside the window, the same field waits under an overcast sky, corn stubble from last season still flattened in rows. Renata's manager wants the recommendation memo by end of day — the bank meeting is tomorrow morning. She minimizes the email thread, opens both boundary files side by side, and reaches for her tablet to start the write-up.

Background

Renata Suh is a GIS land-use analyst for a farm-management company operating corn and soybean quarter-sections across Nebraska's pivot-irrigation country. She finalizes the planted-boundary map her company files with the landowner and the landowner's lender each growing season — the map that sets how many acres of “certified planted ground” this field counts as, for both the lease and the loan collateral behind it.

This 160-acre quarter-section (the standard unit these Nebraska fields are surveyed and leased on) sits under a center-pivot irrigation system, and its lease is up for renewal this year. The landlord's bank wants a locked planted-acreage figure to set next year's cash rent before it approves the renewal. At the same time, a neighboring farm's center-pivot has, for several seasons, swung its outermost “tail” span across the disputed corner of this property's line (see Appendix B). Under Nebraska law, ten years of open, continuous, adverse use of another's land can ripen into a legal right to keep using it — this is the season Renata's company wants the boundary settled on paper.

Renata is weighing two ways to draw this year's planted boundary.

Option A — The Squared Block. A standard convex rectangular planted block, sized to the pivot's basic circle (see Appendix A), ceding the four corners of the quarter-section as unplanted ground. This block never touches the disputed strip the neighbor's pivot tail crosses.

Option B — The Reclaimed Line. An irregular, partly concave planted boundary that reclaims corner acreage beyond the pivot's basic circle and asserts the property line up to the disputed strip, forcing the boundary question with the neighbor this season.

Both boundaries sit on the same parcel, irrigated by the same fixed pivot equipment (see Appendix A); no new hardware is under consideration. What changes is the boundary's shape — which vertices are convex and which are reflex (concave), and its total perimeter — because shape drives two real costs: guidance-line/fencing cost for a longer perimeter (see Appendix C and Appendix D), and turning-margin crop loss a combine eats at every concave notch (see Appendix E). Lease and rental-rate stakes are in Appendix F.

Every hard number here is sourced with a citation or, absent a public figure, stated as a Modeling Assumption once research is complete (see Modeling Assumptions and Appendix G). One fact is settled now: the neighbor dispute above is fictional, built for this case, not a real property dispute.

  3. Include the following:
    • D1 — A labeled diagram of the Squared Block (Option A): a to-scale sketch of the convex rectangular planted boundary using the pivot dimensions in Appendix A, every vertex marked, every interior angle shown, the shape explicitly labeled as a quadrilateral.
    • D2 — A labeled diagram of the Reclaimed Line (Option B): a to-scale sketch of the irregular, partly concave planted boundary from Appendix B, every vertex marked, each interior angle classified as convex or reflex (concave), the full shape labeled as an irregular polygon.
    • D3 — A disputed-strip classification note: identify the shape of the neighbor's pivot-tail crossing strip using the dimensions in Appendix B, and mark on your D2 diagram exactly where the Reclaimed Line boundary does or does not touch it.
    • D4 — A usable-acreage and perimeter comparison table: total planted acres inside each option's boundary, and total perimeter (feet) of each boundary.
    • D5 — A cost-and-loss comparison table: the added guidance-line/fencing cost of the Reclaimed Line over the Squared Block, and the harvest turning-margin crop loss at each reflex vertex.
    • D6 — A recommendation, stated in dollars per acre, combining your D4 acreage gain and D5 added costs into the case's primary metric, and stating which boundary Renata should finalize before tomorrow's bank meeting.
  4. An appendix showing evidence of your original thinking — pictures, screenshots, notes, work. Doesn't count toward your word count.
  5. Participate twice or more in the case study discussion. If absent, post 500 "excellent" words on the questions above instead.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Classify the boundaries (D1 / D2 / D3)

    Goal: count the reflex (concave) vertices on the Reclaimed Line (Option B), and classify the disputed strip's shape.

    Step 2 — Acreage and perimeter (D4)

    Goal: using Appendix A and Appendix B's own dimensions, compute total planted acres and total perimeter for each option.

    Step 3 — Price the shape (D5)

    Goal: price the perimeter Option B adds over Option A, and the harvest turning-margin crop loss at every reflex vertex you found in Step 1.

    Step 4 — The recommendation (D6)

    Goal: combine your D4 acreage gain and D5 added costs into the case's primary metric — net dollars gained by choosing B over A — and state a recommendation.

    This step doesn't tell you which option is “right” — it only checks whether your own arithmetic is internally consistent with the case's own sourced and stated figures, using whichever fencing choice you selected in Step 3. Your recommendation is still yours to defend.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    Renata's own numbers depend on one more choice she hasn't made yet: which real fencing type her company specs for the Reclaimed Line. Appendix C gives two equally real, equally sourced options — woven-wire and high-tensile electrified — from the same Iowa State Extension document. They are not close to the same price per foot.

    This case's own recommendation has been built around the pricier of the two, woven-wire, as its primary scenario. That is a real, defensible choice a farm-management company might make given a live boundary dispute — not the only one. Go back to Step 3 of your Working Notebook, flip the fencing selector to high-tensile electrified, and re-run your own D5/D6 numbers.

    Does the fencing choice change who wins — or only by how much? What kind of company would plausibly choose the pricier option here, given the live boundary dispute?
