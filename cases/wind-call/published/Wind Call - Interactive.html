Overview

Renata Alvarez has four minutes before the dispatch release locks, and the printer is still warming up. Through the trailer window she can see the Caravan idling on the ramp, prop already turning, the cargo door still cranked open while the ramp agent finishes strapping the last pallet. Her headset crackles — the captain, asking for the release altitude so he can brief it before taxi. She doesn't answer yet.

Two number pads sit open on her second monitor, one behind the other, each one a completed wind triangleA simple diagram, drawn as three connected arrows, that pilots and dispatchers use to work out an aircraft's actual path over the ground: one arrow for the airplane's own speed and heading through the air, one for the wind, and a third -- their sum -- for the real path and speed over the ground. See the worked example in the Working Notebook.Open a reference on this → she built by hand herself twenty minutes ago rather than trust the scheduling software's canned estimate for a route this close to a decision. One triangle is for the low cruise. The other is for the high one. She already knows what the high option means operationally — the portable oxygen bottle has to come out of the hangar locker, get weighed, get logged, and the captain has to brief the crew on how to breathe through it before engine start, all inside a shrinking window. She built both triangles anyway, because "operationally simpler" and "numerically better" are not obviously the same flight today.

Her coffee has gone cold in the two hours she's been staring at these numbers. The captain's voice comes through again, quieter this time: "Renata, gate's closing. What have you got?"

She pulls both printouts toward her.

Background

Renata Alvarez is the flight dispatcherThe person legally responsible for the route, altitude, and fuel-load decisions on a flight release before it departs.Open a reference on this → of record for Highline Air Cargo, a Part 135The federal rulebook for commuter and on-demand charter/cargo operators, stricter than the rules for private flying.Open a reference on this → cargo and feeder operator flying Cessna 208B Grand Caravans. For every flight she releases, she is the person of record who signs the dispatch releaseThe single paperwork package that bundles the route, the cruise altitude, and exactly how much fuel to load for one flight.Open a reference on this → — and she is accountable if that fuel load turns out to be wrong.

Today's flight is a single leg from Denver Centennial Airport (KAPA) to Wichita Dwight D. Eisenhower National Airport (KICT) (SEE APPENDIX A). The aircraft, weather, and cargo load are fixed; the only open decision is the cruise altitude Renata files on the release, because the winds aloftWind speed and direction at cruising altitudes, forecast separately from surface winds because they can differ sharply. The government agency that publishes this forecast reissues a fresh version every six hours.Open a reference on this → — and therefore the fuel burned to complete the flight — differ by altitude (SEE APPENDIX C).

Two altitudes are under consideration, and Renata has nicknamed them the way the crew will hear them read back over the radio:

  • Niner-Thousand — cruise at 9,000 feet MSL"Mean sea level" -- altitudes in aviation are almost always measured as height above average sea level, not above the ground directly below.Open a reference on this →. Below 12,500 feet, no supplemental oxygenExtra breathing oxygen carried and used above altitudes where the air itself doesn't carry enough for the body to function normally.Open a reference on this → is required for the crew under federal regulation (SEE APPENDIX D).
  • One-Eight-Thousand — cruise at 18,000 feet MSL. Above 12,500 feet, federal regulation requires the flight crew to use supplemental oxygen for any part of the flight above that altitude lasting more than 30 minutes — and above 14,000 feet, oxygen is required regardless of duration (SEE APPENDIX D). The Caravan's cabin is unpressurized, so its cabin pressure altitudeThe altitude the air inside the cabin effectively feels like to a person breathing it. In a pressurized airliner this stays low even at high true altitude; here it simply equals the aircraft's real altitude.Open a reference on this → just equals its real altitude, so "above 12,500 feet" means the whole cruise segment.

The aircraft's true airspeedAn aircraft's speed through the air mass itself, before accounting for any wind -- different from groundspeed, its speed over the actual ground.Open a reference on this → and fuel-flow rate are fixed by its published performance figures and do not change with the altitude decision on their own (SEE APPENDIX B) — what changes the outcome is how the wind at each altitude combines, as a vector, with the aircraft's airspeed vector to produce the actual groundspeed the aircraft will fly. A higher groundspeed means less time in the air, and less time in the air means less fuel burned, for the exact same distance covered. Because the manufacturer does not publish a complete fuel-flow chart broken out by altitude, this case holds fuel-flow rate constant across both options as a stated, disclosed assumption (SEE APPENDIX E) rather than inventing a second, uncited number.

The dispatch release locks in four minutes, when the captain begins his pre-taxi checklist. After that, altitude cannot be changed without re-running the entire weight-and-balanceThe paperwork confirming the loaded aircraft's total weight and its balance point are both within safe limits for flight -- required fresh any time the fuel load or cargo changes.Open a reference on this → and fuel package from scratch, which would blow the delivery window for tonight's cargo.

Appendix B — Aircraft Performance: Cessna 208B Grand Caravan

"Representative cruise power" is the single throttle/propeller setting (1,750 rpm) the manufacturer's own fact sheet uses as its standard example cruise condition — the setting a pilot would normally fly at, not a maximum or minimum. This case treats the fuel-flow figure as constant across both altitude options (see Appendix E, MA1) — a stated simplification, not a separately published per-altitude figure. (Jet-A weighs about 6.7 pounds per gallon, which is where 415 lb/hr and 62.0 gal/hr come from — the same fuel, two common units.) Neither altitude in this case comes close to the 25,000 ft service ceiling or the 332-gallon fuel capacity above — both are listed for completeness, not because either is a binding limit here.

Appendix D — Regulatory Constraint: Supplemental Oxygen
14 CFR § 91.211(a)(2) — no person may operate a civil aircraft at cabin pressure altitudes above 12,500 feet (MSL) up to and including 14,000 feet (MSL) unless the required minimum flight crew is provided with and uses supplemental oxygen for that part of the flight at those altitudes of more than 30 minutes duration; and § 91.211(a)(3) — above 14,000 feet (MSL), the required minimum flight crew must be provided with and use supplemental oxygen during the entire flight time at those altitudes. [S5]

The Caravan's cabin is unpressurized, so cabin pressure altitude equals actual altitude. A cruise segment at 18,000 feet is entirely above 14,000 feet.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Wind components (dot product)

    For each altitude, find the headwind/tailwind component (along the 106.0° course) and the crosswind component of the wind vector.

    Step 2 — Resultant groundspeed and heading

    Use your Step 1 components and the Pythagorean/right-triangle relationship to find each altitude's resultant groundspeed.

    Step 3 — Flight time and fuel burned

    Convert distance and groundspeed into elapsed time, then time and fuel-flow rate into total fuel burned.

    Step 4 — Gallons per nautical mile

    Divide each altitude's total fuel by the route distance to get the primary metric, and compare.
