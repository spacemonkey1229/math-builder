Overview

Jaylen Ortiz has the recommendation slide open and still blank on a Sunday night, laptop propped against a stack of unopened mail, the kitchen table lit by one lamp because everyone else in the house is asleep. Two documents sit in adjacent tabs. One is a media plan so simple it fits on a single slide: one mention, one moment in the video, one call-to-action, running straight through the quarter. The other is a spreadsheet Jaylen has been rebuilding all weekend — a full test matrix, every placement timing crossed with every call-to-action style, color-coded cells stretching past the edge of the screen.

The brand's growth lead texted twice today, both times some version of we need real signal before the board meeting, not a guess dressed up as a plan. The media buyer, on a call this afternoon, said the opposite: every variant you add is budget you're taking away from the one that might actually work. Both of them are right, which is the problem.

Jaylen scrolls back to the top of the test matrix, counting the cells again, watching the number of distinct combinations climb faster than the fixed budget the finance team has already locked in for the quarter. Somewhere in that grid is either the most defensible test this brand has ever run, or a plan so thinly spread that none of it will mean anything by the time the data comes back.

The cursor blinks on the empty recommendation slide, and the coffee next to the laptop has gone cold.

Background

Jaylen Ortiz is the Social Media/Influencer Marketing Strategist for a mid-size direct-to-consumer brand, responsible for recommending how the brand's paid-placement budget for its next sponsored-video campaign should be spent, and accountable to the growth lead and finance team for defending that recommendation before next quarter's ad budget is allocated against it (see Appendix B). The brand pays a creator to produce one sponsored video per campaign cycle; the question in front of Jaylen is not what the video says, but how the brand tests which paid-placement approach around that video actually drives purchases.

The Straight Cut — a single fixed placement. The product mention airs at one fixed point in the sponsored video, paired with one call-to-action style, for the entire campaign window (Appendix B).
The Full Rotation — a full multi-variant test. The creator cuts three separate versions of the video, each moving the product mention to a different point — an Early Mention, a Mid Mention, and a Late Mention — and each cut runs behind two different call-to-action styles, an Urgency CTA and a Social-Proof CTA, for six distinct variants in total (Appendix A).

The two plans face the same hard limits. The creator delivers only one newly edited video cut per production week — a call-to-action style is a text overlay applied at ad-serving time and does not require a new edit, but each placement-timing cut does — so The Full Rotation's three cuts must be produced across whatever weeks remain in the test window before quarter-end (Appendix A). Both plans draw from the exact same fixed ad-spend budget the finance team has already locked in for the quarter; The Straight Cut spends all of it behind one variant, while The Full Rotation divides it across six (Appendix B). And every variant, in either plan, needs to clear the ad platform's own minimum recommended spend before its conversion numbers can be trusted as a real read rather than noise (Appendix C).

The Full Rotation is a genuinely bigger test — three placement timings and two call-to-action styles is real signal a single fixed placement can never produce. But splitting one fixed budget six ways instead of one way changes what each variant can actually prove.

Every sourced and assumed figure in this case is listed in Appendix E (Modeling Assumptions) and Appendix F (Sources); the vocabulary and “how to…” skills this case will ask you to research are listed in Appendix G — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

Challenges

  1. Research and find an online resource that answers one of the "how to…" questions from Appendix G. Post a link to the resource as your submission.
  2. Post 250+ "excellent" words pitching a firm stance on which plan you'd choose and why. Your pitch must use the resource from Challenge 1 and include at least one original visual display of the data.
  3. Your pitch must include:
    • D1 — A permutation calculation (Appendix A), shown with the formula and every step, for the number of distinct possible production-order sequences in which the three placement-timing video cuts could be produced across the production weeks remaining before quarter-end.
    • D2 — A budget-per-variant table (Appendix B) comparing The Straight Cut's single-variant budget to The Full Rotation's six-variant budget split, in dollars per variant, with the arithmetic shown.
    • D3 — A minimum-viable-spend clearance table (Appendix C) showing, for each plan, what percent of the ad platform's minimum recommended per-variant spend threshold each variant's budget actually represents.
    • D4 — A final recommendation table (Appendices B, C and D) reporting statistically attributable conversions per dollar of ad spend for The Straight Cut and for The Full Rotation, with your recommendation stated and defended using that number.
  4. An appendix showing evidence of your original thinking, work, calculations and processes you used to complete the challenges (pictures, screenshots, notes, etc.). Your work in the appendix does not count toward your word count.
  5. Participate twice or more in the Case Study discussion. If you are absent the day of a discussion post 500 "excellent" words on the questions above.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 · Production-Order Permutations (D1)

    Appendix A gives 5 production weeks remaining [MA1] and 3 placement-timing cuts that must each be produced in a distinct week, in some order. Use the permutation formula P(n,r) = n! / (n−r)! to find the number of distinct production-order sequences.

    Step 2 · Budget Per Variant (D2)

    Split Appendix B's total budget across each plan's number of variants. Show the arithmetic: total budget ÷ number of variants.

    Step 3 · Threshold Clearance (D3)

    Using Appendix C, find the derived minimum-viable spend per variant, then express each plan's per-variant spend as a percent of that threshold: (spend ÷ threshold) × 100.

    Step 4 · Conversions Per Dollar (D4)

    Using Appendix D's conversion rate and CPM, find impressions/dollar (1000 ÷ CPM), then conversions/dollar. Any variant that fails D3's clearance check contributes zero attributable conversions — exclude it, don't count it at face value. Report each plan's total attributable conversions per dollar of ad spend.

    The calculator checks your arithmetic against the case's own numbers — it does not tell you which plan to recommend. That comparison, and the recommendation it implies, is the pitch you're building in Challenge 2.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before opening this — in a live discussion, this section is addressed at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    This Case Ships Without a Twist

    Research for this case considered one natural candidate for a mid-discussion twist — "what if the budget shrinks after fees" — and rejected it for the same reason a couple of other cases in this series have: introducing a second, unsourced dollar figure here would either leak the tie's fragility early or require inventing a number this case's research does not turn up a defensible basis for.

    This case's dilemma is a one-shot budget-allocation recommendation with a hard deadline — the quarter's budget locks — and there is no real, sourceable fact this case's research turned up that could honestly be withheld from Background and then revealed mid-discussion without either fabricating a number or simply restating information you already have by the time you reach the Deep Conflict discussion.

    The pressure a twist would normally add is still here — it's in the "what if the numbers moved" pressure your facilitator's Anchor 4 discussion carries instead, once you've worked D4 yourself.
