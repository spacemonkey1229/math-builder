# Guide Notes: Phone Plan

**Case:** Phone Plan · **Tier:** T2 · **Band:** MS · **Studio:** Heroes Academy
**Standards:** see `case.json.standards[]` for the exact ids (10 skills — linear equations,
slope/y-intercept, systems of equations, percent, and comparison)

Legacy conversion, 2026-08-01, reformatted from the original 2021 Guide Notes
(`source/Y3/Session 3/Phone Plan/Guide Notes.docx`) onto the current 11-anchor ladder. The
original's twist idea (a free-upgrade promotion from the carrier not chosen) is kept, now with a
real, computed dollar value that genuinely changes the first-year math.

## Intro

Frame the format: this is a real household budgeting decision, not a worksheet problem. Name the
opener — "Two carriers, two prices, and a family that's about to add a third line whether they plan
their phone bill around it or not."

## Launch

Read Appendix A's pricing table aloud with no monthly totals computed yet. Ask: "Just from the
rates, which carrier do you think is cheaper right now? Which one do you think is cheaper later?"
Do not confirm or correct answers — the point is a gut read before anyone writes an equation.

## Anchor 1 — Take a stand and vote

**TAKE A STAND AND VOTE. No reasoning yet, just commit.**

"Who signs up for Carrier V? Who signs up for Carrier T?" Show of hands, no discussion. Record the
count — Close will ask them to vote again.

## Anchor 2 — What is the problem we're solving?

**"Is this the right question?" · "Is it just as simple as which carrier is cheaper, or is there
something else to consider?"**

Let confusion grow here, deliberately. Push on the family's own stated plan: "Is 'cheaper' a
fixed fact about a carrier, or does it depend on how many lines you're comparing at?" If the room
lands on "cheaper today and cheaper in a year might be different carriers" on its own, that's the
case working as designed.

## Anchor 3 — What is the process?

**"If we go through this process and find the data, how will we decide?"**

Once the room has a working definition of the tradeoff, ask them to name the actual steps: write
each carrier's cost as an equation, compute totals with tax at 2/3/4 lines, solve the system to
find where the lines cross. Re-ask: "Do we agree on that process?"

## Anchor 4 — What don't we know?

**"Do we need to make assumptions? Which ones are critical?"**

Surface Appendix B's two real conventions: the flat 8% tax, and the assumption that today's
additional-line rate holds when the 3rd/4th line is actually added a year from now — a real,
disclosed uncertainty neither carrier guarantees in writing.

## Data Deep Dive

Now, and only now, the numbers. Walk D1's two equations and D3's system-of-equations solution
together as a room, cold-calling for the slope/y-intercept identification specifically — this is
where a Hero who mixed up which number is the rate and which is the fixed cost will visibly
disagree with everyone else's equation.

## Twist — drop this at ~15 minutes into the discussion

Deliver slowly, with pauses:

"Fiona stops by the Carrier V store just to compare notes. The rep tells her: sign up this month
and get a one-time $150 device-upgrade credit. Carrier T isn't offering anything like it right now."

**Facilitator-only: what the numbers actually do (`case.json.derived`).** At the family's current
2-line need, Carrier T saves $10.80 a month over Carrier V — $129.60 over a full year. Carrier V's
one-time $150 credit is *larger* than that entire year of savings. **The twist flips the first-year
verdict**: a Hero who recommended Carrier T purely on monthly cost has to reckon with a real number
that says otherwise once the credit is priced in. This was designed to be genuinely close ($150 vs.
$129.60, not $150 vs. $50) — say so if a Hero notices how tight it is.

Do not tell a Hero which time horizon is the "right" one to optimize for — a family that might
switch carriers again in eight months should weigh this differently than one settling in for years.
That framing question is the point of the twist, not a script to hand over unprompted.

## Immediate recommit

Re-vote immediately. Ask by name: "Did the credit change your answer? If you recommended Carrier T
and didn't change your mind, what's your argument for ignoring $150?"

## Deep conflict — three threads (20-30 min)

1. **Monthly cost vs. one-time credit.** A recurring $10.80/month saving and a one-time $150 credit
   are different shapes of money. Is comparing them over exactly one year fair, or does the
   comparison depend on how long the family expects to stay with either carrier?
2. **Today's need vs. next year's plan.** The break-even point (3 lines) lands almost exactly when
   Charity is expected to get her phone. Should a family budget for the line count they have, or
   the one they're planning for?
3. **What a promotion is worth trusting.** Carrier V's credit is a one-time offer; the per-line
   rates in Appendix A are the ongoing price. Should a one-time incentive carry the same weight in
   the decision as a permanent price difference?

## The trap

A Hero computes the 2-line monthly cost, finds Carrier T cheaper, and stops — never checking what
happens at 3 lines even though the case's own Background states the family's 1-year plan explicitly.
The real tension is that "cheaper today" and "cheaper for the plan you've already stated" are
different questions with different answers here. Watch for a pitch that reports one line count and
calls the case closed.

## Socratic questions

1. Your D2 numbers say Carrier T is cheaper by 14.29% at 2 lines — how would you explain to Nate and Fiona why a percentage that size is or isn't worth building a full recommendation on?
2. The break-even point lands at exactly 3 lines, right when Charity is expected to get her phone — is that a coincidence worth mentioning in your pitch, or just a fact about the numbers?
3. Carrier V's $150 credit beats a full year of Carrier T's savings by less than $21 — how would your recommendation change if that credit were $100 instead of $150?
4. Appendix B assumes each carrier's additional-line rate stays the same when Charity's line is added — what would you tell the family if that assumption turned out to be wrong?
5. If the Handlesons were confident they'd only need 2 lines for the next three years, would today's monthly cost or the eventual break-even point matter more to their decision?

## Close

**VOTE AGAIN.** "Did we ask the right question at the start?" "What math was new to you tonight, and
how did you actually attack it?" Compare the closing vote to Anchor 1's — a room that didn't move at
all after the twist is worth asking about directly: did the credit not land, or did the room already
weigh a one-time credit against the standing family plan on their own?
