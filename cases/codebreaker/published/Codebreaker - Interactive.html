Overview

3:14 AM. James Charmichael circles Rieterpark for the third time, breath fogging in the cold, before he lets himself sit. The bench is empty except for a man in a dark coat who adjusts his hat, sets a book on his lap — the two signals, both correct. Klaus Muller, in person, for the first time in six months of dead drops and forum handles.

“Here are the photographs,” Klaus says, sliding a manila envelope down the bench without looking at him.

“We cannot be too careful,” Charmichael replies, tucking it into his coat. “We’re being watched. From now on, handwritten only. There’s a man on Bahnhofstrasse who sleeps near the tram stop — when you have something, put it in a coffee cup and leave it with him.”

Klaus’s hand is shaking slightly. He’s not a professional. He’s a name in a terror cell’s back office who decided, six months ago, to start talking to a stranger on the dark web, and every message since has been a small bet that no one is reading over his shoulder.

Charmichael stands to leave. “We need to write in code.”

“What code?”

He doesn’t answer yet. He has two, and only one of them is right for a man like Klaus.

Background

James Charmichael — Agent 008, British Secret Service — has spent four years working to dismantle a terrorist cell headquartered in Berlin. Klaus Muller, his source inside the organization, has fed him intelligence for six months and has become reliable enough that Charmichael now wants to lean on him harder, which means putting more of Klaus’s own safety on the line.

Charmichael is fluent in dozens of hand ciphers and can decode most of them in his head. Klaus is not. Klaus has never been trained in espionage, has no cryptography background, and his messages will sometimes be time-sensitive — a schedule, a name, a location that stops mattering in a matter of hours. Charmichael has to pick a cipher Klaus can actually execute correctly, by hand, under pressure — while assuming the terror cell may eventually recognize that a cipher is in use, even without the key (see Appendix D, Kerckhoffs’s Principle).

Two ciphers are on the table:
  • The Alphanumeric Cipher — every letter is assigned a fixed number 1–26, that number is multiplied by a secret multiplier, and the results are written as digit strings separated by zeros (Appendix A).
  • The Circle Shift Cipher — the plaintext is rewritten starting from its first vowel, reading every third letter and wrapping around the message until every letter has been used once (Appendix B).

Both ciphers must be able to encode the same two messages Charmichael needs to send Klaus this month (Appendix C). The rules for counting each cipher’s keyspace, and for counting how much Klaus actually has to write, are fixed conventions (Appendix D) — use them, or your numbers won’t match anyone else’s.

Appendices

Appendix A — The Alphanumeric Cipher

Assign each letter of the alphabet a unique value 1–26:

LetterABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ
Value2017481022111614193122315525713221182624619

To encrypt: multiply each letter's value by a secret single-digit multiplier. Write each letter's resulting number in order. Between each pair of consecutive letters, write a 0. Do not write spaces.

Worked example (multiplier = 2):

Plain text: NEED EXTRACTION NOW

Cipher text: 46020020016020048040140400804028030046046030052

Appendix B — The Circle Shift Cipher

To encrypt: find the first vowel in the plain text. Starting there, write every third letter, circulating back to the beginning of the message each time you reach the end, until every letter (and space) has been written exactly once. Write spaces as _.

Worked examples:

Plain text: MISSION COMPROMISED → Cipher text: ii_mosmsnoridsocpme

Plain text: NEED EXTRACTION NOW → Cipher text: e_tconndxai_weertno

Appendix C — The Two Required Messages

Charmichael needs Klaus able to receive both of these messages, in whichever cipher is chosen:

#Message
1Your brothers are alive
2Your wife is a double agent
Appendix D — Modeling Assumptions and Conventions

Convention 1 — Keyspace counting. Counted under Kerckhoffs’s Principle: the terror cell knows which cipher type is in use but not the specific key.

  • Alphanumeric key = a permutation of 1–26 assigned to the 26 letters, times an unknown single-digit multiplier (1–9).
  • Circle Shift key = the number of letters skipped; for a message of length n (incl. spaces), the skip ranges 1 to n − 1.

Convention 2 — Counting handwritten characters. Alphanumeric: every digit written plus every 0 separator between consecutive letters (none after the last letter); spaces are not encoded. Circle Shift: every output character 1:1 with the plaintext, spaces included as underscores.

Convention 3 — Multiplier. Worked examples and D4–D6 use a representative multiplier of 2.

Convention 4 — Transcription error rate (Modeling Assumption). For D7, assume Klaus has a 0.15% (0.0015) probability of miswriting any single character when copying a cipher text by hand under field conditions. This is a stated scenario assumption, not a sourced figure — no external source gives a real handwriting-under-duress error rate for this exact situation.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Keyspace & blind-guess probability (D1–D3)

    Compute each cipher's keyspace under Kerckhoffs's Principle (Appendix D, Convention 1), then the probability a single blind guess is correct.

    Step 2 — Encode both messages, count the characters (D4)

    Encode both required messages (Appendix C) in each cipher (multiplier = 2 for the Alphanumeric Cipher, Appendix D Convention 3), then enter the total characters your cipher text uses.

    Step 3 — Digit histogram, separator share & the Pigeonhole Principle (D5–D6)

    Using your two Alphanumeric cipher texts from Step 2, count how many times the digit "0" appears in total, and what share of all characters that is.

    Step 4 — Zero-error transcription probability (D7)

    Using Appendix D Convention 4's 0.15% per-character error rate and your Step 2 totals, compute the probability Klaus writes BOTH required messages with zero character errors, for each cipher.

    Reveal the Twist

    A few months later. You are now inside the terror cell. In Klaus's coat, you find a note: one of your associates is a trained codebreaker, and they've been quietly collecting every message Klaus has sent for the last six months — all encrypted with the same key, the same cipher, every time. They didn't need to brute-force the astronomical keyspace from Step 1. They ran frequency analysis on the pile of intercepted ciphertext instead — counting which numbers (or letters) show up most often and matching that pattern against ordinary written English.

    This is a real, sound mechanism, not an invented plot device: a monoalphabetic substitution cipher preserves the plaintext's own letter-frequency pattern in its ciphertext no matter how large its one-time keyspace is (see Appendix E, "Frequency analysis"). Sixty seconds — does this change your recommendation, or not?
