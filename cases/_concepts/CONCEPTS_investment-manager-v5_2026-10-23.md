### C1 — The Eight-Percent Bet   ·   score 9.6   ·   tier T4
**Protagonist.** Marcus Hale (fictional), finance director of the **Harbor City Gulls** (fictional club). The club has released an aging star but owes him his $5.9M salary for the 2000 season. The player's agent offers to be paid later, with interest.
**Personally at stake (stated carefully).** If the invested money falls short, **the pool pays the checks first and the roster budget Hale controls covers only the shortfall**; if it ends with a surplus, **the club keeps the surplus** (it becomes player-development money). The league-minimum salary in 2000 was **$200,000** ("the lowest amount which a club can pay a player for a full season"; Baseball-Reference Bullpen, `baseball-reference.com/bullpen/Minimum_salary`, confirmed via search excerpt). The deal's payment of $1,193,248 a year equals about six such players; a **$4.9M shortfall at the start of 2011** means finding about **$426,000 a year for 25 years: two players cut from the roster every season** (the visible cost students weigh).
**The dilemma.** A.) Pay the **$5.9M now** OR B.) Defer it at **8% interest** and repay in **25 equal payments** (the first 11 years later, the last 35 years after signing), investing the $5.9M under the club's **policy: 60% stocks / 40% 10-year Treasuries, rebalanced to 60/40 each January** (a stated constraint; other allocations appear as sensitivity).
**Timeline convention and what is tested (stated in student text).** The case invests on **January 1, 2000** and treats the first payment as due **January 1, 2011**, each later payment on January 1 (the real first payment was July 1, 2011; the case simplifies). Both sides are valued on **January 1, 2011**: the pool has earned the 11 calendar years **2000-2010**, and the debt has grown at 8% for 11 years. **This is a one-date snapshot**; it does not follow the pool through 2011-2035. If the pool is short on that date, the case assumes the club funds the gap in equal yearly amounts over the 25 payments **at 8%** (the rate the debt grows). That is where $426K a year comes from ($4,910,108 divided by the annuity-due factor 11.5288 for 8%).
**Student-chosen assumption (T4), now computed.** In D6 the student chooses the **rate at which the club funds a shortfall** and **recomputes the yearly gap payment** for the 2000-2010 outcome ($4,910,108 short at 60/40): **6% -> $362,360 a year; 8% -> $425,901; 10% -> $491,761** (annuity-due factors 13.5504, 11.5288, 9.9847). The student states which rate to defend and why (what the club could actually earn or borrow at), and what the club does with a surplus. How the pool should be invested after 2011 is a discussion question, not graded.
**Primary metric.** Pool ratio on January 1, 2011 = value of the invested $5.9M divided by the **$13.757M** owed then. B beats A when the ratio is at least 1; A has no pool and no debt, so the comparison is the sign of the net position (stated plainly).
**Reproduction (computed).** $5.9M x 1.08^11 = **$13.757M**; the annuity-due payment on that at 8% is **$1.1932M** a year (the real figure is $1,193,248.20; R1b recomputed it exactly); 25 x that = **$29.83M**, the undiscounted total reported in the press.
**Why it's close (computed; 88 rolling 11-year windows of calendar years, 1928-2025).**
| Allocation | Pool at or above owed | Median pool / owed | 10th pct | 90th pct |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **60/40, rebalanced (the club's policy)** | **52.3%** | **1.051** | 0.681 | 1.704 |
| 100% stocks (sensitivity) | 67.0% | 1.285 | 0.619 | 2.388 |
| 100% 10-year T-bond (sensitivity) | 20.5% | 0.655 | 0.523 | 1.158 |
(With buy-and-hold instead of annual rebalancing the 60/40 median is 1.085 and the share 53.4%; the case states rebalancing and shows this as a note.) At the club's policy the median outcome is **5%** above what is owed: a near coin flip with a wide spread, inside the ~20% balance rule. With 100% stocks it is 28.5%, outside it, which is why the allocation is policy and not a free choice.
**What actually happened (whole calendar years).** **2000-2010** at 60/40 returned **3.75% a year**: pool **$8.85M**, **$4.91M short**; at 100% stocks **0.39% a year**, pool $6.16M, $7.60M short; at 100% T-bonds 6.46%, pool $11.74M, $2.02M short. All three fell short of 8%. (2001-2011 at 60/40: 4.33%, pool $9.41M, $4.35M short.)
**Independence lesson.** The 88 overlapping windows are really **8 non-overlapping 11-year blocks**; the number at or above 8% depends on where the blocks start: at 60/40 **3 to 5 of 8** (37.5-62.5%), for 100% stocks **4 to 7 of 8** across start years 1928-1938.

### C2 — The Average Lies   ·   score 7.8   ·   tier T4
**Protagonist.** Ayo Bankole (fictional), a planner at a credit-union advice desk, and **Mara**, the granddaughter (fictional, an eighth-grader, who speaks in the meeting). A grandmother has given the family **$50,000** for Mara's **four years of in-state public college tuition and fees**, starting in five years; she wants **whatever is left over** to go toward Mara's graduate school.
**Mara's lines (student text; she starts college in five years).** *"What if the market drops the year before I start?"* · *"If there's money left, can it pay for grad school?"* · *"The calculator says we have plenty. Why are you still worried?"*
**The hook.** Every online college-savings calculator asks for one number: an assumed **steady annual return**. Ayo runs it. The calculator says the family needs far less than the grandmother gave. The case asks whether that is true. (The case models a calculator's assumed rate with each mix's historical compound average, the most generous honest version of what a calculator would assume.)
**The dilemma (asset allocation).** A.) A **70% stocks / 30% bonds** mix, held to the last bill OR B.) A **30% stocks / 70% bonds** mix.
**What "failing" means (student text).** If the money runs short, the family must **borrow the unpaid part of that bill**; the case counts the dollars borrowed (the largest total unpaid is about $21,100 for A and $4,900 for B, with unpaid amounts not carried forward).
**Primary metric.** The **share of historical 9-year windows in which all four bills are paid in full** at the grandmother's **$50,000**: B wins it by **4.9%** relative (96.7% vs 92.2%). **Second axis (symmetric):** the share of windows in which the family also has at least **$20,000 left** at the end of year 9: A wins it by about **87%** relative (64.4% vs 34.4%). Neither mix dominates **for any leftover threshold from about $10,000 up**:
| At $50,000, 3.5% tuition growth (90 windows) | A (70/30) | B (30/70) |
|---|---|---|
| All four bills paid | **92.2%** (fails in 7) | **96.7%** (fails in 3) |
| Bills paid **and** at least $20,000 left | **64.4%** | 34.4% |
| Median leftover (failures counted as $0) | **$36.7K** | $15.5K |
Leftover threshold sensitivity (bills paid and at least this much left): **$10K** A 80.0% / B 73.3%; **$20K** A 64.4% / B 34.4%; **$30K** A 56.7% / B 21.1%. The pattern (B safer, A richer) holds at tuition growth of 2.40% and 3.97%. **Teaching point:** for any leftover threshold **below about $8,500**, B leads on both axes (at $5K: B 92.2% vs A 87.8%); A leads the leftover axis only from about $8,500 up, so the student's threshold must be defended, and the case bounds the choice to **$10,000-$30,000**.
**Student-chosen assumptions (T4).** The student chooses and defends (a) the **tuition growth rate** within the measured band **2.40% (10-year) to 3.97% (20-year) a year** and (b) how much **leftover** is worth wanting, from **$10,000 to $30,000**, and tests the funding levels below.
**Setup (sourced, confirmed by R1b).** College Board *Trends in College Pricing 2025* (Excel data, Table CP-4, in-state public four-year tuition and fees, current dollars): **2005-06 $5,490; 2015-16 $9,430; 2025-26 $11,950** (CP-1); growth **2.40%/yr over 10 years, 3.97%/yr over 20** (the band mixes a 10-year and a 20-year rate; the case says so). Sticker price; net tuition and fees for first-time full-time in-state students is an estimated **$2,300 in 2025 dollars** (so sticker is the conservative input). **Scope:** tuition and fees only (housing and food are $13,900; the College Board's estimated budget is $30,990 including books, transport and other expenses; none modeled). Bills at 3.5% growth (the case's illustrative middle rate) are due at the **start** of years 6, 7, 8, 9 from today: about $14,193, $14,690, $15,204, $15,736, **total about $59,822** (bills due at the start of each year). **Window and dates:** each window is 9 consecutive calendar years; each January the balance earns that year's return after any bill is paid; the last bill is due at the start of year 9 (index 8); leftover is measured at the **end of year 9**, one year after the last bill (for reference, the balance **just after the last bill, before that year's return**, is about $34.2K for A and $14.9K for B).
**The calculator vs history (the heart of the case; computed).** The calculator's steady rate is each mix's own 1928-2025 average annual return, the **geometric (compound) mean: A 8.83%, B 6.63%** (the arithmetic mean, 9.75% and 6.93%, is the contrast). The closed-form lump sum for the four bills at that rate (a finite geometric series), and what it really delivers:
| | Calculator lump sum | Share of the 90 windows in which that lump sum pays all four bills | Lump sum for 95% of windows (smallest amount that pays all four bills in at least 86 of the 90 windows) | **Planning gap** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A (70/30) | **$34,545** | **54.4%** (**49 of the 90** nine-year stretches since 1928) | $53,722 | **+$19,177 (+56%)** |
| B (30/70) | **$39,417** | **36.7%** (33 of 90) | $49,813 | **+$10,396 (+26%)** |
The calculator names one number and **no probability**. Its practical reliability is the 54% and 37% above. **It also reverses the ranking:** it says A is cheaper ($34.5K vs $39.4K); history says B needs less at every reliability target above 85% (at 85% they tie, $226 apart), because returns bounce around and **the order of returns matters once money is being withdrawn**.
**Independence caveat (facilitator note, and one student question: "how many independent 9-year stretches are there?").** The 90 windows overlap (about 10 independent blocks). On **non-overlapping 9-year blocks**, B pays all four bills in at least as many blocks as A, but only by **0 or 1 block of 10** depending on the start year (offsets 1928-1936: A 7-10, B 8-10); five of A's seven failures start in 1928-1937. So "B is safer" is suggestive, not proven.
**Sequence experiment (illustrative, not proof).** At $50,000, A fails in **7** windows; running the same nine returns in **reverse order**, **6 of the 7 pay all four bills**; B fails in 3, and **3 of 3** pay. Same returns, same average, a different order and (mostly) a different result.
**Funding levels (a deliverable).** The two mixes pay all four bills equally often at about **$42,100 at 2.4% growth, $45,200 at 3.5%, $46,600 at 4.0%** (the first dollar level from which B ties or leads and stays ahead; the curves cross within roughly $1,000 because there are only 90 windows). Below it A pays more often; above it B does. **Fragility, disclosed:** $50,000 sits only $187 above B's 95% lump sum ($49,813), so the "B pays, A does not" reading at $50,000 rests on a thin margin; at $45,000 A leads (84.4% vs 83.3%) and at $55,000 B leads (100% vs 95.6%).
