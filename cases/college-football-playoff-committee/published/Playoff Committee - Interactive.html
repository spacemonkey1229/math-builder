Overview

The door shuts with a soft, deliberate click, the kind that says the outside world is now irrelevant. Phones go into a basket by the door. The windows are frosted glass. Somewhere beyond them, sports radio is still shouting about strength of schedule and who got snubbed, but in here the noise fades to static.

Thirteen people sit around a polished oval table. Laptops glow. A wall-sized digital board scrolls rankings, résumés, and power ratings nobody outside this room has seen yet.

Derek Talcott, Athletic Director at a mid-major program that recently moved up to a power conference, has done this dance before — but never with the room split like this. Eleven of the twelve playoff spots are locked, signed off, unanimous. The twelfth is not. It has come down to two names on the board, and every time the committee votes, it comes back tied.

“Walk us through it again, Derek,” the chair says.

Talcott pulls up two windows side by side. On the left: a team that has not lost all season, sitting comfortably inside the human poll's top five. On the right: a team the human poll has parked in the back half of the top ten — but whose computer rating, the cold arithmetic of point differential and schedule strength, says it is one of the best three teams in the country.

Somebody down the table mutters, “The computer doesn't know what it feels like to watch them play.”

Somebody else mutters back, “The eye test doesn't know how to count.”

Talcott looks at both spreadsheets. The room looks at him. A vote is called in five minutes.

Background

Every December, a thirteen-member College Football Playoff selection committee — athletic directors, former coaches, and administrators serving staggered terms — meets in person to rank the top 25 teams in the country and select the twelve who advance to the national championship bracket. The committee weighs each team's full résumé: strength of schedule, conference championships, head-to-head results, and comparative outcomes against common opponents, with no single factor given fixed weight (see Appendix D).

Derek Talcott was appointed to the committee this year, replacing a member who stepped down for personal reasons. Before this, Talcott built his career in athletic administration — stops at two smaller programs before landing his current post as Athletic Director, where he guided the program through its own move into a new power conference. He is known in the room as a strategist who reads a spreadsheet as comfortably as a box score.

This year, the field is unusually deep, and eleven of the twelve bracket spots came together with something close to consensus. The twelfth did not. Two teams remain on the table for the final at-large bid, and the committee's straw votes keep landing exactly split. Every figure in this case that isn't a direct citation is a disclosed, stated Modeling Assumption (see Appendix F), backed where possible by real sourcing (see Appendix G) — none is invented quietly:

  • The Resume Path would extend the bid to the team that has not lost all season and sits near the top of the season's final human poll — voters representing sports media and coaches across the country, who each rank their own top 25 and whose points are tallied into a single composite ranking (see Appendix B).
  • The Power-Rating Path would extend the bid to the other team instead: its won-loss record is less pristine, and human voters have ranked it well back in the top ten, but its Simple Rating System score (SRS), a computer power rating built from average point differential and strength of schedule, is one of the highest of any team in the country this season (see Appendix C; see Appendix A for how SRS, OSRS, and DSRS are defined).

Every committee member in the room has access to the same two data sets — the human poll and the computer ratings — and both tell a coherent, defensible story. They just don't agree with each other. The five-minute clock on the wall is still running. Background reading on how the selection process actually works is available (see Appendix E); the vocabulary and “how to…” skills this case will ask you to research are listed separately in Appendix H — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

Appendix D — How the Selection Committee Has Actually Voted
  • The committee has 13 members serving staggered terms, drawn from athletic directors, former coaches, players, and administrators across the sport [S2].
  • The committee weighs championships won, strength of schedule, head-to-head competition, and comparative outcomes against common opponents — with no single factor given fixed weight; each member forms an independent judgment [S2].
  • During the ten seasons of the original four-team playoff (2014–2023), the committee had no automatic-qualifier rule for any conference — every one of the four spots each year was an at-large selection based purely on the committee's own judgment of each team's résumé, not a guaranteed berth for any conference champion [S3].
  • The playoff expanded to twelve teams beginning with the 2024 season, following a Board of Managers vote, and is set to continue in that format through the 2027 season [S3]. A new $1.3 billion media-rights deal with ESPN takes effect starting with the 2026 season [S6].

Appendix F — Modeling Assumptions
IDAssumption
MA1The committee's eleven already-decided spots are treated as fixed; only the twelfth, contested spot is modeled in this case.
MA2A team's “Human score” is its AP voting points expressed as a percent of the maximum possible points (63 first-place voters × 25 points, confirmed directly from Appendix B [S1]).
MA3A team's “Machine score” is its SRS rating expressed as a percent of the single highest SRS rating in the full national dataset.
MA4The “Composite Selection Score” used for comparison purposes in this case is the unweighted mean of the Human score and the Machine score — a modeling choice made for this exercise, not an official CFP formula.
MA5This case is set in a fictional near-future December Selection Sunday; the underlying poll and power-rating figures are drawn from a real, historical FBS season's dataset (see Appendix G), reused here to give the exercise real, internally consistent numbers.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Human score (percent of maximum)

    Goal: express each team's AP voting points (Appendix B) as a percent of the maximum possible AP voting points (63 first-place voters × 25 points).

    Step 2 — Machine score (percent of ceiling)

    Goal: express each team's SRS (Appendix C) as a percent of the season's single highest SRS in the full national dataset (25.94).

    Step 3 — Composite Selection Score (D3)

    Goal: find each team's Composite Selection Score — the unweighted mean (MA4) of its Human score (Step 1) and its Machine score (Step 2).

    Step 4 — Margin

    Goal: find the percent margin between the two Composite Selection Scores — 100 × |A − B| / the lower of the two scores.

    This tells you the size of the gap, not which side of it is “right.” A different, equally defensible weighting than MA4's 50/50 split could move it — that's for your pitch to argue, not for this notebook to decide.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    “The committee chair points out something,” the story goes. “Georgia — the team supplying the SRS ceiling you've been dividing by — isn't a real competitor for this spot. It's the presumptive #1 overall seed, locked in from week one. If you recompute the Machine score using the highest SRS among teams that are actually in the room's conversation for an at-large bid — Alabama's 20.95, from Appendix C — instead of Georgia's 25.94, what happens to your numbers?”

    Recheck Step 2 with the box below checked, then recheck Steps 3 and 4. A team's Machine score can now come out to more than 100% of its own “ceiling” — which is a genuinely useful moment: it means the ceiling was never a hard cap, only a reference point the case chose (MA3), and choosing a different reference point changes the story you can tell with the same underlying data. Does this change your plan? You have 60 seconds — are you changing your recommendation, or not?
