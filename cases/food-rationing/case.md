Case Study: Food Rationing

MS | Math | Case Study | 2026 | Adventure

---

## Overview

Rain lashes the cabin windows sideways. Tamara Murray braces one boot against the nav table and hauls on a line that isn't holding anything anymore — the sail tore an hour ago, and the boat has been drifting since. "We have to fix this!" she yells. Her father-in-law John, gripping the other end of the torn canvas, yells back, "I can't get it to hold — it's too damaged!"

Behind them, Fiona is crying into a cushion. "What are we going to do, Mom? We're stranded!" Easton has his arms wrapped around himself, pale and silent, the way he's looked for a couple of days now. Chance just wants to be picked up.

By morning the wind has dropped. The sail is gone. The power is out. Tamara works through the boat systems one at a time, the way she was trained to. Water filters — fine, they can run for a long while. Cooking fuel — plenty. She opens the food locker last, already knowing the news won't be good, and it isn't. Far from the nearest port, drifting instead of sailing, with no radio and no way to know if a ship passes tomorrow or not for a long while — and a family who all need to eat every single day until one does.

Tamara sits back on her heels, the locker still open, and starts doing the math in her head.

## Background

The Murray family left mainland Mexico 12 days ago, sailing toward a planned resupply stop at Ebon Atoll. The storm hit six days short of that stop, snapping the boat's power and its sail and leaving it adrift somewhere in the open Pacific, within roughly 1,000 miles of Ebon Atoll (SEE APPENDIX B). With no radio and no way to steer, the family's only real plan is to wait for a passing ship — which could appear as soon as tomorrow, or not for as long as a month. Nobody back home expects a check-in until the family reaches port, so nobody yet knows they are missing.

Aboard are five of the six Murrays (SEE APPENDIX A): Tamara (35), an experienced sailor and mother to all four children; John (70), Tamara's father-in-law, also an experienced sailor, in good general health but managing Type 2 diabetes; Fiona (7), in good health; Easton (9), seasick for the past two days and not keeping much food down; and Chance (2), in good health. Aaron, the children's father, stayed in the U.S. for work and is not aboard.

Water and cooking fuel are not the problem — the boat's purifiers and fuel supply could last for months (SEE APPENDIX B). Food is the problem. The family provisioned for a short final leg to Ebon Atoll, not for weeks adrift, and what's left in the food locker (SEE APPENDIX C) has to somehow cover five people for an unknown number of days.

Tamara has to decide how the remaining food gets divided. She could **ration the food equally**, giving every family member the identical daily amount, regardless of age, health, or seasickness. Or she could **ration the food by need** — protecting John's diabetes management with a full, dependable ration, and giving Chance's much smaller toddler body and seasick Easton's unsettled stomach less than a full adult share (SEE APPENDICES D, E, F and G). Every figure in this case that isn't a direct citation is a disclosed, stated Modeling Assumption (SEE APPENDIX H) — none is invented quietly.

---

## Dilemma

You are Tamara Murray.

Do you:

A.) Ration the food equally — every family member gets the identical daily amount, regardless of age, health, or seasickness OR

B.) Ration the food by need — protect John's diabetes management and give Chance's smaller body its real toddler-sized share, with reduced rations for seasick Easton who isn't keeping food down

### Appendix F — Two Ways to Ration: The Setup

**Option A — Equal.** Every one of the five family members receives the same flat daily ration: **2,000 kcal/person/day** — the same 2,000-calorie figure the FDA uses as its one general reference amount on every U.S. Nutrition Facts label [S18], regardless of age or size.

**Option B — Need-based.** Each family member receives their own individual full recommended calories from Appendix E — except Easton, whose ongoing seasickness has him mostly confined to his bunk rather than at his usual activity level. His ration is matched to the *sedentary* (not moderately active) column of the same official table for a 9-year-old boy: **1,600 kcal**, not an invented percentage cut (SEE APPENDIX H, MA1). John's ration is his own full, protected 2,200 kcal — his diabetes changes how consistently he needs to eat, not how much (SEE APPENDIX G).

D4 asks you to total each option's daily consumption, divide it into Appendix D's supply, and compare how many days each option covers — neither option gets the family anywhere close to a full month at everyone's *full* recommended calories, which is exactly why D2 asks you to decide, and defend, how many days you are actually rationing for, and D3 asks you to design a real plan for that many days.

### Appendix G — Real-World Rationing and Health Context

**International maritime survival rations.** Under the international Life-Saving Appliances (LSA) Code, every ocean-going liferaft is required to carry a minimum food ration of **10,000 kJ (about 2,390 kcal) per person** it's rated to carry [S19] — issued identically to every occupant, regardless of age or health. This is real international maritime practice for exactly this kind of emergency, and it treats "equal" as the default.

**Type 2 diabetes and meal consistency.** The American Diabetes Association's clinical guidance emphasizes that for a person managing diabetes with a fixed medication routine, keeping the *timing and amount* of carbohydrate intake consistent from day to day is what matters most for safe blood sugar control [S20] — not necessarily eating more total calories than anyone else.

**Seasickness and eating.** Public health travel guidance recommends eating small amounts of food frequently, rather than large meals, to manage ongoing motion sickness and nausea [S21].
