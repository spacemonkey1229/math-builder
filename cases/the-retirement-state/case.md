## Overview

The projector fan hums, and the conference room smells of dry-erase marker and old coffee. Lindiwe Haverstock has two printed maps of the Southeast pinned side by side on the wall, drawn from the same spreadsheet, and she keeps stepping from one to the other. On the left map Florida is the darkest red in the region. On the right, the same Florida is nearly white, and Georgia, pale on the left, has taken its place.

She has checked the printing twice. Neither map is wrong. Her analyst built both in one afternoon from one spreadsheet, and the only difference is which of two death rates the colors show.

Above the whiteboard hangs the Fund's founding letter, one sentence underlined in yellow highlighter by some trustee years ago: the Fund exists to lower the death rate in the communities it serves. Two trustees who agree on almost nothing have each phoned her this week to say which map is the honest one. Each used the same word. Each said "obviously," and each meant a different map.

Tomorrow the board gives out one award to one state. The board does not read legends. It reads colors, and it reads the person standing beside them.

Lindiwe steps back from the wall. Down the corridor a door opens, a chair scrapes, and the board chair's voice carries cheerfully down the hall: "Lindiwe? Which one are we looking at?"

## Background

Lindiwe Haverstock is a program officer at the Harbor Light Health Fund, a regional health foundation. Each year the Fund's board gives one grant, paid to health programs in one state of its region, and Lindiwe recommends which state. The Fund's charter says it exists "to lower the death rate in the communities it serves." It does not say which death rate, and it does not say what an award must buy: direct care and capacity (such as hospice, home health and ambulance coverage), prevention programs (such as screening and smoking cessation), or both. The board has not decided [MA5].

The board has narrowed the finalists to two states and the case treats that shortlist as given [MA1]. The comparison is one year, 2017, the last year in the Fund's data pack (the public files it works from), which is built from the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) [S7] public mortality file [S1] and the Census Bureau's population estimates [S2][S8]. The board meets in January 2020, and no figure published after the data pack appears in this case [MA2]. The measure is deaths from all causes at all ages, as a rate per 100,000 residents [MA3]. Raw death counts are out of scope: a state with twice the residents has about twice the deaths at the same risk, so a count mostly measures how big a state is, and the Fund's award is not sized by head-count (SEE APPENDIX E).

Two rates are in play. A **crude death rate** is a state's deaths in a year divided by its residents, scaled to 100,000. The NCHS file does not publish it, so this case derives it as deaths divided by the Census estimate of residents on July 1 [S1][S2][S5][MA4]. An **age-adjusted death rate** is the rate a state would have if its residents had the same mix of ages as a fixed standard population, here the U.S. population of the year 2000. NCHS publishes it [S1], and the method is described in an NCHS statistical note [S6]. It removes differences that come only from one state having older residents than another, and NCHS describes it as an index, not a direct measure of anyone's chance of dying [S4]. The inputs for both are in (SEE APPENDIX A), and the Census counts of residents aged 65 and over, which show that Florida's population is older than Georgia's, are in (SEE APPENDIX B).

**The Crude Map** shades the region by crude rate; on it, Florida is the finalist with the higher rate. **The Adjusted Map** shades the region by age-adjusted rate; on it, Georgia is the finalist with the higher rate.

Appendix C lists all 50 states and the District of Columbia on both rates, and Appendix D gives the two finalists' rates for 2010 through 2016. Appendix E explains how this pair was chosen. Appendix F lists the stated assumptions, Appendix G every source, and Appendix H the "how to…" questions you will research.

## Dilemma

You are Lindiwe Haverstock.

Do you:

**A.)** Give the Fund's award to Florida, **The Crude Map** finalist, where residents die at the higher crude rate **OR**

**B.)** Give the Fund's award to Georgia, **The Adjusted Map** finalist, where residents die at the higher age-adjusted rate

## Appendix B — Age Structure, July 1, 2017

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D2). Residents aged 65 and over, from the Census Bureau's estimates by age [S3]. These come from an earlier estimate series than Appendix A's residents, so each total differs from Appendix A's by well under one percent.

| | Florida | Georgia | United States |
|---|---|---|---|
| Residents aged 65 and over | 4,209,641 | 1,406,519 | 50,793,923 |
| All residents (same series) | 20,976,812 | 10,413,055 | 325,147,121 |
| Share aged 65 and over | 20.1% | 13.5% | 15.6% |

Among the 50 states and the District of Columbia, Florida has the highest share aged 65 and over, and Georgia's is 47th highest, which is fifth lowest of the 51. The counts say how many residents are in each age group. They do not say why people live where they do.

How to read an age-adjusted rate: suppose every state had the same share of its residents in each age group as one fixed standard population (here, the U.S. population of the year 2000). The age-adjusted rate is the death rate each state would then have, using that state's own death rates at each age [S4][S6].

A picture in words. Imagine two towns where, at every age, people have exactly the same chance of dying, but one town is mostly young and the other mostly old. The old town has the higher crude rate only because more of its people are in the age groups where deaths are more common. Age adjustment gives both towns the same age mix, and their rates come out equal. The "standard population" is that shared age mix: a fixed set of age shares from the U.S. population of the year 2000, not a real state, so that every state is measured against one yardstick [S6]. An age-adjusted rate is an "index," meaning a number built by a formula so that places can be compared, not a count of anything that happened to anyone [S4].

## Appendix E — Why These Two States, and What Is Out of Scope

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D2, D3). This appendix states the rule that picked the pair, so you can check it, and what the case leaves out.

**How the pair was chosen.** The 50 states and the District of Columbia make 1,275 pairs. In 280 of them the two rates disagree about which of the two is worse. In 241 of those 280, the gap on each rate is under 20 percent of the higher of the two rates. Of those 241 pairs, Florida and Georgia is the one whose smaller gap is the largest. It is one pair among many that disagree; for example, New York and Texas also disagree. The case does not claim these two are the largest, the oldest or the most typical pair.

**What is out of scope.**

- *Raw death counts.* Appendix A shows deaths because the crude rate needs them. The Fund's award is not sized by counts, because a count rises with the number of residents even when each resident's risk is the same.
- *Any year but 2017 in the decision.* Appendix D is for checking the 2017 picture, not for replacing it.
- *Causes of death, and the reasons people live where they do.* The files do not hold either.
- *Whose deaths matter more.* This case compares statistics about whole populations. It does not ask you to value one age group's deaths over another's, and age adjustment is a statistical device, not a verdict on older residents.
