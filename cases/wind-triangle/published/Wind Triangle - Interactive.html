Overview

The plotter on Reza Haddad's second monitor finishes drawing the jet stream across the middle of the country, and it lands exactly where he did not want it. The coffee beside his keyboard has gone cold. Somewhere behind him on the dispatch floor a colleague laughs at something on a phone. Nobody else in the room is looking at his screen.

Meridian 412 pushes back within the hour, and the release cannot go until it carries his certificate number. Not the captain's. His. If that airplane rolls out at the far end with less fuel in the tanks than the rules required him to plan for, the letter comes to his desk, and it comes with his name on it.

He has two flight plans open side by side. One is the route the schedulers assume he will file — the straight one, the one that looks shortest on the map, the one that runs directly into the ribbon of wind now glowing across his display. The other bends south, adds miles he will have to justify to somebody, and slides underneath the worst of it.

The forecast is a forecast. It is the best product the government publishes, and it is still, in the end, a statement about air that has not happened yet.

The captain's name appears in the crew chat: Anything I should know before I sign?

Reza drags the two plans onto one screen and looks at them together for the first time.

Background

Reza Haddad is a certificated aircraft dispatcher on the domestic desk at Meridian Air. Under the rules his airline flies by, he and the captain share responsibility for the flight: the captain commands the aircraft, but the dispatcher signs the release, chooses the route, and computes the fuel load, and neither of them may depart without the other's agreement. The fuel figure on the release is Reza's professional judgment, filed under his own certificate.

Meridian 412 is a scheduled transcontinental service from New York Kennedy to San Francisco, flown westbound in the morning against the prevailing upper-level flow. Two routings are on Reza's screen (see Appendix A).

The Great Circle follows the short northern track — within a couple of nautical miles of the shortest distance between the two airports across the curved surface of the earth — and passes through the forecast core of the jet stream (see Appendix B).

The Southern Swing bends the routing south of the forecast jet core. It is a longer track over the ground, but the upper-level wind along it is forecast to be far weaker (see Appendix B).

Both routings are flown by the same aircraft, at the same cruise altitude, at the same true airspeed and the same cruise fuel flow (see Appendix C) — so the two options differ in exactly two things: how far the aircraft flies through the air, and how hard the air pushes back.

Two hard constraints apply to either choice. First, the regulations set a minimum dispatch fuel load: enough to fly to the destination, then to the most distant filed alternate, then for a fixed additional period at normal cruising fuel consumption (see Appendix C). Reza has to compute that figure for whichever routing he files, and put it on the release. Second, the fuel Meridian buys is a real cost per gallon, and the difference between two routings is money the airline actually spends or actually keeps, on every departure, all year (see Appendix D).

The wind data Reza is planning against is a published forecast for a stated date and valid period, not a measurement of the flight itself, and Meridian's system pulls the whole corridor's worth of stations whether a given routing uses them or not (see Appendix B). Where a figure in this case is not a matter of public record, it is stated as an explicit modeling assumption (see Appendix E) rather than guessed, and every sourced figure is listed in Appendix F. The vocabulary and the "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in Appendix H — that is where Challenge 1 sends you. Curious about the real dispatcher's job, the jet stream itself, or the aircraft in this case beyond what the Dilemma requires? See Appendix G for further reading.

Appendix B — Upper-Level Winds

Wind is reported the way meteorology reports it: the direction the wind is blowing from, in degrees clockwise from true north, and its speed in knots. A wind "from 300 at 78" is blowing out of the north-west at seventy-eight knots — which is to say the air itself is moving toward 120°.

The figures below are taken verbatim from the National Weather Service's Winds and Temperatures Aloft Forecast, product FBUS33 KWNO / FD3US3, issued 12 August 2026 at 2001Z, data based on 121800Z, valid 130600Z, for use 0300–1200Z [S3]. The raw coded group is reproduced exactly as the product publishes it, in the 34,000 ft column.

How to read the coded group. The first two digits are the wind direction in tens of degrees true; the next two are the wind speed in knots; the last two are the temperature in degrees Celsius (negative above 24,000 ft, so the sign is not printed). 307843 is therefore a wind from 300° at 78 knots, temperature −43°C.

MA1Meridian's frequency on this city pair, used only to annualise the per-flight difference 2 departures per day, 365 days a year (730 departures) Meridian Air is fictional; no schedule for it can exist publicly. Two daily frequencies is an ordinary mainline transcontinental pattern. Scales the annual dollar figure linearly. Cannot change which routing wins — it multiplies both routings by the same number.
MA2Airway-structure allowance: the percentage by which filed track miles exceed the great-circle distance, because real routings follow published airways and fixes rather than an exact great circle +1.0% on the northern corridor (Routing A), +1.5% on the southern corridor (Routing B) A real, well-known effect with no published per-corridor figure. The southern corridor is given the larger allowance on the reasoning that it offers fewer direct routings and more weather deviation. That direction is asserted, not measured, and you may reasonably argue the opposite. Directly load-bearing, and the single largest stated lever in the case. Flexing each value ±50%, one at a time, leaves the gap between the two routings between 0.95% and 2.40% on the primary metric; taking both to their opposite extremes at once widens that band to 0.46%–2.88%. No setting inside that range changes which routing comes out ahead. Reversing the two values widens the gap rather than narrowing it, to 2.64%.
MA3One wind vector per leg, taken at the leg's downwind-end waypoint and held constant along the whole leg Applied to all six legs A real flight-planning system samples the forecast grid continuously. Holding one published point wind per leg is what makes this case computable by hand. Direction of error: it understates how much the wind varies along a long leg. It hits Routing A's 957.4 nm KDBQ→KSLC leg and Routing B's 936.0 nm KJFK→KSGF leg hardest, and those two legs are roughly the same length — so the simplification does not obviously favour one routing over the other.
MA4Density of Jet A used to convert pounds of fuel to US gallons 6.7 lb per US gallon The planning density used throughout US aviation. The governing specification (ASTM D1655) is behind a paywall and no US-government page stating a pounds-per-gallon figure was located and fetched for this case — so this is declared as an assumption rather than cited. Affects only the dollar figure, and affects both routings identically. Cannot change which routing wins.
MA5Leg geometry: each leg's length is the great-circle distance between its endpoints, while its true course is the initial great-circle course of that leg, held constant from end to end Applied to all six legs of both routings, and to the KSFO→KLAX alternate leg A great circle's true course changes continuously along the leg: on the 957.4 nm KDBQ→KSLC leg it runs from 271.4° where the leg begins to 257.2° where it ends. Holding the initial course constant is the standard hand-planning simplification, and it is what makes a single dot product per leg meaningful at all. A track flown on a genuinely constant true course is a rhumb line, which on these legs is longer than the great circle by roughly a tenth of a percent — so the two halves of this assumption pull very slightly against each other. Load-bearing on every along-course and crosswind component in D3 and D4, and the least conservative assumption stated here. Re-solving the whole case instead on the mean of each leg's initial and final course, on its final course, or on true rhumb-line courses with rhumb-line distances, moves the gap on the primary metric by well under a percentage point in each case and changes which routing comes out ahead in none of them.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 · D1 — Wind vector: magnitude/direction → components

    Pick a leg. Read that leg's forecast wind from Appendix B (direction it blows FROM, and speed), then resolve it into east and north components in knots. Remember: a wind FROM 300° blows TOWARD 120°.

    Step 2 · D2 — Course unit vector

    Same or a different leg. Read the leg's initial true course from Appendix A, and express it as a unit vector (east, north). Then confirm its magnitude is exactly one.

    Step 3 · D3 — Wind component along the course (dot product)

    Project the leg's wind vector onto its course unit vector. The along-course component is the dot product (negative = headwind, positive = tailwind, under the sign convention this notebook uses); the crosswind component is what's left over, at 90° to the course.

    Step 4 · D4 — Wind triangle: ground speed

    Add the true-airspeed vector and the wind vector head-to-tail. The aircraft crabs into the wind so the ground vector lies along the course; its magnitude is the ground speed. Closing the triangle: ground speed = √(TAS² − crosswind²) + along-course component.

    Step 5 · D5 — Trip fuel and break-even

    Sum leg time (filed nm ÷ ground speed) × fuel flow across all three legs of each routing to get trip fuel. Then find the break-even: by what percentage would Routing A's along-course headwind components have to be overstated for the two routings to burn identical fuel?

    If you've revealed and applied the Twist below, this checks Routing B re-filed via KMEM instead of the as-filed track.

    Reserve check: minimum dispatch fuel = trip fuel + alternate leg (KSFO→KLAX, against the KSFO wind, no airway allowance) + 45 minutes at cruise fuel flow.

    Reveal the Twist

    It is now forty minutes to push. A line of thunderstorms has gone up across the Ozarks, and air traffic control has shut the corridor through Springfield. Nobody is going through there tonight.

    If you still want the southern plan, you re-file it through Memphis instead — which is further again. Both of those waypoints are already in your corridor file (Appendix A's corridor table has KMEM's position; Appendix B has KMEM's forecast wind), so you can price it. Same aircraft, same fuel flow, same rules.

    You have about a minute. Is the southern plan still the plan? What just broke in your original model — were you wrong, or were you missing information?
