Overview

Wes Okonkwo has the drawing pulled up on two monitors and a paper printout of the same drawing on the desk between them, because he has learned not to trust himself to catch an error on a screen alone.

The satellite ships to the launch site in two weeks. Everything else on it is done — solar arrays folded and stowed, harness routed, thermal blankets closed out — except for the one signature block still empty at the bottom of the separation-joint drawing: his. The clampband is the last thing holding the satellite to the rocket, and the first thing that has to let go, cleanly, on command, four hundred miles up, with no technician standing by to give it a second try.

Three different shops made the three parts that stack together at that joint, and all three came back from inspection this morning marked "accept" — each one is genuinely within its own printed tolerance. That is exactly the problem. Wes has two spreadsheets open, each one a different way of adding those three "accept" numbers together into a single predicted margin, and the two spreadsheets do not agree on how much room the joint actually has.

His phone buzzes with a text from the program manager asking if the drawing is signed yet. Wes doesn't answer it. He minimizes the message, leans back, and looks at the two numbers side by side.

Background

Wes Okonkwo is a mechanical integration engineer at a small-satellite prime contractor. He owns the mechanical sign-off on the payload separation system for the company's current spacecraft, and his signature on the clampband joint drawing is the one that certifies the joint is ready to fly. Once the satellite is integrated to the launch vehicle at the range, this decision cannot be revisited — there is no on-orbit rework for a separation joint that fails to release (see Appendix A).

The joint in question is a Marman-style clampband — a band that clamps two mating flanges together under preload, and is designed to release both flanges cleanly the instant the band is cut or unlatched. Three separate interfaces stack together to determine how much margin the joint actually has once it's assembled, and each interface comes from a different supplier: the bus adapter flange outer diameter, the clampband ring inner diameter, and the preload bolt-and-shim assembly (see Appendix C). Every one of the three parts delivered for this build passed its own supplier's inspection against its own printed tolerance. None of the three suppliers did anything wrong.

The question is how to combine those three independently-toleranced parts into a single predicted number for how tight or loose the joint will actually be — and how that predicted number should be checked against published clampband design-margin thresholds (a NASA design guideline's own preload criterion, paired with the numeric margins real clampband programs design to) (see Appendix B).

Two methods are on the table.

The Worst-Case Stack. Add all three suppliers' tolerances directly, assuming the worst possible combination of all three parts could occur at once. This is the conservative approach: it guarantees that every accepted joint releases correctly under any combination of in-spec parts, at the cost of a wider predicted tolerance band — which means rejecting (and reworking, or requesting replacement parts for) some individual parts that are genuinely within their own printed spec, simply because the specific combination on hand happens to fall near the edge.

The Modified RSS Stack. Combine the three suppliers' tolerances using the root-sum-square method (the square root of the sum of the squares), then apply the correction factor that real tolerance-stacking practice applies on top of it. Pure, uncorrected RSS assumes every supplier's printed ± tolerance is a true 3-sigma bound on a centered, normal, independent process — an assumption that a 1962 tolerance-stacking study (later adopted into Motorola's Six Sigma mechanical design practice) found routinely fails in practice, because printed tolerances are often really closer to a 2-sigma range. The correction is a flat ×1.5 multiplier on the RSS result (see Appendix D) — real, industry-standard practice, not a full worst-case sum, but not naive uncorrected RSS either.

Both methods are legitimate, real engineering practice; nothing about the joint's design requires one over the other. What changes the comparison is that this contractor's satellite line is scaling: near-term programs call for clampband joints with more than three contributing supplier interfaces (see Appendix D). Extending both stack-up formulas to those future joints, and converting NASA-adjacent industry's dimensionless margin thresholds into a comparison against a dimensional tolerance stack, requires stating a handful of scenario modeling choices, disclosed in full — including how much confidence to place in each one — in Appendix E (see Appendix E). Published recommended design-margin thresholds for clampband systems, the real per-supplier tolerance values for this build, and the stack-up math for both methods across a growing supplier count are in the appendices below. Every external source cited anywhere in this case is collected in Appendix F. The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case asks you to research are listed in Appendix G — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

Appendix D — Stack-Up Formulas Across a Growing Supplier Count

The Worst-Case Stack predicts a total tolerance equal to the direct sum of every contributing interface's tolerance: T_WC(n) = t₁ + t₂ + … + tₙ.

The Modified RSS Stack predicts a total tolerance equal to the root-sum-square of every contributing interface's tolerance, scaled by a real, industry-standard correction factor of 1.5 (the "Benderizing" correction, see Appendix F, S6): T_MRSS(n) = 1.5 × √(t₁² + t₂² + … + tₙ²), where t is the ± tolerance of each contributing supplier interface and n is the number of contributing interfaces.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    Beat 1. The company's next satellite program is already contracted to use a clampband joint with substantially more contributing supplier interfaces than this one — and Wes's sign-off memo is being adopted as the default policy for that program too, not re-derived from scratch. Whatever you decided by reasoning only from today's n=3 numbers just stopped being the whole question. What just broke in your original model — were you wrong about the math, or just missing information about how far this decision actually reaches?

    Beat 2. By now, most rooms have converged on whichever method still clears both of Appendix B's thresholds across the full modeled range — treating it as the safe, durable pick, "safety margin built in twice over." Before you settle there: you're trusting a ×1.5 correction factor from a 1962 study, carried into practice by a 1988 Motorola guide. What if it's not exactly 1.5 — what if the real correction for this build's suppliers should be larger, say ×2.25? Does your durable pick still survive, at the largest modeled supplier count?
