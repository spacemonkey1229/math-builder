Overview

Jess Okafor has the CRM export open in one tab and the board deck open in the other, and the cursor won't stop blinking in the "Send To" field. Two lists sit side by side on the screen: one column of everyone who bought a Basecamp Crate box in the last ninety days, another column of everyone who ever checked the newsletter box at checkout. Nobody on this three-person marketing team has ever actually compared them.

A Slack message from the CEO is still pinned at the top of the channel: "Board meeting Friday. Need to see the plan for the number by then." The quarter is behind, and this send is the last one the ad budget can cover before the quarter closes. Jess has already dragged the two lists into a spreadsheet twice and closed it twice, because both times the honest next step was a diagram, not a guess.

The office is empty except for the hum of the space heater under the desk and a half-cold oat-milk latte. Jess pulls up a blank circle-and-circle sketch, types the two list sizes into the corners, and stops with the cursor hovering between "Send to everyone" and "Send to just the middle."

The file has to go out today. One list. One send. One number to bring to the board on Friday.

Background

Jess Okafor is the sole growth marketer at Basecamp Crate, a small outdoor-gear subscription box company. Marketing owns the CRM, the newsletter signup form, and the paid-send budget, and Jess is the only person who touches all three — there is no one else to double check the math before Friday's board meeting.

Basecamp Crate's CRM currently holds two separate customer lists (a Set of contacts, in the vocabulary below), built by different tools at different times (SEE APPENDIX A): Set P, everyone who purchased a box in the last 90 days, and Set L, everyone who has ever opted into the email newsletter. The purchase-tracking form and the newsletter signup form were built by two different vendors two years apart, and nobody on the current team has ever run the two lists against each other to see how much they overlap.

This quarter's remaining paid-send budget is fixed and cannot be exceeded (SEE APPENDIX B): whatever list Jess chooses, the total cost of the send — every name on the list, billed at the same flat per-recipient rate regardless of whether that person ever opens the email — must fit inside it. There is no partial-send option and no way to add more budget before Friday.

The two options on the table are named plainly on Jess's whiteboard: Cast The Net, mailing every name that appears in either list (the union of P and L), or Mail The Middle, mailing only the names that appear in both lists (the intersection of P and L). A published industry benchmark report gives a real, sourced conversion-rate figure for what a broad, undifferentiated send typically achieves, and that same body of published research informs — without directly measuring — how much better a tightly-overlapping segment is likely to convert (SEE APPENDIX C). The vocabulary and "how to…" procedures needed to work through the two sets are listed in (SEE APPENDIX D), and every sourced or assumed figure in this case is documented in the Sources list below.

Jess has to recommend one list, defend the expected revenue math behind it, and file it before the office closes today.

What "conversion" means here: a placed-order (conversion) rate of 0.16% means that out of every 1,000 people who receive the send, 1.6 of them go on to complete a purchase — place an actual order, not just open the email or click a link. Challenge 3 (D4) multiplies this rate by reach and by AOV to turn "how many people saw the email" into "how much revenue the send is expected to generate."

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — D1: Fill in the Venn diagram's remaining regions

    Using Appendix A's given counts (P = 21,400, L = 38,600, P ∩ L = 6,900, U = 90,000), compute the three regions the diagram above left blank, then check whether any of the four regions is the empty set.

    Step 2 — D2: Set-notation summary and subset determination

    Use inclusion-exclusion to compute |P ∪ L| from |P|, |L|, and |P ∩ L|. Then decide, from Appendix A's actual counts, whether P ⊆ L, L ⊆ P, or neither.

    This calculator checks your arithmetic — it will not tell you which option to recommend. That's your D5/pitch to make.

    Step 3 — D3: Reach-and-cost table

    Compute each option's reach and total send cost at Appendix B's flat per-recipient fee, then check whether each fits Appendix B's $4,000 remaining budget.

    Step 4 — D4: Expected net revenue for each option

    Using Appendix C's conversion-rate figures and AOV, compute each option's expected net revenue: reach × conversion rate × AOV − total send cost.

    Still just checking your own arithmetic. If you've revealed and applied the Twist below, this step re-checks against the challenged number instead.
