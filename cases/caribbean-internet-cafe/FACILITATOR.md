# Facilitator Guide — Caribbean Internet Café

MS/HS | Heroes Academy | Anchor-ladder format (SPEC.md, Section 7, "The facilitator artifacts")
**Primary metric:** CIC's expected monthly pre-tax operating cash flow under Appendix E's
realistic scenario, Option A (self-financed) vs. Option B (JTL offer) · **Margin:** disclosed in
`case.json.balance` and this guide's Sensitivity note — deliberately not printed in case.md.

## Intro

Write on the board: *"Would you rather own 100% of a struggling dream, or 50% of a thriving
one?"* Don't open with numbers. Open with the moment: two pieces of paper on a kitchen table, one
handwritten, one with someone else's letterhead.

## Launch

Read the Overview aloud. Optional: a simple visual of 1990s Jamaica, a period Internet café, a
Pentium-era computer, a dial-up loading screen — texture only, nothing load-bearing. Stop exactly
where the case does: David picking up his pen, no decision made yet.

## Anchor 1 — TAKE A STAND AND VOTE

Move to one side of the room for Option A (go alone), the other for Option B (accept JTL). No
reasoning yet. Then ask: *why did you move there? What scared you about the other side?* Write the
split on the board and let the disagreement sit.

## Anchor 2 — WHAT IS THE PROBLEM WE'RE SOLVING?

Point to three words on the board: INTERNET ACCESS · CAFÉ · WINE LOUNGE. *"If you had to say CIC
is ONE of these businesses, which is it?"* Force a second physical move. Then: *which part
actually generates the money? Which part is the bait? What happens to this business if home
Internet access gets cheap someday?* Let the room sit in genuine uncertainty here — there is no
clean answer yet, and that is correct.

## Anchor 3 — WHAT IS THE PROCESS?

*"If we had every number in this case in front of us, how would we actually decide between A and
B?"* Push the room toward naming a real comparison (a cost, a cash flow, a break-even point) rather
than a vibe. List candidate variables on the board: customer traffic, rent, staff wages,
computer-hour cost, loan interest, ownership share. Force a rank of the five that matter most — no
ties. Then re-ask Anchor 2's question: *given this process, are we even asking the right thing?*

## Anchor 4 — WHAT DON'T WE KNOW?

*"What are we assuming, and which assumptions actually matter?"* Surface, without resolving yet:
what interest rate would David actually pay on his own, going it alone? (The case never quotes
one directly — Appendix F's MA1 is exactly this gap.) How many customers will really walk in the
door? Bank these; the Data Deep Dive and the Twist both answer pieces of them.

## Data Deep Dive

Now, and only now, hand out Appendices B–E. Let Heroes build D1 (the PSI table) and D2 (the
financing-cost comparison) before they touch D3/D4's break-even math. Watch for the trap forming
in real time: someone will compare 10% to 25% and declare the case solved. Let it happen.

## Twist

**Drop this at roughly the 15-minute mark**, slowly:

> "It's now three years later. Home computer prices have fallen further and faster than anyone in
> 1996 predicted, JTL itself is starting to sell cheap home Internet packages, and a second Internet
> café has opened across town. This isn't a hypothetical — it's exactly what happened to Internet
> cafés worldwide as home broadband spread in the years that followed. If Appendix E's *realistic*
> scenario was ever going to happen, this is roughly when it stops holding — usage doesn't grow
> from here, it erodes toward pessimistic and below.
>
> One more thing, and this is the part neither of you noticed while you were building your D1
> table: **add up exactly how much money each option actually raises against how much it actually
> needs.** Option B raises David's $500,000, plus JTL's $500,000 equity, plus JTL's $1,250,000 loan
> — a combined $2,250,000 against a roughly $1,573,000 need. Option A raises David's $500,000 plus
> just enough borrowed money to cover the rest — exactly the amount needed, no more."

**What this invalidates:** the popular "A is obviously the safer, more prudent choice — no
partner, no dilution, no risk of losing control" position. It doesn't make B "obviously correct"
either — B still costs David half the company forever, even in the good years. But it reframes the
whole comparison from *which option makes more money in a good year* to *which option can survive
a bad one* — a question the realistic-scenario cash-flow numbers alone never surface.

**The follow-up (the whole point):** *"What just broke in your model? Were you wrong, or just
missing information?"* Push the room to name that D1–D4's math was never wrong — it's still true,
every month, under Appendix E's realistic scenario — but it silently assumed the realistic scenario
holds indefinitely and that neither option ever needs a cash cushion. Both assumptions were always
implicit, never stated, and the Twist is what makes a room notice.

**Soundness check (SPEC.md §6 / case-write §11):** read sequentially — Appendix F's MA1 (Option A's
25% rate), MA2 (internet cost tracks paid computer-hours), MA3 (52⁄12 weeks/month) — the Twist adds
new information (the historical trend, and the "how much is raised vs. needed" arithmetic) without
contradicting any of the three. Read simultaneously — holding all three assumptions plus the Twist
at once — nothing in D1–D4's arithmetic changes; only what a Hero should conclude from it changes.
The "amount raised vs. needed" fact is arithmetic straight off Appendix B/D's own numbers (equipment
$1,426,000 + up-front $120,000 + pre-opening ad $20,000 + utility deposit $7,000 = $1,573,000 need;
Option A raises exactly that; Option B raises $2,250,000) — sound under either reading, no
assumption-order dependency, because it doesn't depend on Appendix F's assumptions at all.

## Close (10 minutes left)

VOTE AGAIN. *"Did we ask the right question the first time? What math was new to you, and how did
you attack it?"* Ask specifically who moved because of the interest-rate trap (D2 alone) versus who
moved because of the Twist's cushion arithmetic — two different kinds of "wrong," worth naming
separately. Final question to let sit before closing: *"Is entrepreneurship more about having a
brilliant idea, or surviving uncertainty long enough for the idea to work?"*

## Lessons Learned

*(Preserved and extended from the legacy discussion framework.)* The interest-rate comparison (10%
vs. 25%) is real, but it is one line in a much larger monthly cost structure, and by itself it
answers the wrong question — which option is *cheaper*, not which option *survives*. A Hero who
stops at the interest rates has done real, correct arithmetic and still missed the case's actual
lesson: a financing decision made under a single-year, single-scenario lens looks completely
different once you ask what happens if the business's demand curve is temporary rather than
permanent — a question every real venture eventually has to answer, whether the "twist" that forces
it is a market shift, a competitor, or the discovery that money raised was never really enough.

## Five Socratic questions

1. Option B's loan rate (10%) is well under half of Option A's assumed rate (25%). If interest rate alone decided this, B wins in a landslide — so why does the case still ask you to build D3 and D4 instead of stopping at D2?
2. Walk me through exactly how you converted Appendix C's "90 hours per week" staff figure into a monthly dollar cost. What did you have to assume about how many weeks are in a month, and would a different assumption change which option wins?
3. Appendix E gives you three *annual* visit-rate scenarios. What arithmetic turns "40% of 20,000 people, 3 visits per year" into a number you can actually compare to a monthly break-even figure?
4. Under the pessimistic scenario, does either option actually turn a profit in a typical month? If not, what does "better" even mean when comparing two options that both lose money?
5. The Twist told you Option A raises exactly as much money as it needs, no more. Recompute: how much does Option B raise beyond what it needs? What would have to be true for that gap to matter more than the interest-rate difference you found in D2?
6. Your D2-D4 tables compare CIC's costs and cash flow as a company. Option B only exists because JTL now owns 50 percent of CIC. Recompute Option B's monthly cash flow as *David's own take-home* instead of CIC's — what happens to the comparison, and why didn't D1-D4 ask you to do this?

## The trap, named explicitly

A Hero looks at Option B's 10% loan rate next to Option A's assumed 25% rate, concludes Option B
is obviously and hugely superior, and stops there. This is the plausible reasoning that produces a
confident, incomplete answer: financing cost is real, but it is a small piece of CIC's full monthly
cost structure — rent, staff wages, insurance, marketing, and the cost of the coffee/food/internet
CIC actually sells all matter as much or more, and once D3's fixed costs and contribution margin
are worked through in full, the gap between the two options on realistic-scenario cash flow is
real but far smaller than the raw interest-rate comparison suggests. Facilitators should let the
room reach the "B wins by a landslide" conclusion out loud before D3/D4 corrects the scale of it.

**A second trap sits directly underneath the first, and it is the more dangerous one because D1–D4
appear to have already "solved" it.** A room that works all the way through D1–D4 correctly lands
on CIC's company-level cash flow: Option A JA$85,045.83/month, Option B JA$96,983.33/month — Option
B ahead by 14.0%. A Hero who stops there concludes Option B is modestly, comfortably the better
financial choice for David, full stop. It is not. Option B only exists because JTL buys 50 percent
of CIC (Appendix D) — so David's own personal take-home under Option B is half of CIC's cash flow,
JA$48,491.67/month, not the full JA$96,983.33. Compared honestly, David personally keeps
JA$85,045.83 under Option A versus JA$48,491.67 under Option B — Option A ahead by roughly **75%**,
the opposite side from the company-level comparison and well outside SPEC §2's 20% balance cap.
This is exactly why `case.json.primary_metric` is scoped, explicitly, to CIC's cash flow *before*
any split of profit — the case is graded on the company-level comparison D1–D4 actually build, not
on David's personal payout, because the source case never specifies a dividend policy for JTL's
stake. But a pitch that treats "Option B wins by 14%" as settling the question for David personally
has made exactly the same kind of error as the first trap: comparing the wrong number with total
confidence. Socratic question 6 exists to force this recomputation. Argument B's real defense is not
that it out-earns Option A for David — on that comparison it clearly does not — but that guaranteed
financing and a real JA$677,000 cushion (versus Option A's JA$0 cushion; see the Twist) are worth
giving up half the company's upside for. Let the room reach "B wins" a second time before this
lands.

## Sensitivity note

A business-financing case: no illness/death/severe-hardship content requiring a SPEC.md §6 flag,
though "David risking his life savings" and "giving up half his company" are real personal-stakes
material worth naming gently for a room that includes anyone with family entrepreneurship history.

**Assumption-sensitivity disclosure (required reading before this discussion):** the case's one
genuine load-bearing stated assumption — Option A's 25% financing rate (Appendix F, MA1) — is the
dial in this case's whole company-level comparison. See `cases/caribbean-internet-cafe/sensitivity_model.py` and
`case.json.balance.stability` for the exact ±50% flex results before defending any specific margin
number in the room. This case's calendar-conversion assumption (MA3, weeks per month) and its
staff-hours reading (MA4, added 2026-08-17 after the Tier 2 P1 cold-solve surfaced the ambiguity)
are both much smaller, secondary dials by comparison — neither flips the winner nor breaches the
20% cap across a ±50% sweep.

**Metric-scope disclosure (required reading before this discussion, added 2026-08-17 after Tier 2
P2 both-sides-solve):** `case.json.primary_metric` is CIC's company-level cash flow, computed
*before* any split of profit between David and JTL — the comparison D1–D4 actually build, margin
14.0% in Option B's favor. A separate, equally real comparison — David's own personal take-home
once Option B's 50% ownership split (Appendix D) is applied — is NOT the graded metric, uses no
extra assumption, and swings the other way by roughly 75% in Option A's favor
(`case.json.balance.personal_take_home_note`, `case.json.derived.david_personal_take_home_*`,
`sensitivity_model.py`'s `david_personal_take_home()`). Facilitators must be explicit in the room
about which comparison is meant before defending any "the margin is 14%" or "Option B wins"
statement — see "The trap, named explicitly" above and Socratic question 6.

## 45-Minute Socratic Plan

| Time | Move |
|---|---|
| 0:00–3:00 | Cold-call launch (Overview read aloud), 30-second commitments, side-of-room vote — no explanation (Anchor 1) |
| 3:00–10:00 | "What business is David really in?" — force a second move, define the real question (Anchor 2) |
| 10:00–15:00 | Name a process; rank the top-5 variables that matter; hand out Appendices B–E; D1–D2 develop (Anchor 3, Data Deep Dive begins) |
| **15:00** | **DROP THE TWIST** — delivered slowly, both halves |
| 15:00–20:00 | Immediate recommit: who reverses, and why? "What just broke?" |
| 20:00–30:00 | Threaded deep conflict: (1) the interest-rate trap vs. full cost structure (D2 vs. D3/D4), (2) ownership/control vs. cash flow, (3) survival under the pessimistic scenario for either option |
| 30:00–45:00 | Close, re-vote, reflection, lessons learned |
