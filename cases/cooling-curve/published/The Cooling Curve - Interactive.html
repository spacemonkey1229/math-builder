Overview

Advait Rao holds two cold-plate samples under the fluorescent lights of the data center's mechanical mezzanine, one in each palm. The GPU racks arriving next quarter aren't the same profile the site was built for — hotter, denser, closer together — and the liquid cold plates bolted to each accelerator are the only thing standing between them and a thermal throttle. He turns the first sample over: dozens of tiny four-sided points machined into the copper base, catching the light like a bed of nails. Then the second: cleaner, simpler, tapered pins standing in neat rows. Both hum faintly on the demo rig behind him, hooked to a chiller loop cycling through clear tubing.

His manager wants the fin spec locked before the mold order goes out — once the tooling is cut, it doesn't change until the next hardware refresh. Somewhere down the mezzanine a fan array kicks on, loud enough that Advait feels it more than hears it. He sets the two samples side by side on the workbench, next to a laptop open to a blank spec sheet, and reaches for his calipers.

Background

Advait Rao is a Facilities Engineer at a hyperscale AI data center responsible for thermal design sign-off on new compute deployments. Next quarter's GPU racks are rated to draw more power per rack than anything the site has previously cooled, and the liquid cold plates mounted directly on each accelerator carry the last line of defense against throttling: fin geometry machined into the plate's underside, designed to pull heat into the coolant loop as efficiently as possible for the material spent.

Two fin geometries are under consideration for the new cold-plate order, and Advait's recommendation sets the tooling for the entire deployment (see Appendix E).

Option A — The Pyramid Array. Each fin is machined as a four-sided pyramid rising from the cold-plate base. A pyramidal fin exposes more per fin than a simpler shape of the same footprint and height, and the ASHRAE TC9.9 data-center thermal guidelines identify exposed fin surface area as the dominant factor in a fin's heat-dissipation capacity (see Appendix A). But a pyramidal fin's four flat, angled faces require a more complex mold than a shape of revolution, and at data-center volumes — tens of thousands of fins across a single rack deployment (see Appendix E) — mold complexity shows up directly in per-fin manufacturing cost (see Appendix C).

Option B — The Cone Standoffs. Each fin is machined as a simple cone, tapering from a circular base to a point. A cone mold is a shape of revolution, tooled by a faster and cheaper process than a multi-face pyramid mold (see Appendix C), but a cone's curved surface encloses less total surface area than a pyramid of matching base footprint and height, which caps how much heat each fin can dissipate (see Appendix A, Appendix B).

Both fin geometries fit inside the same cold-plate footprint and the same coolant-loop flow budget the site's chiller plant already supports — geometry, not flow capacity, is what is on the table. What the deployment actually needs is not the fin that dissipates the most heat, and not the fin that costs the least to make, but the fin that dissipates the most heat per dollar spent producing it, at the volume this deployment requires (see Appendix C, Appendix D, Appendix E). Vendor cold-plate technical datasheets and published cooling-infrastructure cost benchmarks anchor the figures Advait is working from; every hard number in this case is either sourced with a citation or, where no public figure exists, stated openly as a Modeling Assumption once research is complete (see the Modeling Assumptions and Appendix F below).

Appendix A — ASHRAE TC9.9 Thermal Guidelines: Heat Dissipation and Surface Area

Liquid cold-plate fins pull heat from the accelerator into the coolant loop across their exposed surface — the more surface area a fin presents to the coolant at a given coolant supply temperature, the more heat it can carry away per unit time. ASHRAE TC9.9 publishes recommended coolant supply temperature ranges and cooling-capacity guidance for liquid-cooled data-center hardware.

Cold-plate fins of this kind are typically machined or cast from a high-conductivity metal. Manufacturing cost per fin has two components: the material used (a function of the fin's volume) and the mold itself, amortized across the number of fins that mold produces before replacement.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Volume per fin (D1)

    Pyramid volume = (1/3) × base edge² × height. Cone volume = (1/3) × π × radius² × height, where radius = base diameter ÷ 2. Use Appendix B's dimensions. Answer in in³, to 6 decimal places.

    Step 2 — Exposed surface area per fin (D2)

    Only the exposed (coolant-facing) surface counts — the base is bonded to the cold plate. Pyramid: 4 triangular faces, area = 2 × edge × slant height, where slant height = √(height² + (edge/2)²). Cone: lateral area = π × radius × slant height, where slant height = √(radius² + height²). Answer in in², to 6 decimal places.

    Step 3 — Fully-loaded manufacturing cost per fin (D3)

    Material cost = your D1 volume × $2.12/in³ (Appendix C). Mold-amortized cost = mold cost (Appendix C, MA5/MA6) ÷ fins per mold (MA7, 300,000). Fully-loaded cost per fin = material cost + mold-amortized cost. Answer in dollars, to 6 decimal places.

    Step 4 — Watts per dollar (D4)

    Watts per fin = your D2 surface area × 13 W/in² (Appendix A). Watts per dollar = watts per fin ÷ your D3 cost per fin. The sliders below let you flex MA5, MA6, and MA7 — the case's own most load-bearing assumptions (see the Margin Note above) — and watch each option's watts-per-dollar figure recompute live.     Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    Someone on the procurement team points out that Appendix E's entire deployment — 16 racks × 72 cold plates × 48 fins — needs exactly 55,296 fins. Appendix C's mold-life figure (MA7) assumes a mold produces 300,000 fins before it's replaced. This one deployment doesn't even use up a fifth of a single mold's assumed lifetime — and the Overview says the tooling doesn't change until the next hardware refresh, which is rated for a different, hotter rack profile than this fin geometry was sized for.

    If this mold might never get a second production run, is spreading its cost over 300,000 fins still the right way to price it into your recommendation?
