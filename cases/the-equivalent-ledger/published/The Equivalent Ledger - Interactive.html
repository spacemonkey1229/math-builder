Overview

The overnight reconciliation run finished four minutes ago, and Priya Chandran is still staring at the diff. Two windows sit open side by side on her monitor: the pull request an AI coding assistant opened last week, rewriting the ugly, decade-old formula that decides how many dollars get swept between two suspense accounts every time the ledger closes with unmatched line items — and the on-call engineer's message from six minutes ago: "ran it against the last two real batches we had sitting around, numbers match exactly both times. approving now so we make the freeze window."

Down the hall, the deployment freeze for quarter-end regulatory close starts before sunrise. If the rewritten formula isn't approved before then, it waits out the freeze, and Priya's team keeps hand-patching the old code's known quirks in the meantime. She has read the AI's rewrite twice. It is shorter, cleaner, and it produced the same number as the old formula on both of the two batches anyone actually tried it against. She has also spent eleven years around systems where "it matched on the data we happened to have" and "it is actually the same formula" turned out, more than once, to be two very different sentences.

Her cursor sits over the Approve button. Behind her, the on-call engineer is already drafting the deployment ticket.

Background

Priya Chandran is the verification lead for the reconciliation engine at Ledgerline, a company that operates the overnight settlement ledger connecting a network of regional banks — the nightly process by which each bank's payments to the others are finalized and the money is considered actually moved, not just promised. Every night, Ledgerline's system tallies which transactions matched cleanly between partner banks and which did not; for every batch of unmatched line items (a transaction one bank's records show but the other bank's don't, or where the two banks disagree on the amount), a dollar amount gets swept between two internal suspense accounts — temporary holding accounts that carry a discrepancy until someone sorts it out — to keep both banks' books balanced until the mismatch is resolved by hand. That sweep amount has been computed, since before Priya joined the team, by a formula nobody fully trusts anymore — hand-derived years ago by an engineer who has since left, buried in legacy code nobody wants to touch (SEE APPENDIX A).

Last week, as part of a broader effort to modernize that legacy module, an AI coding assistant proposed a rewritten version of the sweep formula. This kind of AI-assisted rewrite of deterministic financial code is a live, widely discussed tension in enterprise engineering right now — a system of record has to reconcile exactly, every time, while the tools being used to modernize it are built to be merely probably right (SEE APPENDIX A). The on-call engineer ran the rewritten formula against the two most recent real unmatched-item batches Ledgerline's own logs had on hand — the same practice software teams call regression testing: re-running a change against cases the old code already handled correctly, to check nothing broke — and it produced the exact same dollar sweep as the original formula on both (SEE APPENDIX B).

Priya's team has narrowed the decision to two named paths. The Regression Pass means approving the rewritten formula for production now, on the strength of that two-batch match — fast, and it clears before the deployment freeze (a stretch of time when engineers are barred from pushing any code change to the reconciliation engine) locks all changes out ahead of quarter-end regulatory close, the periodic financial-reporting deadline when a bank must be able to show regulators its numbers were produced by unchanged, already-verified code (SEE APPENDIX C). The Full Factor means holding the deployment and spending the day having senior engineers fully factor both formulas by hand, to prove algebraically that they produce an identical dollar sweep for every possible batch size — not only the two batch sizes that happened to already get tested (SEE APPENDIX C).

The reconciliation engine is a system of record: every dollar it sweeps has to reconcile exactly, for every partner bank, every night, indefinitely — not only for the two nights someone happened to check. If the rewritten formula is wrong for batch sizes Ledgerline has not yet seen in production, it will not announce itself; it will simply mis-sweep dollars between suspense accounts, silently, until an auditor or a partner bank eventually notices the books don't balance and raises it through the bank's own audit escalation process — the formal chain by which a discovered error gets reported upward and investigated (SEE APPENDIX D).     Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — D1: Factor O(n) by pulling out its greatest common factor

    O(n) = 48n² + 180n. Prime-factor 48 and 180, find their greatest common factor, then write O(n) as GCF × n × (remaining binomial). Enter the GCF and the two remaining coefficients (so O(n) = GCF·n·(a·n + b)).

    Step 2 — D2: Factor R(n) the same way

    R(n) = 54n² + 96n + 240. Prime-factor 54, 96, and 240, find their greatest common factor, then write R(n) as GCF × (remaining trinomial). Enter the GCF and the three remaining coefficients (so R(n) = GCF·(pn² + qn + r)).

    Step 3 — D3: Sample-test table

    Evaluate O(n) and R(n) at all three batch sizes in Appendix B, and find the percent gap between them at n = 25.

    This calculator checks your arithmetic — it will not tell you whether that means the formulas are "the same." That's D4.

    Step 4 — D4: Proof verdict from the factored forms only

    Using ONLY your D1/D2 factored forms (not D3's plugged-in numbers), decide: does every term of O(n) share a factor of n? Does every term of R(n)? Are O(n) and R(n) the same expression for every possible n?

    Step 5 — D5: Cost comparison

    Using Appendix C's verification-labor figures and Appendix D's risk-exposure figures, calculate Option A's expected cost and Option B's verification cost, and the percent gap between them.

    D6's recommendation memo (using both D4's proof and D5's cost comparison) is written outside this notebook — that reasoning is still yours to make.
