Case Study: Boulderfield

MS | Math | Case Study | 2026-27 | Adventure

## Overview

The radio on Talia Ferro's belt crackles at 2:14 a.m. *"Dispatch, we have voice contact. Male,
twenty-six, ankle injury, can't bear weight, last known position near the summit approach."* Talia
is already standing at the Longs Peak Trailhead kiosk, headlamp off to save the battery, staring
at the inside of her own helmet — where a strip of athletic tape holds four numbers she wrote
there herself, in Sharpie, after four different seasons of climbing both routes on this mountain
solo, stopwatch running, just to know for certain how long each one actually takes.

Two duffel bags of rescue gear sit at her boots. One is packed for the Keyhole — ropes coiled but
unlikely to be needed, headlamps, the radio repeater. The other is packed for the Loft — the same
gear, plus the extra rigging gear her team carries whenever the route isn't cairned the
whole way up. Her radio is still open. Somewhere above her, past 12,000 feet, wind is moving
across rock she cannot see.

Dispatch keys up again. *"Talia, incident command needs your call. Which way is the team going?"*

She looks at the two numbers taped inside her helmet.

## Background

Longs Peak (14,259 ft) is the highest point in Rocky Mountain National Park, and its standard
approach trailhead sits at 9,400 ft (SEE APPENDIX A) [S1]. Rocky Mountain National Park's own
guidance for climbers describes the Keyhole Route's round trip as a 10-to-15-hour undertaking and
recommends starting by 3:00 a.m. specifically so a party is off the exposed upper mountain before
the thunderstorms that build most afternoons in June, July, and August (SEE APPENDIX C) [S3].
Search-and-rescue operations on Longs Peak are real and recurring — the park has publicly reported
multiple rescue and search operations on the peak's upper routes in recent seasons (SEE APPENDIX
E) [S9].

Talia Ferro is the lead Search and Rescue ranger for the Longs Peak zone. She is not the one
who decides *whether* a rescue happens — that call was already made the moment the injured
climber's party made voice contact with dispatch. Her call is narrower and, in the ranger station,
considered just as consequential: which of the two routes that reach the upper mountain does she
send her four-person ground team up, right now, tonight. Talia spent three seasons as a backcountry
wilderness EMT before she became a SAR ranger, and she has never once sent a team up a route timed
only from a guidebook page — every season since, she has personally climbed both the Keyhole and
the Loft solo, off-duty, stopwatch running from the trailhead kiosk to the point where each route
leaves the shared lower trail, because a number she measured herself is the only kind she fully
trusts when a climber's condition is what's riding on it.

Both of Longs Peak's most-used routes share the same lower trail out of the trailhead, through
treeline, and up to a boulder-strewn basin called **the Boulderfield** — a flat, exposed staging
area at roughly 12,760 ft where climbing parties often camp before a summit push (SEE APPENDIX A)
[S1]. Above the Boulderfield, the routes split:

**The Keyhole Route** continues on the maintained, cairned standard trail — Rocky Mountain
National Park's own signed and most heavily used route to the summit, 14.5 route-miles round trip
with 5,100 ft of total elevation gain (SEE APPENDIX A) [S1].

**The Loft** splits west below the Boulderfield and climbs a steeper, shorter line past the base of
Mount Meeker — 13.0 route-miles round trip with 5,300 ft of total elevation gain, unmaintained and
largely unmarked above the point where it leaves the standard trail, with the same "considerable"
rockfall potential — the park's own scale for how likely loose rock is to fall on climbers — and
high exposure rating — how far and how dangerous a fall from the trail itself would be — the park
assigns to Longs Peak's technical routes (SEE
APPENDIX A) [S2].

Every number in this case is either cited to a real published source or disclosed as a stated
Modeling Assumption (SEE APPENDICES B, C AND D), with every citation collected in Appendix E — never
invented quietly. The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed
in Appendix F, which is where Challenge 1 sends you.

## Dilemma

You are Talia Ferro.

Do you:

A.) Send the team up **The Keyhole Route** — the longer, maintained, cairned standard trail, 14.5
route-miles round trip with 5,100 ft of elevation gain OR

B.) Send the team up **The Loft** — the shorter, steeper, unmaintained route, 13.0 route-miles
round trip with 5,300 ft of elevation gain, that requires extra time for route-finding above the
Boulderfield

## Appendix B — Timing Model

**Naismith's Rule** (1892): allow one hour of travel time for every 3 miles of horizontal distance,
plus one additional hour for every 2,000 ft of elevation gain (SEE APPENDIX E) [S4]. In formula
form, with *d* = one-way distance in miles and *g* = elevation gain in feet:

```
Naismith time (hours) = d / 3 + g / 2000
```

**This case's own timing adjustments**, applied on top of the Naismith estimate (SEE APPENDIX D
for basis):

| Adjustment | Applies to | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Night / full-gear rescue-pace multiplier | Both routes | ×1.3 [MA1] |
| Route-finding / exposure penalty | The Loft only, above the Boulderfield split | +55 minutes [MA2] |

Total adjusted time = (Naismith time × night-pace multiplier), converted to minutes, plus the
route-finding penalty where it applies.

## Appendix C — Temperature, Wind Chill, and the Turnaround Guidance

**Standard environmental lapse rate**: air temperature drops by approximately 3.5°F for every
1,000 ft of elevation gained, under typical (non-inversion) atmospheric conditions [S6].

**National Weather Service Wind Chill Temperature Index** (valid for air temperatures at or below
50°F and wind speeds above 3 mph) [S7]:

```
Wind Chill (°F) = 35.74 + 0.6215·T − 35.75·(V^0.16) + 0.4275·T·(V^0.16)
```

where *T* is air temperature in °F and *V* is wind speed in mph. This case supplies a pre-computed
wind-factor lookup table (below) so the exponent itself does not need to be evaluated by hand:

| Wind speed, V (mph) | V^0.16 (case-supplied) |
|---|---|
| 25 | 1.6735 |
| 35 | 1.7663 |

**Rocky Mountain National Park's own recommendation** is to start a Longs Peak climb by 3:00 a.m.
specifically to be off the exposed upper mountain before afternoon thunderstorms typically build
in June, July, and August (SEE BACKGROUND) [S3]. A widely used mountaineering rule of thumb built
on that same seasonal pattern is to plan to be off an exposed summit by noon [S8].
