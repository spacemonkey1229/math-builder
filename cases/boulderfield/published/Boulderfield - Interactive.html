Overview

The radio on Talia Ferro's belt crackles at 2:14 a.m. "Dispatch, we have voice contact. Male, twenty-six, ankle injury, can't bear weight, last known position near the summit approach." Talia is already standing at the Longs Peak Trailhead kiosk, headlamp off to save the battery, staring at the inside of her own helmet — where a strip of athletic tape holds four numbers she wrote there herself, in Sharpie, after four different seasons of climbing both routes on this mountain solo, stopwatch running, just to know for certain how long each one actually takes.

Two duffel bags of rescue gear sit at her boots. One is packed for the Keyhole — ropes coiled but unlikely to be needed, headlamps, the radio repeater. The other is packed for the Loft — the same gear, plus the extra rigging gear her team carries whenever the route isn't cairned the whole way up. Her radio is still open. Somewhere above her, past 12,000 feet, wind is moving across rock she cannot see.

Dispatch keys up again. "Talia, incident command needs your call. Which way is the team going?"

She looks at the two numbers taped inside her helmet.

Background

Longs Peak (14,259 ft) is the highest point in Rocky Mountain National Park, and its standard approach trailhead sits at 9,400 ft (SEE APPENDIX A). Rocky Mountain National Park's own guidance for climbers describes the Keyhole Route's round trip as a 10-to-15-hour undertaking and recommends starting by 3:00 a.m. specifically so a party is off the exposed upper mountain before the thunderstorms that build most afternoons in June, July, and August (SEE APPENDIX C). Search-and-rescue operations on Longs Peak are real and recurring — the park has publicly reported multiple rescue and search operations on the peak's upper routes in recent seasons (SEE APPENDIX E).

Talia Ferro is the lead Search and Rescue ranger for the Longs Peak zone. She is not the one who decides whether a rescue happens — that call was already made the moment the injured climber's party made voice contact with dispatch. Her call is narrower and, in the ranger station, considered just as consequential: which of the two routes that reach the upper mountain does she send her four-person ground team up, right now, tonight. Talia spent three seasons as a backcountry wilderness EMT before she became a SAR ranger, and she has never once sent a team up a route timed only from a guidebook page — every season since, she has personally climbed both the Keyhole and the Loft solo, off-duty, stopwatch running from the trailhead kiosk to the point where each route leaves the shared lower trail, because a number she measured herself is the only kind she fully trusts when a climber's condition is what's riding on it.

Both of Longs Peak's most-used routes share the same lower trail out of the trailhead, through treeline, and up to a boulder-strewn basin called the Boulderfield — a flat, exposed staging area at roughly 12,760 ft where climbing parties often camp before a summit push (SEE APPENDIX A). Above the Boulderfield, the routes split:

The Keyhole Route continues on the maintained, cairned standard trail — Rocky Mountain National Park's own signed and most heavily used route to the summit, 14.5 route-miles round trip with 5,100 ft of total elevation gain (SEE APPENDIX A).

The Loft splits west below the Boulderfield and climbs a steeper, shorter line past the base of Mount Meeker — 13.0 route-miles round trip with 5,300 ft of total elevation gain, unmaintained and largely unmarked above the point where it leaves the standard trail, with the same "considerable" rockfall potential — the park's own scale for how likely loose rock is to fall on climbers — and high exposure rating — how far and how dangerous a fall from the trail itself would be — the park assigns to Longs Peak's technical routes (SEE APPENDIX A).

Every number in this case is either cited to a real published source or disclosed as a stated Modeling Assumption (SEE APPENDICES B, C AND D) — never invented quietly. The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in Appendix F, which is where Challenge 1 sends you.

Appendix B — Timing Model

Naismith's Rule (1892): allow one hour of travel time for every 3 miles of horizontal distance, plus one additional hour for every 2,000 ft of elevation gain [S5]. In formula form, with d = one-way distance in miles and g = elevation gain in feet:

Naismith time (hours) = d / 3 + g / 2000

This case's own timing adjustments, applied on top of the Naismith estimate (SEE APPENDIX D for basis):

AdjustmentApplies toValue

Route-finding / exposure penaltyThe Loft only, above the split+55 minutes [MA2]

Total adjusted time = (Naismith time × night-pace multiplier), converted to minutes, plus the route-finding penalty where it applies.

Appendix C — Temperature, Wind Chill, and the Turnaround Guidance

Standard environmental lapse rate: air temperature drops by approximately 3.5°F for every 1,000 ft of elevation gained, under typical (non-inversion) atmospheric conditions [S6].

National Weather Service Wind Chill Temperature Index (valid for air temperatures at or below 50°F and wind speeds above 3 mph) [S7]:

Wind Chill (°F) = 35.74 + 0.6215·T − 35.75·(V^0.16) + 0.4275·T·(V^0.16)

where T is air temperature in °F and V is wind speed in mph. This case supplies a pre-computed wind-factor lookup table so the fractional exponent itself does not need to be evaluated by hand:

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which route to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — One-way route breakdown (D1)

    Divide each route's round-trip distance by 2 to get the one-way distance. Elevation gain is already stated as the one-way ascent total.

    Why one-way at all? Appendix A's mileage and elevation figures are round trip — there and back. But the question Talia actually has to answer tonight is how long it takes a team to reach the patient, not how long the full mission takes including the walk back out. Every timing challenge from here on (D2–D4) is built on the one-way numbers you calculate in this step, so get the division right before moving on.

    Step 2 — Naismith's Rule raw time estimate (D2)

    Substitute each route's one-way distance and elevation gain into Naismith's Rule: d/3 + g/2000. Round to four decimal places.

    What Naismith's Rule actually computes: it's a rule of thumb dating back to 1892 (see Appendix E's source), built from two ratios — a horizontal hiking pace (1 hour per 3 miles) and a climbing pace (1 hour per 2,000 ft of gain) — added together. That's all it knows about. It has no idea this team is moving in the dark, carrying full rescue gear, or that one of these two routes has no cairns to follow above the Boulderfield. Treat this step's output as a starting estimate, not a finished answer — Step 3 is where those missing real-world conditions get added back in.

    Step 3 — Adjusted total time and margin (D3)

    Apply the night-pace multiplier (Appendix B) to both raw Naismith times, add The Loft's route-finding penalty to Option B only, convert to minutes, then compute the margin as a percent of the slower route's time.

    This is where Step 2's raw estimate becomes a real-world number. The night-pace multiplier (Appendix B, MA1) accounts for reduced visibility and a loaded pack, and it applies to both routes equally, since both teams travel at night. The route-finding penalty (MA2) applies to The Loft only, because that's the route with no cairns above the split — it's the arithmetic version of the illustration in the Background section. Once you have both routes' adjusted times in minutes, the percent margin tells you how big the gap between them actually is, scaled so it's comparable regardless of which route ends up slower.

    Step 4 — Required-pace equation (D4)

    Using Appendix D's MA5 deadline, write and solve an equation for the minimum average moving pace (mph) each route needs, holding elevation-gain time, the night-pace multiplier, and (for The Loft) the route-finding penalty fixed.

    Steps 1–3 answered "how long will this take." Step 4 flips the question around: given a hard deadline (Appendix C's turnaround guidance, fixed as Appendix D's MA5), how fast would the team's average moving pace need to be to still make it? Set the equation up with pace as the unknown, keep every other Appendix B constant fixed, and solve for it — a required pace that turns out to be unrealistically fast is itself a piece of evidence worth writing about in your pitch.

    Step 5 — Signed temperature & wind chill (D5)

    Use Appendix C's lapse rate to find each location's air temperature, then the NWS formula (with the case-supplied wind-factor table) to find each wind chill. Both are signed integers. Then find each location's integer difference (air temperature minus wind chill).

    This step is separate from the time comparison in Steps 1–3 — it's about what condition the team (and the patient) will be in once they arrive, not how fast they got there. Air temperature falls as elevation increases (the lapse rate), and wind chill falls further on top of that as wind speed increases, so expect signed — possibly negative — values at both locations. Work the lapse-rate subtraction first, then substitute into the wind chill formula using the case-supplied V^0.16 values so you don't need to evaluate the exponent by hand.
