Overview

Bang. Bang. Bang. Rulon pounds on the bathroom door. “Heather! You’ve been in there forever, let me in!” “Go away, I’m still getting ready,” Heather snaps back through the door. George and Olivia come running down the hall, already late, and start knocking too. Frustrated, Heather storms out — and collides with Thomas, who was rounding the corner to brush his teeth. Thomas hits the carpet and starts wailing. Everyone else comes running to find a horrified Heather kneeling next to him.

It’s been like this every morning since the flood. The upstairs sink got left running for an entire school day, and by the time anyone noticed, water had soaked through the ceiling, down the stairs, and into the kitchen and the guest bathroom below. A cleanup crew installed a wall of industrial fans that have been roaring in the hallway ever since, and the insurance agent already warned it’ll be months before any of it is repaired.

Will Thomason steps over the drying fans’ cords, scoops Thomas up, and waits for the crying to slow down. Everyone looks at him — Mom, all the kids, the one working bathroom door standing open behind them. “Okay,” he says. “This clearly isn’t working. Let’s all take a breath. Tonight, we sit down as a family and figure out a schedule that’s actually fair — for everyone.” Heads nod, some more reluctantly than others. Will pulls out a notepad. Tonight, he has to turn a week’s worth of frustration into one schedule the whole family can live with.

Background

Yesterday, the Thomason home flooded — Rulon had left the upstairs bathroom sink running with a washcloth in the drain, and by evening the water had ruined the upstairs bathroom, the hallway, the stairs, the downstairs kitchen, and the guest bathroom. A restoration crew installed a dozen large drying fans that will keep running for the next several days. Until repairs are finished, the family of eight has exactly one working bathroom in the house: the master bathroom (see Appendix B).

To prepare for the family meeting, Will and his wife Marta had every family member track their own morning bathroom routine for a few days — how long they actually spend showering, brushing their teeth, and on hair/makeup or shaving, and what time they need to leave the house (see Appendix A). The master bathroom itself has one shower, one bathtub (used only by two-year-old Thomas), and a water heater whose hot-water supply is limited (see Appendix C and Appendix E).

Three house rules govern how the one bathroom can be used. First, Will and Marta may use the bathroom together, at the same time, for anything — their two routines can run in parallel. Second, nobody else may be in the bathroom while another family member is showering; the bathroom otherwise serves one person at a time. Third, the family has a standing tradition of gathering together for a five-minute devotional every morning before anyone leaves the house — with the whole family in one room, no one is showering during it. Rulon’s baby brother Thomas has had his crib moved into the closet next to the master bathroom since the flood damaged his nursery, and he reliably wakes around 6:45 AM; running the shower before then risks waking him, so the family has agreed no one uses the bathroom before he’s up. That means the five-minute devotional happens right when Thomas wakes, and individual bathroom turns can’t start until it’s over — a fixed shared starting line for the whole morning (see Appendix D).

Will and Marta are now choosing between two ways to run the schedule going forward (see Appendix D). The Round-Robin gives every family member the same length of turn every day, with the order rotating each week. The Priority Line gives family members turns based on who has to leave the house earliest each morning, with turn lengths that match what each person actually says they need — even though that means some people get a lot more time than others.

It sits directly next to the master closet, where Thomas’s crib has been moved since the flood damaged his nursery.

The First Hour Rating is the amount of hot water a storage water heater can deliver in one hour, starting from a fully heated tank — it combines the tank’s stored hot water with how fast the heater can reheat more while it runs [S2]. Once that stored hot water is used up, whoever is still showering gets progressively cooler water until the tank recovers.

Appendix D — Two Scheduling Policies: The Setup

The family’s shared morning bathroom window runs from 6:50 AM — five minutes after Thomas’s usual 6:45 AM wake-up, once the family’s shared devotional is finished (see Background) — to 8:00 AM, the latest “needs to leave the house by” time in Appendix A. That’s a shared window every morning, and the bathroom can only serve one person (or the Will-and-Marta pair) at a time.

Appendix E — Real-World Context

Family showerhead flow rates vary. The EPA’s WaterSense program reports that a standard (non-water-efficient) showerhead uses about 2.5 gallons per minute, while WaterSense-labeled showerheads use no more than 2.0 gallons per minute [S3] — the Thomason master bathroom’s 2.25 GPM showerhead (Appendix C) sits between those two, a realistic mid-range fixture.

A water heater’s First Hour Rating is a standard, published spec on every residential storage water heater sold in the U.S., required for exactly the kind of planning this case asks the family to do [S2].

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — D1: The shared morning window

    The devotional ends at 6:50 AM (Appendix D). The latest leave-by time in Appendix A is 8:00 AM. How many minutes long is the shared window every family member's schedule has to fit inside?

    This only checks the window length — it doesn't build your full minute-by-minute D1 schedule for you. Use Appendix A/D to place every family member in the bathroom one at a time (remembering Will and Marta can overlap).

    Step 2 — D2: Total shower-water usage

    Add up every family member's shower minutes from Appendix A (Thomas takes a bath, not a shower — leave him out of this total). Multiply the total by the showerhead's flow rate (Appendix C) to get total gallons.

    Step 3 — D3: Hot-water availability

    Divide the water heater's First Hour Rating by the showerhead's flow rate (Appendix C) to find how many minutes of hot shower time the family actually has. Then compare it to the total shower minutes you found in Step 2.

    This checks your arithmetic, not what it means for your schedule — that's part of your pitch.

    Step 4 — D4: Allocation and the useful-minutes comparison

    Option A: divide the shared window evenly across all 8 family members — that's everyone's flat slot. Option B: give each family member a share of the window proportional to their own stated total daily need (Appendix A). For each option, add up everyone's useful minutes (the smaller of their assigned slot or their real need) to get one family total. Then find the percent difference between the two option totals.

    This checks your arithmetic, not which option you should pick — there is no single right recommendation, only correct or incorrect math.

    Step 5 — D5: Two original visual displays

    Describe each chart you built. This just confirms you made both — it can't grade a picture.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    Knock, knock. Grandma Polly and Grandpa Ben heard about the flooding and just showed up — with their suitcases. They're staying to help with repairs. Now what?

    Two more adults now need bathroom time every morning, sharing the exact same window you already built your schedule around. Neither the devotional's start time nor the family's leave-by deadlines have moved — only the number of people sharing the bathroom has.

    Good — now go back to your Step 4 numbers. Would you recompute a flat share for 10 people instead of 8? Would Option B's proportional allocation change the same way for both options, or by different amounts? That difference is the actual point of the Twist.
