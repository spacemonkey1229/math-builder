## Overview

Marisol Aguon has the appeal open in one window and the denial letter open in the other, and the
two of them don't agree with each other the way they're supposed to.

The claimant's roof came apart in a windstorm, water got into the attic before anyone could get a
tarp up, and the adjuster denied the whole thing under the flood exclusion — the clause that says
if the damage was caused by flood, the loss isn't covered. Marisol has read that clause a thousand
times. She's about to read the adjuster's reasoning for the thousand-and-first, and something
about it keeps snagging.

The adjuster didn't find flood damage and then deny the claim. He looked at the denial he wanted
to write and worked backward — water got in, so it must have been the flood clause. That is not
the same sentence read forward, and Marisol knows it isn't, even if she can't yet say cleanly why.

She pulls up the audit-standard memo she's supposed to sign off on before her afternoon call — the
one that tells the whole appeals team, going forward, exactly how to read every clause like this
one. Two drafts sit open at the bottom of her screen: one where the standard stays exactly as
written, and one where she rewrites it as its own logical mirror image, so the same clause can't
be misread in the other direction again.

Her manager's message is still sitting unread at the top of her inbox: *Need your call on the
audit standard before the call.* Marisol's cursor sits between the two drafts, and the clock in
the corner of her screen just ticked forward another minute.

## Background

Marisol Aguon is a senior claims auditor at a mid-size property and homeowners insurer. She
reviews denied claims that policyholders have formally appealed, and she is responsible for
setting the review standard the appeals team applies going forward — whichever standard she picks
becomes the rule every adjuster in her group is trained to follow, and she answers for that choice
to underwriting leadership and to the company's compliance office (SEE APPENDIX A).

The clause at the center of this file is a standard flood-damage exclusion (SEE APPENDIX A):
*if damage was caused by flood, the loss is not covered.* Adjusters are trained to apply that
sentence literally — check the cause of loss first, deny coverage when flood is the cause. This
case calls that standard the **Plain-Text Standard**. Marisol's alternative is to direct her team
to apply the clause's logical contrapositive instead — *if coverage was not denied, damage was not
caused by flood* — a standard this case calls the **Mirror-Image Standard**, built to catch
appeals where an adjuster's reasoning ran in a direction the original clause never licensed.

This is not a hypothetical problem. Company leadership has flagged that the appeals team's
justified-complaint rate has moved sharply over the last reporting year, tracking a pattern the
California Department of Insurance's own annual Consumer Complaint Study shows across several
major homeowners carriers statewide (SEE APPENDIX B). The National Association of Insurance
Commissioners' Market Conduct Annual Statement program collects a comparable claims-handling
measure nationally across 51 jurisdictions, though — as Appendix C explains — it does not publish
a complaint-specific rate for the homeowners line the way California does (SEE APPENDIX C). Neither
source proves that adjusters are confusing the
clause's converse with its true contrapositive — that specific mechanism is this case's own
modeling premise, stated here openly as a scenario assumption, not a documented finding — but the
reasoning errors it describes have real, formally recognized names in logic and in law, and a
pattern of wrongful denials carries real legal exposure for the company that lets it continue
(SEE APPENDIX D).

Marisol has one constructed log of past appeals to test both candidate standards against before
she has to send her recommendation (SEE APPENDIX E), and a hard deadline she cannot move. Whatever
she recommends becomes the appeals team's standing rule the moment she sends it.

All figures in this case are either cited to a real source (Appendix G — Sources) or stated openly
as a scenario modeling assumption (Appendix F — Modeling Assumptions) — never invented quietly.
The vocabulary and the "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in Appendix
H; that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

## Appendix A — The Policy Clause

The flood-damage exclusion clause under audit, reproduced from the standard ISO Homeowners 3 —
Special Form (the most widely issued homeowners policy form in the United States), Section I —
Exclusions, heading "Water" [S4][S5][S6]:

> **SECTION I — EXCLUSIONS**
>
> We do not insure for loss caused directly or indirectly by any of the following. Such loss is
> excluded regardless of any other cause or event contributing concurrently or in any sequence to
> the loss.
>
> **3. Water**
>
> a. Flood, surface water, waves, including tidal wave and tsunami, tides, tidal water, overflow
> of any body of water, or spray from any of these, all whether or not driven by wind, including
> storm surge.

Confidence note: the "Water" heading's definitional list (the clause's own definition of "flood")
is quoted verbatim from a footnoted legal white paper's reproduction of the ISO HO3 form language
[S4], and a second, independent legal-analysis source confirms this same category still governs
under the current ISO HO 00 03 (edition 03 22, © 2021) form, in the same Section I — Exclusions
location [S5]. The anti-concurrent-causation lead-in sentence ("We do not insure for loss caused
directly or indirectly...") is standard, widely-reproduced ISO homeowners-form language, confirmed
verbatim by legal commentary discussing its enforcement [S6] — its exact placement was not
independently confirmed against a specific current-edition page. All three fragments are assembled
here as a single clause for classroom use; see Appendix G — Sources for full citations.

For reference, the clause's antecedent (p) and consequent (q):

| Symbol | Statement |
|---|---|
| p | Damage was caused by flood |
| q | Coverage is denied |

The clause as written is the conditional p → q. Marisol's two candidate standards apply this
clause and its logical relatives differently to the same appeal file (SEE APPENDICES D and E).

## Appendix D — Legal & Regulatory Context

Two real, formally recognized reasoning errors bear directly on Marisol's decision: **affirming
the consequent** (treating a conditional's converse as if it were logically equivalent to the
original statement) and **denying the antecedent** (treating a conditional's inverse the same
way). Both are standard, textbook fallacy names in formal logic — the Ninth Circuit's own case
library keeps a subject file connecting the name "denying the antecedent" to *Carver v. Lehman*,
No. 06-35176 (9th Cir. 2008) [S7], though the fallacy's name does not appear in the opinion's own
text itself (SEE APPENDIX G, S7 note) — the case names are cited here for their formal-logic
content, not as a claim about what any specific opinion's text says.

First-party bad-faith claim handling is a real, litigated cause of action under California law —
California Civil Jury Instructions No. 2332, "Bad Faith (First Party) — Failure to Properly
Investigate Claim" [S8] — with real governing precedent (*Hangarter v. Provident Life and
Accident Insurance Co.*, 373 F.3d 998, Ninth Circuit, 2004) [S9]. A documented pattern of
wrongly-denied claims, if the review
standard behind it can be shown to be unsound, is the mechanism by which an audit-standard choice
like Marisol's becomes exposure that reaches well past any single appeal file.

A comparable pattern — automated or standardized claim-review processes producing a high rate of
denials that are later overturned on appeal, discovered only after regulators or plaintiffs'
counsel got there first — has already drawn federal scrutiny and litigation in health insurance, a
different line of business from the property/homeowners scenario in this case. That is offered
here only as context for why getting the review standard right, before a pattern like this
appears in a company's own numbers, is a real and current concern in the insurance industry — not
as data this case's own metric is computed from.
