Case Study: The Marginal Call
MS | Math | Case Study | 2026 | Adventure

## Overview

The shop floor still smells like wet sawdust and machine oil when Renata Osei's phone lights up for the third time in an hour — the mill, again, wanting an answer she doesn't have yet. Through the window of her small office she can see the crew mid-shift, stacking finished chair frames near the loading dock, oblivious to the number sitting open on her screen: the hotel chain's purchase order, the biggest single order the shop has ever signed. Black walnut, all of it, FSC-certified, and there is exactly one mill drying run left this season that can supply it before the ship date.

Her laptop has two tabs open. One is the deposit form for the batch size they always order — the one her credit line covers without a second thought. The other is a spreadsheet she has been rebuilding all morning, chasing the point where a bigger batch actually starts paying for itself. That number keeps landing above what her credit line covers on its own, which means a call to the bank — a call that could take days to clear, if it clears at all before someone else locks the mill's last slot.

Her operations partner leans into the doorway. "The mill needs the batch size by end of day. Not tomorrow." She nods without looking up. Behind her, out on the floor, a table saw rises and falls, rises and falls, and she still hasn't touched either tab.

## Background

Renata Osei is the operations manager of a small furniture workshop that has spent a decade building custom pieces to order, never before at the scale this order requires. A regional hotel chain has placed a holiday bulk order for guest-room furniture — every piece specified in FSC-certified black walnut — with a firm five-week ship date. The workshop's mill has exactly one more kiln-drying run scheduled this season that can deliver certified black walnut in time, and Renata has learned, informally, that a national furniture chain is also bidding for space in that same run (SEE APPENDIX D).

Renata and her operations partner have started calling the choice by the two batch sizes on the table. **The Steady Order** means locking in the shop's usual run size — the board footage they have ordered from this mill for years, priced and financed the way it has always been financed (SEE APPENDIX A, APPENDIX B). Their existing line of credit covers that deposit without incident. **The Full Kiln** means locking in a much larger run — large enough to claim the mill's volume price break and spread the run's fixed setup cost over more board feet, which lowers the average cost of every board foot produced (SEE APPENDIX A) — but large enough that the required deposit exceeds what the credit line covers on its own, requiring a same-day bank approval that is not guaranteed to clear before the mill's last slot fills (SEE APPENDIX D).

A larger run does not automatically mean a cheaper order, however. The hotel's order and the shop's other standing accounts can only absorb so many finished walnut board feet within the five-week window (SEE APPENDIX C); any board footage locked in beyond that amount sits as unsold inventory, tying up cash and carrying its own cost for as long as it goes unsold (SEE APPENDIX C). Both the run's total cost and the cost of any unsold leftover scale with the batch size Renata locks in today — before she knows whether the bank call clears, and before she knows whether the mill's last slot is even still open. The vocabulary and "how to…" procedures this case will ask you to research are listed in (SEE APPENDIX F); every priced or sourced figure is documented in (SEE APPENDIX E).

## Dilemma

You are Renata Osei.
Do you:
A.) Lock in The Steady Order — the shop's usual batch size, financed comfortably within the existing credit line OR
B.) Lock in The Full Kiln — a larger batch size that lowers the average cost per board foot, financed only if a same-day bank call clears in time
