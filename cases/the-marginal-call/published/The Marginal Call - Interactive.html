Overview

The shop floor still smells like wet sawdust and machine oil when Renata Osei's phone lights up for the third time in an hour — the mill, again, wanting an answer she doesn't have yet. Through the window of her small office she can see the crew mid-shift, stacking finished chair frames near the loading dock, oblivious to the number sitting open on her screen: the hotel chain's purchase order, the biggest single order the shop has ever signed. Black walnut, all of it, FSC-certified, and there is exactly one mill drying run left this season that can supply it before the ship date.

Her laptop has two tabs open. One is the deposit form for the batch size they always order — the one her credit line covers without a second thought. The other is a spreadsheet she has been rebuilding all morning, chasing the point where a bigger batch actually starts paying for itself. That number keeps landing above what her credit line covers on its own, which means a call to the bank — a call that could take days to clear, if it clears at all before someone else locks the mill's last slot.

Her operations partner leans into the doorway. “The mill needs the batch size by end of day. Not tomorrow.” She nods without looking up. Behind her, out on the floor, a table saw rises and falls, rises and falls, and she still hasn't touched either tab.

Background

Renata Osei is the operations manager of a small furniture workshop that has spent a decade building custom pieces to order, never before at the scale this order requires. A regional hotel chain has placed a holiday bulk order for guest-room furniture — every piece specified in FSC-certifiedA label from the Forest Stewardship Council certifying that wood was harvested from responsibly managed forests.Open a source on this → black walnut — with a firm five-week ship date. The workshop's mill has exactly one more kiln-dryingA controlled-heat process used to dry lumber to a target moisture content faster and more evenly than air drying alone.Open a source on this → run scheduled this season that can deliver certified black walnut in time, and Renata has learned, informally, that a national furniture chain is also bidding for space in that same run (SEE APPENDIX D).

Renata and her operations partner have started calling the choice by the two batch sizes on the table. The Steady Order means locking in the shop's usual run size — the board footageA unit of lumber volume equal to a board 12 inches by 12 inches by 1 inch thick -- the standard way hardwood is priced and ordered.Open a source on this → they have ordered from this mill for years, priced and financed the way it has always been financed (SEE APPENDIX A, APPENDIX B). Their existing line of creditA flexible loan arrangement letting a borrower draw funds up to a set limit as needed, paying interest only on the amount actually drawn.Open a source on this → covers that deposit without incident. The Full Kiln means locking in a much larger run — large enough to claim the mill's volume price break and spread the run's fixed setup cost over more board feet, which lowers the average cost of every board foot produced (SEE APPENDIX A) — but large enough that the required deposit exceeds what the credit line covers on its own, requiring a same-day bank approval that is not guaranteed to clear before the mill's last slot fills (SEE APPENDIX D).

A larger run does not automatically mean a cheaper order, however. The hotel's order and the shop's other standing accounts can only absorb so many finished walnut board feet within the five-week window (SEE APPENDIX C); any board footage locked in beyond that amount sits as unsold inventory, tying up cash and carrying its own cost for as long as it goes unsold (SEE APPENDIX C). Both the run's total cost and the cost of any unsold leftover scale with the batch size Renata locks in today — before she knows whether the bank call clears, and before she knows whether the mill's last slot is even still open.     Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — D1: Build the total production-cost function C(x)

    Using Appendix A's fixed cost, variable cost, and overtime coefficient, evaluate C(x) = F + vx + kx² at each real batch size on the table.

    Step 2 — D2: Quotient rule — find x* and classify it

    Differentiate A(x) = C(x)/x with the quotient rule, set A′(x) = 0, and solve for x*. Then use the first derivative test to say whether x* is a minimum.

    Step 3 — D3: Average-cost comparison table

    Evaluate A(x) = C(x)/x at x*, The Steady Order, and The Full Kiln. Then compute the raw percent gap between the two real options.

    This calculator checks your arithmetic — it will not tell you which option to recommend. That's your D5/pitch to make.

    Step 4 — D4: Net cost per board foot actually sold

    Using Appendix C's sellable limit and carrying-cost rate, find how many board feet (if any) of each option go unsold, fold that carrying cost into the total, and divide by board feet actually sold.

    Step 5 — D6: Financing memo

    Using Appendix D's 50% deposit rate and the shop's credit-line headroom, find each option's deposit and how much (if any) additional financing The Full Kiln needs.

    D5's graph of A(x) is built outside this notebook — in Desmos, or by hand — with x*, The Steady Order, and The Full Kiln all labeled.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    The mill calls back that afternoon: the national furniture chain's counter-bid for the same kiln slot came in higher than Renata expected. To hold The Full Kiln's slot at all, she would need to match it — raising the effective cost of booking that specific, larger run by about half again. The Steady Order, her own standing arrangement with this mill, isn't part of that bidding war at all; its cost is unaffected.

    Nothing about Appendix A's fixed cost, Appendix B's two batch sizes, or Appendix C's sellable limit was ever a certainty carved in stone — MA1 (the fixed run cost) was always a stated Modeling Assumption, not a sourced figure. If the contested slot's fixed cost really did jump like this, does the D4 arithmetic you already built still say what you thought it said?
