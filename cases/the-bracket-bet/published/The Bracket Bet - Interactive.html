Overview

Devon Osei has the HR portal open on a cracked phone screen, thumb hovering between two radio buttons neither of which is highlighted yet.

The breakroom microwave beeps behind them — someone else's lunch, ignored. First real paycheck posts Friday, and payroll needs an answer before then or the contribution doesn't go anywhere at all.

“Traditional or Roth,” the enrollment screen asks, like that's a normal thing to know at twenty-four. Devon has scrolled the linked explainer twice. One button takes a tax break this year and pays the government back decades from now, whatever the government wants by then. The other button pays the government now, at the rate printed on this year's pay stub, and never again — not on a single dollar this account ever earns.

A coworker leans against the doorframe, keys jangling. “Roth. Everybody says Roth.”

Devon doesn't move the thumb yet. Everybody assumed a lot of things about a tax bracket forty years from now that nobody in this breakroom can actually see. The portal's countdown clock sits at the top of the screen, still running. Devon opens a blank note instead of tapping either button.

Background

Devon Osei is twenty-four and just converted from part-time to full-time, salaried staff — the first job with a retirement plan attached to it at all. The employer's payroll system requires a one-time election, made before the first contribution posts, of which individual retirement account type future contributions will flow into: a Traditional IRA or a Roth IRA. Once made, the election governs every contribution Devon plans to make in the years ahead (see Appendix A). Devon is the only person accountable for this decision — there is no employer match and no advisor on the other end of the portal, only the two radio buttons and the clock.

Both account types let Devon set aside the same annual amount, growing for the same number of years at the same assumed rate of return — the accounts are mechanically identical on the growth side of the math (see Appendix B). Where they differ is when the government takes its cut. Call the two options by what they actually do to Devon's paycheck and tax return: Pay Later — the Traditional IRA — lets Devon deduct this year's contribution from this year's taxable income, then taxes the entire withdrawal, including every dollar of decades of growth, at whatever Devon's marginal tax rate turns out to be in retirement. Pay Now — the Roth IRA — gives no deduction this year, so Devon pays tax on the contribution up front at this year's known rate, and then owes nothing at all on any future withdrawal, growth included (see Appendix C).

The entire dilemma rides on a single number nobody in the breakroom can actually know: the tax bracket Devon will be in the year this money finally gets touched, decades from now. Today's marginal rate is fixed and knowable. The future one is not — it depends on Devon's future income, and on tax law that could look completely different by then (see Appendix D). The IRS also caps how much can go into either account in a single year, and Devon's contribution plan has to fit inside that cap regardless of which button gets pressed (see Appendix A).

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    Devon submits the election. Two weeks later, a headline hits the breakroom TV: Congress just changed the tax brackets for the year Devon retires — the exact rate used in Scenario 1 or Scenario 2 no longer exists.

    You have 60 seconds: does your recommendation change, or was it never really about the exact number in the first place?

    Try a new future rate

    Type a new hypothetical future tax rate (any percent) and see which option it favors, using the same D3 method — today's 12% rate on Pay Now, the new rate on Pay Later.
