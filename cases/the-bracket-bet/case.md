# Case Study: The Bracket Bet

MS | Math | Case Study | 2026-27 | Adventure

---

## Overview

Devon Osei has the HR portal open on a cracked phone screen, thumb hovering between two radio
buttons neither of which is highlighted yet. The breakroom microwave beeps behind them — someone
else's lunch, ignored. First real paycheck posts Friday, and payroll needs an answer before then
or the contribution doesn't go anywhere at all.

"Traditional or Roth," the enrollment screen asks, like that's a normal thing to know at twenty-four.
Devon has scrolled the linked explainer twice. One button takes a tax break this year and pays the
government back decades from now, whatever the government wants by then. The other button pays the
government now, at the rate printed on this year's pay stub, and never again — not on a single
dollar this account ever earns.

A coworker leans against the doorframe, keys jangling. "Roth. Everybody says Roth."

Devon doesn't move the thumb yet. *Everybody* assumed a lot of things about a tax bracket forty
years from now that nobody in this breakroom can actually see. The portal's countdown clock sits at
the top of the screen, still running. Devon opens a blank note instead of tapping either button.

---

## Background

Devon Osei is twenty-four and just converted from part-time to full-time, salaried staff — the
first job with a retirement plan attached to it at all. The employer's payroll system requires a
one-time election, made before the first contribution posts, of which individual retirement account
type future contributions will flow into: a **Traditional IRA** or a **Roth IRA**. Once made, the
election governs every contribution Devon plans to make in the years ahead (SEE APPENDIX A). Devon
is the only person accountable for this decision — there is no employer match and no advisor on the
other end of the portal, only the two radio buttons and the clock.

Both account types let Devon set aside the same annual amount, growing for the same number of years
at the same assumed rate of return — the accounts are mechanically identical on the *growth* side of
the math (SEE APPENDIX B). Where they differ is *when* the government takes its cut. Call the two
options by what they actually do to Devon's paycheck and tax return: **Pay Later** — the Traditional
IRA — lets Devon deduct this year's contribution from this year's taxable income, then taxes the
entire withdrawal, including every dollar of decades of growth, at whatever Devon's marginal tax
rate turns out to be in retirement. **Pay Now** — the Roth IRA — gives no deduction this year, so
Devon pays tax on the contribution up front at this year's known rate, and then owes nothing at all
on any future withdrawal, growth included (SEE APPENDIX C).

The entire dilemma rides on a single number nobody in the breakroom can actually know: the tax
bracket Devon will be in the year this money finally gets touched, decades from now. Today's
marginal rate is fixed and knowable. The future one is not — it depends on Devon's future income,
and on tax law that could look completely different by then (SEE APPENDIX D). The IRS also caps how
much can go into either account in a single year, and Devon's contribution plan has to fit inside
that cap regardless of which button gets pressed (SEE APPENDIX A).

The vocabulary and the "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in the final
appendix — that is where Challenge 1 sends you (SEE APPENDIX E). All figures in this case are
sourced (SEE APPENDIX F).

---

## Dilemma

You are Devon Osei.

Do you:

**A.)** Elect **Pay Later** — contribute to a Traditional IRA, take the tax deduction this year, and
pay tax on the full withdrawal (contributions plus all growth) at retirement **OR**

**B.)** Elect **Pay Now** — contribute to a Roth IRA, pay tax on the contribution this year at
today's known rate, and owe nothing at all on the withdrawal at retirement

## Appendix C — How Traditional and Roth IRAs Are Actually Taxed

The tax mechanics that make Pay Later and Pay Now different, stated precisely enough to compute
from.

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Traditional IRA (Pay Later): tax treatment of the contribution in the year it is made | Fully tax-deductible — Devon has no other workplace retirement plan, so no phase-out applies [S6] |
| Traditional IRA (Pay Later): tax treatment of a qualified withdrawal in retirement | Entire withdrawal (contributions plus all growth) taxed as ordinary income at the withdrawal-year rate [S6] |
| Roth IRA (Pay Now): tax treatment of the contribution in the year it is made | Made with already-taxed income; no deduction [S7] |
| Roth IRA (Pay Now): tax treatment of a qualified withdrawal in retirement | Qualified withdrawal (contributions plus all growth) entirely tax-free [S7] |

Referenced from Background and forces D3's after-tax value computation for both options.

## Modeling Assumptions

> These figures are **not sourced** — nobody can look up a tax bracket that has not happened yet.
> They are stated scenario assumptions, chosen to be realistic and to genuinely straddle Devon's
> current, known bracket in both directions. A Hero may challenge them in discussion — that is a
> legitimate move.

| # | Assumption | Value used | Basis | Sensitivity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MA1 | Devon's current marginal federal tax bracket | 12% | Not itself an assumption — computed from the real, current 2026 IRS bracket table [S8] against Devon's assumed entry-level income. Carried here as context for MA2/MA3. | Fixed; not a free variable. |
| MA2 | Assumed future marginal tax bracket, Scenario 1 (rates rise) | 22% | A real, currently-published federal bracket [S8], one step above Devon's current 12%. Chosen as a realistic mid-to-late-career trajectory over a ~43-year working life — reaching a six-figure-equivalent income by retirement age is an ordinary outcome over four decades, not an extreme one. | Work D3/D4 under this scenario to see which option comes out ahead, and by how much. If the true future rate were instead higher than 22%, or exactly 12% (no change from today), how would your D3/D4 results change? |
| MA3 | Assumed future marginal tax bracket, Scenario 2 (rates fall or stay low) | 10% | The real bottom bracket of the federal schedule [S8], one step below Devon's current 12%. Chosen as a realistic "modest, largely Social-Security-supported retirement income" trajectory — the required counter-scenario so the case cannot be resolved by assuming taxes only ever rise. | Work D3/D4 under this scenario to see which option comes out ahead, and by how much. If the true future rate matched today's 12% exactly, how would your D3/D4 results change? |

Both MA2 and MA3 are real, currently-published federal brackets (see Appendix D and Appendix
F — Sources); only their *selection* as Devon's specific future outcome is assumed, and that
selection is disclosed here rather than buried in Appendix D's table.
