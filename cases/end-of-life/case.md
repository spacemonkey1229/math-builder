# Case Study: End Of Life

MS | Math | Case Study | 2026-27 | Heroes Academy

---

## Overview

Tyler Escovedo has read the same paragraph of the discharge pamphlet four times without absorbing a word of it. Across the cafeteria table, Lily picks the crust off a grilled cheese she isn't going to eat. Audrey and Caleb sit shoulder to shoulder, phones face-down, untouched. Upstairs, nine floors up, Lauren is exactly where she's been since Tuesday night — on a ventilator, breathing on a machine's schedule instead of her own.

"Dr. Halbert said cold and slow," Audrey says quietly, more to the table than to anyone. "That's what she said. Cold and slow."

Nobody answers. The cafeteria is nearly empty this late — just the hum of the soda case and a janitor mopping near the salad bar. Tyler turns his coffee cup a quarter turn, then another, watching a ring of condensation smear across the tray liner.

His phone buzzes. Grace, already texting from the jet bridge in Los Angeles, landing soon. Three kids in this room and a fourth one still in the air, and every one of them is waiting on him to decide something no father should have to decide alone.

The pamphlet in his hand describes what a ventilator does. What it doesn't tell him is when to turn it off — or whether "when" is even the right question.

Lily finally looks up. "Dad. What are we doing?"

## Background

**Tyler Escovedo (44)**, a software engineer, and **Lauren Escovedo (45)**, a labor and delivery nurse, are the parents of four children: **Lily (14)**, the only one still living at home; **Audrey** and **Caleb**, both away at college; and **Grace**, the oldest, who lives out of state with her own two young children and is flying in to be with the family. Lauren was admitted after several nights without sleep, worsening headaches, and an inability to keep food down. Her heart stopped during a routine procedure on her first night in the hospital; she was resuscitated after six minutes of CPR and has been on a ventilator, unconscious, ever since. **Dr. Ethan Halbert**, overseeing her care, has told the family that Lauren's brain tumor is terminal, that she is not in pain, that there is less than a 5% chance she wakes before she dies, and that she will very likely survive **two to three weeks** but will not survive more than **50 days** (SEE APPENDIX C).

As next of kin, Tyler holds medical decision authority, but the family is treating this as a shared decision. Two choices are in front of them, and only two: end mechanical ventilation on a date the family chooses, or leave the ventilator running and let Lauren's death happen on its own timeline, however long that turns out to be within Dr. Halbert's stated window.

Every day Lauren remains hospitalized generates a new bill (SEE APPENDIX A). The family has employer health insurance through Tyler's job, but their plan has **no maximum out-of-pocket limit** — nothing caps what they could ultimately owe. Tyler and Lauren's combined assets and liabilities, as of this week, are documented in a personal net-worth worksheet the family filled out with a financial planner last year (SEE APPENDIX B); which of those assets are actually liquid enough to spend on a hospital bill without a tax penalty or a slow sale is mapped separately (SEE APPENDIX F). The hospital's own billing statement also lists the current federal poverty guideline, which determines whether a patient qualifies for free or discounted care under state and hospital charity-care rules (SEE APPENDIX D). Appendix E lays out, as a stated modeling convention, how the daily rate observed in Appendix A is projected forward for however many additional days the family's choice requires, and how that daily rate is affected if Lauren ever leaves ICU/ventilator care for an alternative care setting. The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case draws on are listed in Appendix G — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

## Dilemma

You are the Escovedo family.

Do you:

**A.)** Remove Lauren from the breathing machine on a date you choose, ending her life then **OR**

**B.)** Keep Lauren on the breathing machine and let her die naturally, whenever that happens — no later than 50 days from now

## Appendix C: Dr. Halbert's Prognosis Statement

Directly from the family's conversation with Dr. Ethan Halbert, the physician overseeing Lauren's care:

- Lauren is **not in pain**.
- There is **less than a 5% chance** Lauren wakes from her coma before she dies.
- Dr. Halbert is confident Lauren will **likely survive two to three weeks** (14–21 days) from today.
- Dr. Halbert is confident Lauren **will not survive more than 50 days** from today.

These three numbers — the 14–21 day likely window and the 50-day outer bound — are the only stated survival estimates in this case. Nothing in this case asks you to invent a more precise number than the doctor gave.

## Appendix E — Modeling Assumptions

Appendix A gives three real, billed days. This appendix states, as a disclosed modeling convention (not a source), how those three days extend to a longer stay:

- **Day 1** (the Emergency Room admission day) is a one-time charge and is not repeated.
- **Every day from Day 2 onward**, the daily *family balance* (what the family actually owes after insurance, not the gross charge) is held constant at the **Dec 12/Dec 13 rate: $8,743.26 per day** — the real rate the two identical ICU/Ventilator/Pharmacy days in Appendix A already show.
- No inflation, rate changes, or new categories of charge (e.g., additional procedures) are modeled for any day beyond Dec 13. This is a simplification — a real hospital stay would likely include some day-to-day variation — stated here so every Hero's arithmetic starts from the same convention.
- **If the family accepts a treatment option that moves Lauren out of the ICU and off the ventilator** (for example, to go home instead of staying in the ICU), the $8,743.26/day ICU/ventilator rate stops accruing from that point forward — that daily rate is billed only for days Lauren is actually receiving ICU/ventilator care. Whatever alternative care setting she moves to instead is billed separately and is not part of this case's stated daily-rate figures.

Under this convention, the cumulative family balance through Day *n* (n ≥ 1) is:

**Cumulative Balance(n) = $2,488.91 + (n − 1) × $8,743.26**

## Appendix F: Liquid vs. Illiquid Assets

Not every dollar on Appendix B's worksheet is equally available to pay a hospital bill this month. This appendix sorts Appendix B's asset lines into two groups, for D4 and D5:

| Group | Items (from Appendix B) | Total |
|---|---|---:|

| **Liquid — cash + taxable brokerage** | Above, plus the taxable brokerage account | $111,953.63 |
| **Illiquid / restricted** | IRA, 401(k)/403(b), and Pension (early withdrawal typically carries tax and penalty consequences; the pension is a vested future benefit, not a lump sum sitting in an account today) | $461,372.15 |
| **Non-liquid use assets** | Home, cars, home furnishings (would require a sale, and time, to convert to cash) | $94,430.73 |

This grouping is a stated convention for this case, not a claim about what the Escovedos would necessarily choose to do — a real family might tap a 401(k) early despite the penalty, or might not. Your D4 assets map should state which group(s) you draw on and in what order, and why.
