## Overview

Danny Ruiz keys the radio twice before anyone answers.

The collapsed parapet is still smoking along the roofline, a jagged gray tooth against the
orange glow behind it, and the safety officer's tape line is already up — nobody crosses it,
not even to drag hose closer. Danny's boots are planted at the base of the ladder truck, one
hand on the joystick that swings the portable monitor, the other still holding the radio. Two
numbers are chalked on the inside of his forearm in grease pencil, the way he always writes them
before he commits to a stream: one flat, one steep. Behind him, the pump operator is already
dialed to pressure and waiting on his word.

Somewhere past that tape line, on the other side of the debris, is the exact spot the incident
commander needs water landing — and needs it landing *now*, not after Danny walks the stream in
by trial and error while the crew inside a collapse zone waits on him. He has one shot to set
the angle before the water leaves the barrel, and two different angles on his arm both claim
they'll reach it.

Danny closes his fist around the joystick and doesn't move it yet.

## Background

Danny Ruiz is the apparatus operator for Engine 14, part of the Ridgeview Fire Department's
initial alarm assignment to a defensive structure fire at a mixed-use commercial building. A
section of the building's parapet wall has already come down, and the incident commander has
declared the front of the structure a **collapse zone** — no personnel may work inside it, and
every stream aimed at the fire has to be thrown *into* the zone from outside its boundary
(SEE APPENDIX A). Danny's monitor is a portable ground-mounted master-stream device, fed by
Engine 14's pump, discharging through a fixed smoothbore tip (SEE APPENDIX B). The only thing
Danny controls in the next thirty seconds is the monitor's **elevation angle** — the pump
pressure and tip are already set and are not going to change.

The physics of a smoothbore stream is symmetric in a specific, exploitable way: for a fixed
nozzle pressure, tip size, and target distance, there are generally **two** elevation angles
that land water at exactly that same distance — one flat and fast, one steep and lofted (SEE
APPENDIX C, APPENDIX D). Danny has both written on his arm.

**The Low Arc.** The flatter of the two solved angles. It keeps the stream low and fast the
whole way to the target, which means less of the stream's flow is lost to crosswind before it
lands — but a flat, low trajectory travels close to the ground for its entire path, straight
through the collapse zone's own debris field and any low obstruction sitting in front of the
building.

**The High Arc.** The steeper, complementary solved angle — mathematically forced to reach the
identical target distance as the Low Arc (SEE APPENDIX C). It spends far longer in the air and
loses more of its flow to wind before landing, but it rises high enough to come down almost
straight into the target from above, clearing any ground-level obstruction in the collapse zone
without ever needing to pass through it.

Both angles are real, valid solutions to the same equation. Ridgeview's SOP does not pre-select
one over the other — that call belongs to the operator, in the moment, for this fire. Real,
public documentation behind both the collapse-zone standoff rule and the smoothbore discharge
formula this case is built on is in APPENDIX E. Every external source cited anywhere in this case
is collected in APPENDIX G. The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case asks you to research
are listed in APPENDIX H — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

## Dilemma

You are Danny Ruiz.

Do you:

**A.)** Command **the Low Arc** — the flatter of the two solved elevation angles, favoring flow
efficiency **OR**

**B.)** Command **the High Arc** — the steeper of the two solved elevation angles, favoring
obstruction clearance

## Appendix C: The Range Equation

The horizontal distance a smoothbore stream travels, modeled as ideal projectile motion (no air
resistance), is:

$$R = \frac{v^2 \sin(2\theta)}{g}$$

where $R$ is the horizontal range (ft), $v$ is the stream's exit velocity (ft/s) leaving the
nozzle, $\theta$ is the monitor's elevation angle, and $g$ is the acceleration of gravity,
**32.2 ft/s²** (the standard US-customary value used throughout this case; see [S5] for the
formula's textbook derivation) [S5].

**Deriving the exit velocity $v$.** The nozzle's rated discharge (Appendix B) is a volume flow
rate; the nozzle's tip is a fixed circular opening. The exit velocity is the flow rate divided by
the tip's cross-sectional area (a continuity-equation argument, shown in full in your D1 work):

$$v = \frac{Q}{A}, \qquad A = \pi\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^2$$

with $Q$ converted to cubic feet per second and $A$ to square feet. Carried out with the tip
diameter and rated discharge from Appendix B, this gives:

$$v \approx 108\text{ ft/s} \quad \text{(derived — see D1 for the full worked conversion; not itself a directly published figure)}$$

**Solving for the angle.** With $R$ fixed by Appendix A and $v$ fixed above, the range equation
becomes a trigonometric equation in the single unknown $\theta$:

$$\sin(2\theta) = \frac{Rg}{v^2}$$

This is the equation Challenge D2 asks you to solve over the interval $0° < \theta < 90°$.
Because $\sin$ is positive on both $(0°, 90°)$ and $(90°, 180°)$ for the doubled angle $2\theta$,
this equation generically has **two** solutions in that interval, and they are always
complementary ($\theta_{\text{low}} + \theta_{\text{high}} = 90°$) — that complementary
relationship is itself a consequence of a trigonometric identity, which is exactly what
Challenge D1 asks you to verify using the components you compute for each arc.

**The height of a trajectory at a given distance.** Challenge D3 also asks you to check each
arc's height at the parapet remnant's location (Appendix A), not just at its highest point. The
same projectile-motion model that gives the range equation above also gives the stream's height
$y$ at any horizontal distance $x$ from the monitor:

$$y(x) = x\tan\theta - \frac{gx^2}{2v^2\cos^2\theta}$$

Note that $y(R) = 0$ for either arc by construction (that is what "range" means) — this formula
only tells you something about clearing an obstruction when that obstruction stands **short of**
the target distance, which is exactly why Appendix A places the parapet remnant 25 ft short of
the 225 ft target rather than at the target itself.

## Appendix E: The Real Doctrine and the Real Formula

Two real, published facts sit behind this case's dilemma.

**The collapse-zone standoff rule is real, taught fireground doctrine.** Fire service safety
training establishes a minimum standoff distance from a structure with collapse potential, set as
a multiple of the building's height, and instructs incident commanders to mark that perimeter and
keep personnel and apparatus outside it during defensive operations [S1], [S2]. This case treats
that standoff distance as Danny's one hard, non-negotiable constraint — he cannot move the
monitor closer to improve the Low Arc's odds, no matter how much better its flow efficiency is.

**The smoothbore discharge formula is real, standard fire-hydraulics content**, taught to every
apparatus operator: $GPM = c \cdot d^2 \sqrt{NP}$, where $d$ is the tip diameter in inches, $NP$
is the nozzle pressure in psi, and $c$ is a published discharge coefficient close to 29.7 — the
"Freeman Formula," $29.71d^2\sqrt{NP}$ (several fire-service organizations publish slightly
different values of $c$; see Appendix G) [S3]. The standard operating pressure for a smoothbore
master-stream nozzle — 80 psi — is also a real, widely-taught fireground figure [S3], and a
published table of smoothbore tip sizes and their rated GPM at that pressure confirms the 1-3/8
in tip this case uses [S4]. The specific monitor in Appendix B is a real, currently-sold
high-elevation portable monitor [S6] — high-elevation monitors are marketed specifically for
situations like this one, where a target must be reached from beyond an obstruction.

Neither of these real facts, on its own, produces the range equation's two-solution structure —
that structure comes from ordinary projectile motion, the same idealized model taught in any
introductory physics course, including the fact that two complementary launch angles produce the
identical range [S5]. This case combines three real fire-service facts (a real safety rule, a
real discharge formula, a real monitor's real elevation range) with that standard physics model
to build a scenario Danny never actually filed an incident report for — Ridgeview Fire Department
is fictional (SEE APPENDIX F).
