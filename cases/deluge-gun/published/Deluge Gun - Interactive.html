Overview

Danny Ruiz keys the radio twice before anyone answers.

The collapsed parapet is still smoking along the roofline, a jagged gray tooth against the orange glow behind it, and the safety officer's tape line is already up — nobody crosses it, not even to drag hose closer. Danny's boots are planted at the base of the ladder truck, one hand on the joystick that swings the portable monitor, the other still holding the radio. Two numbers are chalked on the inside of his forearm in grease pencil, the way he always writes them before he commits to a stream: one flat, one steep. Behind him, the pump operator is already dialed to pressure and waiting on his word.

Somewhere past that tape line, on the other side of the debris, is the exact spot the incident commander needs water landing — and needs it landing now, not after Danny walks the stream in by trial and error while the crew inside a collapse zone waits on him. He has one shot to set the angle before the water leaves the barrel, and two different angles on his arm both claim they'll reach it.

Danny closes his fist around the joystick and doesn't move it yet.

Background

Danny Ruiz is the apparatus operator for Engine 14, part of the Ridgeview Fire Department's initial alarm assignment to a defensive structure fire at a mixed-use commercial building. A section of the building's parapet wall has already come down, and the incident commander has declared the front of the structure a collapse zone — no personnel may work inside it, and every stream aimed at the fire has to be thrown into the zone from outside its boundary (see Appendix A). Danny's monitor is a portable ground-mounted master-stream device, fed by Engine 14's pump, discharging through a fixed smoothbore tip (see Appendix B). The only thing Danny controls in the next thirty seconds is the monitor's elevation angle — the pump pressure and tip are already set and are not going to change.

The physics of a smoothbore stream is symmetric in a specific, exploitable way: for a fixed nozzle pressure, tip size, and target distance, there are generally two elevation angles that land water at exactly that same distance — one flat and fast, one steep and lofted (see Appendix C, Appendix D). Danny has both written on his arm.

The Low Arc

The flatter of the two solved angles. It keeps the stream low and fast the whole way to the target, which means less of the stream's flow is lost to crosswind before it lands — but a flat, low trajectory travels close to the ground for its entire path, straight through the collapse zone's own debris field and any low obstruction sitting in front of the building.

The High Arc

The steeper, complementary solved angle — mathematically forced to reach the identical target distance as the Low Arc (see Appendix C). It spends far longer in the air and loses more of its flow to wind before landing, but it rises high enough to come down almost straight into the target from above, clearing any ground-level obstruction in the collapse zone without ever needing to pass through it.

Both angles are real, valid solutions to the same equation. Ridgeview's SOP does not pre-select one over the other — that call belongs to the operator, in the moment, for this fire. Real, public documentation behind both the collapse-zone standoff rule and the smoothbore discharge formula this case is built on is in Appendix E. Every external source cited anywhere in this case is collected in Appendix G. The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case asks you to research are listed in Appendix H — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

Appendix C — The Range Equation

The horizontal distance a smoothbore stream travels, modeled as ideal projectile motion (no air resistance), is:

R = v² sin(2θ) / g

where R is the horizontal range (ft), v is the stream's exit velocity (ft/s) leaving the nozzle, θ is the monitor's elevation angle, and g is the acceleration of gravity, 32.2 ft/s² [S5].

Deriving the exit velocity v. The nozzle's rated discharge (Appendix B) is a volume flow rate; the nozzle's tip is a fixed circular opening. The exit velocity is the flow rate divided by the tip's cross-sectional area (a continuity-equation argument — work this out yourself for D1):

v = Q / A,   A = π(d/2)²

Q converted to cubic feet per second and A to square feet, using the tip diameter and rated discharge from Appendix B.

Solving for the angle. With R fixed by Appendix A and v fixed above, the range equation becomes a trigonometric equation in the single unknown θ:

sin(2θ) = Rg / v²

This is the equation Challenge D2 asks you to solve over the interval 0° < θ < 90°. Because sin is positive on both (0°, 90°) and (90°, 180°) for the doubled angle 2θ, this equation generically has two solutions in that interval, and they are always complementary (θlow + θhigh = 90°) — a consequence of a trigonometric identity, which is exactly what Challenge D1 asks you to verify using the components you compute for each arc.

The height of a trajectory at a given distance. Challenge D3 also asks you to check each arc's height at the parapet remnant's location (Appendix A), not just at its highest point. The same projectile-motion model also gives the stream's height y at any horizontal distance x from the monitor:

y(x) = x·tanθ − g·x² / (2v²cos²θ)

Note that y(R) = 0 for either arc by construction (that is what "range" means) — this formula only tells you something about clearing an obstruction when that obstruction stands short of the target distance, which is exactly why Appendix A places the parapet remnant 25 ft short of the 225 ft target rather than at the target itself.

Appendix E — The Real Doctrine and the Real Formula

Two real, published facts sit behind this case's dilemma.

The collapse-zone standoff rule is real, taught fireground doctrine. Fire service safety training establishes a minimum standoff distance from a structure with collapse potential, set as a multiple of the building's height, and instructs incident commanders to mark that perimeter and keep personnel and apparatus outside it during defensive operations [S1], [S2]. This case treats that standoff distance as Danny's one hard, non-negotiable constraint — he cannot move the monitor closer to improve the Low Arc's odds, no matter how much better its flow efficiency is.

The smoothbore discharge formula is real, standard fire-hydraulics content, taught to every apparatus operator: GPM = c·d²√NP, where d is the tip diameter in inches, NP is the nozzle pressure in psi, and c is a published discharge coefficient close to 29.7 — the "Freeman Formula," 29.71d²√NP (several fire-service organizations publish slightly different values of c; see Appendix G) [S3]. The standard operating pressure for a smoothbore master-stream nozzle — 80 psi — is also a real, widely-taught fireground figure [S3], and a published table of smoothbore tip sizes and their rated GPM at that pressure confirms the 1-3/8 in tip this case uses [S4]. The specific monitor in Appendix B is a real, currently-sold high-elevation portable monitor [S6].

Neither of these real facts, on its own, produces the range equation's two-solution structure — that structure comes from ordinary projectile motion, the same idealized model taught in any introductory physics course, including the fact that two complementary launch angles produce the identical range [S5]. This case combines three real fire-service facts with that standard physics model to build a scenario Danny never actually filed an incident report for — Ridgeview Fire Department is fictional (see Appendix F).

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    D1 — Velocity-component verification (Pythagorean identity)

    Pick an arc, compute its horizontal (v·cosθ) and vertical (v·sinθ) velocity components using the nozzle's derived exit velocity (v = 108 ft/s), then verify with the Pythagorean identity that they recombine to v.

    The check verifies (v·cosθ)² + (v·sinθ)² ≈ v² — the Pythagorean identity applied to velocity components.

    D2 — Two-angle solution (solve the trig equation over 0°–90°)

    Solve sin(2θ) = Rg/v² for θ over 0° < θ < 90°. There are two solutions — enter both, low arc first.

    D3 — Hang time, apex, and parapet clearance

    For your chosen arc, compute time of flight, apex height, and the trajectory's actual height at x = 200 ft (the parapet remnant's location) — then see whether that arc clears the 12 ft remnant.
    clearance: not checked

    Clearance is computed live from your own y(200) value against the 12 ft remnant — the notebook won't tell you the answer for the arc you haven't checked yet.

    D4 — Effective-flow table (wind-drift loss)

    Apply Appendix F's wind-drift loss model (MA2: 2% of rated flow lost per second of flight) to each arc's rated 500 GPM, using each arc's own time of flight from D3, to find effective gallons on target per minute for each arc.
