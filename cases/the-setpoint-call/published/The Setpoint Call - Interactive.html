Case Study: The Setpoint Call

Overview

The label applicator on Line 3 hums at its usual clip, and Marisol Feng watches from the catwalk as cases stack up at the palletizer below, half her attention already on the spreadsheet glowing on her tablet. The plant just landed the biggest co-packing contract in its history — a national sports-drink brand, every bottle running through this exact line — and somebody upstairs wants a speed number by the end of her shift.

Her tablet has two tabs open. One is a quote from the guarding contractor: new interlocked panels, sensor curtains, the whole retrofit Line 3 would need to legally run faster than it does today. The other is her own derivative, half-finished, chasing the exact speed where cost per bottle bottoms out before climbing again. That speed keeps landing past the line's current rated ceiling — which means the contractor's quote isn't optional if she wants to actually reach it.

Her shift lead leans over the rail. “Corporate needs the speed setpoint locked before the contract's first production week. Not next week — this week.” Marisol nods without looking up. Below her, the labeler spins bottle after bottle past its glue wheel, steady, ordinary, waiting on a number she hasn't found the courage to write down yet.

Background

Marisol Feng is the process engineer responsible for Line 3 at Bellcross Bottling, a contract manufacturerA company that packages products for its clients, under the client's own brand.Open a source on this → that fills and labels beverages under other companies' brands. Bellcross itself owns and runs Line 3's equipment; the sports-drink brand owns the drink's recipe and label design and pays Bellcross a fee for every bottle Bellcross fills, labels, and ships — the brand never touches the machinery. Bellcross has just signed a multi-month exclusive co-packing agreement with that national sports-drink brand, and every bottle in that agreement will run on Line 3's rotary labelerA labeling machine whose bottles ride a rotating turret past a fixed glue/label-applying station as the turret turns -- the standard way high-speed bottling lines apply labels.Open a source on this →, a machine that carries bottles around a spinning carousel and glues a label onto each one as it passes a fixed applicator station. Marisol is accountable for setting the line's operating speed for the life of the contract — a single number that determines the plant's cost per bottle for months to come (SEE APPENDIX A).

Line 3's labeler currently runs no faster than its manufacturer-rated, ANSI/PMMI-compliant maximum safe speed — the ceiling set by the machine's existing guardingA safety feature -- a shield or device -- covering hazardous areas of a machine to prevent contact with moving parts.Open a source on this → and interlockA device that keeps a machine from operating until its guards are in place or required conditions are met.Open a source on this → configuration (SEE APPENDIX B). Running faster than that ceiling without matching guarding upgrades is not just inadvisable, it is a documented machine-safety-standard violation. Marisol and her shift lead have started calling the choice by its two paths. The Guarding Upgrade means paying a contractor now to install the additional interlocked guarding the safety standard requires to legally clear Line 3 for a higher operating speed — the speed Marisol's own cost model says minimizes cost per bottle — before the new contract's first production week begins (SEE APPENDIX C). The Rated Cap means leaving Line 3's guarding as it is and running the entire contract at the labeler's current rated ceiling — no retrofit, no capital outlay, but also no access to the lower cost per bottle the math says is possible above that ceiling (SEE APPENDIX B, APPENDIX C).

A faster line does not automatically mean a cheaper bottle, however: Line 3's cost per bottle depends on both how fast it runs and how much retrofit capital that speed required, spread across every bottle the new contract will actually produce (SEE APPENDIX C). The guarding contractor has exactly one install slot before the contract's first production week — a scheduled window in which the contractor's crew would take Line 3 fully offline to mount the new panels, guarding, and PLC and test them before handing the line back — and after that slot passes, the next available one is too late to matter for this decision. Put together, these three retrofit items close off every point on Line 3 a person's body could reach while it runs faster than today's ceiling. The turret access panels are fixed metal-and-mesh guards bolted around the gap between the spinning turret and the machine's stationary frame — the pinch point where a hand could get pulled in as the turret rotates. The infeed/outfeed conveyor guarding is the same idea along the straight conveyor belts carrying bottles in and out, and the sensor curtains sit across the labeler's open access points, watching for a hand or arm to break their light beams. The safety PLC is wired to every one of those guards and switches; the instant one opens or a beam breaks, the PLC's control logic cuts power to the motor. That combination is what lets a risk assessment under ANSI/PMMI B155.1 (Appendix B) — typically an on-site inspection followed by a written certificate posted at the machine — sign off on raising Line 3's rated speed at all.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — D1: Build TC(r) and evaluate it at the rated ceiling

    Build CR(r) = a + b·r and MPU(r) = 1/r + d from Appendix A, combine them as TC(r) = CR(r) × MPU(r), then evaluate all three at Line 3's current rated ceiling (Appendix B).

    Step 2 — D2: Product rule — find r*

    Differentiate TC(r) with the product rule (and the power rule for the 1/r term), set TC′(r) = 0, and solve algebraically for r*.

    Step 3 — D3: First derivative test and concavity check

    Confirm r* is a genuine local minimum using both the first derivative test and a concavity (second-derivative) check. Then decide whether TC(r) has a local maximum anywhere for r > 0.

    Step 4 — D4: Check r* against the rated ceiling

    Evaluate TC(r) at both r* and the rated ceiling, and decide whether the unconstrained calculus optimum is legally reachable under Line 3's current guarding.

    Step 5 — D5: Total cost per bottle over the life of the contract

    Amortize Appendix C's retrofit cost over its contract volume, add it to TC(r*) for The Guarding Upgrade, compare against The Rated Cap's TC at the rated ceiling (no retrofit cost), and find the dollar and percent gap.

    D6's graph of TC(r) is built outside this notebook — in Desmos, or by hand — with r* and the rated ceiling both labeled.
