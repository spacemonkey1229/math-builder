Overview

Renata Cruz has three tabs open and one phone call on hold. The first tab is the queue board, where a green cell just flipped: Aurora Ridge — a data-center developer's bid for a hard-won slot on the Fenwick Creek substation — has cleared its final engineering study. The second tab is her own capacity spreadsheet, the one she is not supposed to need a calculator for because every cell in it is already a fraction of the substation's real, physical headroom. The third is an email from her queue-processing supervisor, subject line “DECISION BY 5:00 PM,” timestamp eleven minutes old.

On hold is the developer's interconnection counsel, who has said some version of “we're ready to sign today” four times in six minutes.

Renata's sticky note, stuck to the monitor bezel for three weeks now, still just says: what actually fits in what's left? Two smaller bids — Cobalt Peak and Fenwick Yards — are still sitting lower in the queue, neither one big enough alone to need the whole slot, and nobody has run the numbers on whether the two of them together would use the leftover capacity better than Aurora Ridge does by itself.

She pulls her headset mic back down. “Give me five minutes,” she says, and mutes the line.

Background

Renata Cruz is a senior interconnection queue analyst for Meridian Valley Power, a regional electric utility. Her job is to decide, substation by substation, which pending large-load interconnection requests — mostly AI data-center campuses now, some large enough alone to register as a transmission-planning event [S3] — get a firm capacity commitment and in what order, without ever promising more capacity than a substation actually has. Grid interconnection queues for large loads like this have become a widely reported national bottleneck as data-center demand has surged: nationally, requests now sit a median of several years before a utility can confirm they will not overload the local grid [S1], against a backdrop of gigawatts of capacity already waiting in queues across the country [S2] (see Appendix F). Regulators have pushed utilities toward evaluating combinations of requests together rather than strictly one at a time [S4], precisely because a smaller, better-fitting group of projects can sometimes use the same leftover capacity more completely than the next request in line (see Appendix F).

Meridian Valley Power studies new large-load capacity at the Fenwick Creek substation in standard capacity blocks — a fixed increment the utility's planning department uses so that every bid, reservation, and remaining-headroom figure can be compared on the same footing (see Appendix C; if the block's real-world megawatt size cannot be tied to a single published figure for this exact substation, the disclosed Modeling Assumption used instead is in Appendix D). All of the figures below are stated in blocks, as fractions or mixed numbers, because that is the actual unit the queue board, the engineering study, and the capacity spreadsheet all use — never a decimal, and never a raw megawatt figure alone.

The substation's total interconnection capacity currently open to new large-load requests, and the capacity already promised to an earlier-queued customer, are both fixed prior facts (see Appendix A). Subtracting one from the other — in block form, with unlike denominators — gives the capacity actually remaining for today's decision. Three bids are currently active against that remaining capacity (see Appendix B):

Aurora Ridge

A single hyperscale AI training campus, first in the queue, engineering-cleared, and ready to sign today. Its request alone very nearly fills what remains, but not exactly — approving it now leaves an odd, leftover sliver of capacity that may or may not be large enough for anything else ever to use (see Appendix B).

Cobalt Peak + Fenwick Yards

Two smaller, still-pending bids further back in the queue. Neither is anywhere near large enough alone to be worth holding the slot for. Added together, though, they may or may not fit inside the same remaining capacity Aurora Ridge would use — and if they do, they may leave more or less of that capacity stranded than Aurora Ridge alone would (see Appendix B). Both bids would have to actually confirm before Meridian Valley Power's own queue deadline for this to work — neither is a sure thing the way Aurora Ridge is.

A fourth bid, Briar Hollow, is also active in the queue for context but is not large enough, nor positioned closely enough, to change today's decision (see Appendix B) — it is queue data Renata has to look at and set aside, not data that changes today's math.

Every capacity figure in this case — the substation's total and committed capacity, each bid's size, the leftover capacity under each option, and the head-to-head comparison between them — must be carried through entirely in fraction and mixed-number form, adding and subtracting with unlike denominators exactly as Meridian Valley Power's own capacity spreadsheet does, and never converted to a decimal along the way. The vocabulary and “how to…” procedures needed are listed in Appendix E. All background statistics and the capacity-block figure are cited in Appendix F.

RMI's large-load interconnection guidance reports that loads exceeding approximately 20 MW require direct transmission-level connection [S5] — a real, published order-of-magnitude reference point for how utilities size a "large load" study increment. 24 MW is chosen near that real reference point, scaled up slightly so the substation's stated total (4-5/8 blocks) and committed (1-1/4 blocks) capacities land on whole-megawatt figures. The block size does not change which option wins — Option A vs. Option B is decided by a same-units fraction comparison in blocks, and the block's MW size cancels out of that comparison. It only changes the real-world megawatt scale the pitch reports. A Hero who argues the real increment should be smaller or larger should recompute the MW figures, not the block-fraction comparison.

    Twist

    Reveal this only after you've finished working through the case — the Twist is meant to land after your analysis, not before.

    Reveal the Twist

    The real margin between Option A and Option B is exactly one twenty-fourth of a capacity block — the smallest fractional unit that appears anywhere in this case's own arithmetic. A student who makes a single regrouping error in either D2 or D4 (forgetting to borrow a whole block, or borrowing it incorrectly) will compute a stranded-capacity figure that is off by exactly the size of this margin or larger — and will not be able to tell, from their own wrong number, that anything went wrong. The wrong answer can look just as plausible as the right one.

    Try the classic shortcut yourself: instead of regrouping (borrowing a whole block) before subtracting, just take the absolute difference between the two fraction parts and stop there. Run it on both D2 and D4 below and compare it against the real, regrouped answer — computed live, not printed here.
