Overview

Jordan Reyes is thumb-deep in a retirement-savings app at 11:40 p.m., the phone brightness turned down so it doesn't wake anyone else in the apartment. A text from Priya Nair is still open above it — a screenshot of Priya's own retirement account balance, sent with nothing but “look what all those years add up to 😳.” Priya started putting money away not long after college and never stopped. Jordan meant to do the same and didn't, and tonight is the first night Jordan has actually opened this app instead of closing the reminder notification.

The plan had always been vague and confident at the same time: start later, catch up by putting in more. Jordan even said it out loud once, to a coworker, like it was obvious. Nobody had ever actually run the numbers on what “putting in more” would need to mean.

The app has a blank field waiting for a monthly contribution amount. Jordan's cursor blinks in it. Somewhere between Priya's screenshot and this blank field is a real number — not a vibe, not a guess, an actual dollar figure — and Jordan can't type anything into that box honestly until it's found.

Background

A retirement account is a savings account meant to be left alone and grown for decades: money goes in on a regular schedule (here, a fixed amount every month), it earns a return over time the same way any invested money does, and it is not touched until the saver actually retires.

Growing that money usually means investing it in the stock market rather than leaving it in plain cash, so it can grow at the stock market's own long-run average rate instead of sitting still — in practice, a saver's monthly contribution buys into a single fund that already holds a mix of many companies' stock, rather than the saver picking companies one at a time. This case uses the S&P 500 — a widely-tracked bundle of 500 large U.S. companies, and the most common stand-in for “the stock market” generally — as the source for that growth rate; its long-run average return is one of the two real-world rates Appendix C provides. Because the money sits untouched for so long, two forces work on it the whole time — investment growth, which grows the balance, and inflation, which shrinks what each of those dollars can actually buy by the time they're spent. This case is about keeping both forces in view at once, not just the first one.

Jordan Reyes and Priya Nair are close friends who started their careers around the same time. Priya began contributing to a retirement account at age 25 and has never stopped (SEE APPENDIX A). Jordan meant to do the same thing and didn't, for reasons that don't matter anymore — a decade passed, and Jordan is only now opening a retirement account at age 35 (SEE APPENDIX B). Both friends plan to retire at the same standard retirement age, and both intend for their account balance at that point to be worth the same amount in today's spending power — not just the same number of raw dollars, since inflation quietly shrinks what a dollar buys every year it sits uninvested or under-invested (SEE APPENDIX C).

Jordan has narrowed the decision to two named contribution plans. The Bridge Plan commits Jordan to whatever higher monthly contribution is actually required, starting now, to close the ten-year gap and reach the exact same real, inflation-adjusted account balance as Priya's by the same retirement age Priya is targeting. The Extended Plan keeps Jordan's monthly contribution lower — Jordan is not willing to assume the Bridge Plan's larger monthly number is affordable without seeing it first — but still reaches that same real target balance, by giving the account two additional years to grow before Jordan actually retires (SEE APPENDIX B).

Both plans are computed using the same real rate of return: the long-run average nominal return an investor actually earns, adjusted downward for the long-run average rate at which inflation erodes purchasing power (SEE APPENDIX C). That adjustment is not optional — the whole point of comparing “today's dollars” is that raw, un-adjusted growth overstates what a dollar saved today will actually be worth decades from now. The specific ages, contribution amount, and plan-length figures this case uses are stated scenario choices, disclosed openly rather than buried in a data table (SEE APPENDIX D). Jordan has one more week to decide before the retirement app's contribution amount is locked in for the year.

The vocabulary and the “how to…” skills this case will ask you to research are listed in the final appendix — that is where Challenge 1 sends you (SEE APPENDIX F). All figures in this case are sourced (SEE APPENDIX E).

Challenges

  1. Research and find an online resource that answers one of the “how to…” questions from Appendix F. Post a link to the resource as your submission.
  2. Post 250+ “excellent” words pitching a firm stance on which plan you'd choose and why. Your pitch must use the resource from Challenge 1 and include at least one original visual display of the data.
  3. Include the following:
    • D1 — The real rate of return, derived, not given. Convert Appendix C's sourced nominal average return and sourced average inflation rate from percents to numbers, then derive the annual real (inflation-adjusted) rate of return using real rate = (1 + nominal rate) / (1 + inflation rate) − 1. Then convert that annual real rate into an equivalent monthly real rate using (1 + annual real rate)^(1/12) − 1, showing the exponential expression evaluated step by step.
    • D2 — Priya's real retirement balance. The future-value-of-an-annuity formula FV = PMT × [((1 + r)^n − 1) / r] models Priya's situation. Using D1's derived monthly real rate, Appendix A's monthly contribution, and Appendix A's number of months, compute Priya's account balance in today's real dollars at retirement. Show the (1 + r)^n exponent term evaluated as its own explicit step.
    • D3 — The Bridge Plan's required contribution. Rearrange D2's formula to solve for the monthly contribution: PMT = FV × r / [(1 + r)^n − 1]. Using D2's target balance, D1's monthly real rate, and Appendix B's Bridge Plan number of months, compute the exact monthly contribution the Bridge Plan requires. State this amount as a percent increase over Priya's own monthly contribution from Appendix A.
    • D4 — The Extended Plan's required contribution, and the margin between the two plans. Repeat D3's method using Appendix B's Extended Plan number of months instead of the Bridge Plan's. Then compute the percent by which the Extended Plan's required monthly contribution is lower than the Bridge Plan's D3 figure.
    • D5 — The nominal-rate comparison. Recompute D3's Bridge Plan monthly contribution one more time, using Appendix C's un-adjusted nominal rate of return in place of D1's derived real rate. Show this “nominal-rate” contribution amount side by side with D3's correct, real-rate contribution amount, and compute the percent by which the nominal-rate figure understates the true amount required.
  4. An appendix showing evidence of your original thinking — pictures, screenshots, notes, work. Doesn't count toward your word count.
  5. Participate twice or more in the case study discussion. If absent, post 500 “excellent” words on the questions above instead.

Appendix C — Growth and Inflation Assumptions

The two real-world rates that D1's derivation combines, and the mechanism (inflation) this case is named for.

QuantityValue
Long-run average annual nominal rate of return, diversified U.S. stock index (S&P 500, dividends reinvested)10% (rounded) [S2]
Long-run average annual U.S. inflation rate (Consumer Price Index, CPI-U)3.1% [S3]

A dividend is a small cash payment a company sometimes sends to anyone who owns its stock, out of its own profits; “dividends reinvested” means that cash is automatically used to buy more stock instead of being paid out, which is the standard, complete way a long-run stock-index return like [S2] is measured. The Consumer Price Index (CPI-U) [S3] is built by regularly checking the price of the same fixed list of everyday purchases — groceries, rent, gas, and the like — across many U.S. cities and tracking how that total price changes year over year; that year-over-year change is what “the inflation rate” means throughout this case.
“Nominal” here means the raw, un-adjusted growth rate an account actually earns; it is the number before inflation is taken into account. “Real” (used throughout this case) means that same kind of number after inflation's effect has been removed, so it reflects actual buying power rather than a raw dollar count.
Referenced from Background and forces D1's real-rate derivation, D2–D4's growth computations, and D5's nominal-vs-real comparison.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which plan to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 · The Real Rate of Return (D1)

    Convert Appendix C's nominal return and inflation rate to numbers, derive the annual real rate using (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation) − 1, then convert to a monthly real rate using (1 + annual real rate)^(1/12) − 1.

    Step 2 · Priya's Real Retirement Balance (D2)

    Using D1's monthly real rate, Appendix A's monthly contribution, and Appendix A's number of months, compute Priya's account balance in today's real dollars: FV = PMT × [((1 + r)^n − 1) / r].

    Step 3 · The Bridge Plan's Required Contribution (D3)

    Rearrange D2's formula: PMT = FV × r / [(1 + r)^n − 1]. Using D2's target balance, D1's monthly real rate, and Appendix B's Bridge Plan months, find the required monthly contribution and the percent increase over Priya's own $400/month.

    Step 4 · The Extended Plan's Required Contribution and the Margin (D4)

    Repeat D3's method using Appendix B's Extended Plan months. Then compute the percent by which this contribution is lower than the Bridge Plan's D3 figure.

    Step 5 · The Nominal-Rate Comparison (D5)

    Recompute D3's Bridge Plan contribution using Appendix C's un-adjusted nominal rate instead of D1's real rate. Find the “nominal-rate” contribution amount and the percent by which it understates D3's correct figure.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    There is no additional fact hidden from you in Appendices A–D — this case's tension is already fully disclosed and fully computable from the numbers you have. The “twist” is the moment D5 actually lands: recompute the Bridge Plan's contribution using the nominal rate instead of the real rate, and compare.

    Many rooms expect “just use the rate everyone quotes” to be close enough. Once you finish D5, check: how far off was the nominal-rate figure from D3's correct, real-rate figure? Does that change how much you trust your own Anchor 1 gut vote?
