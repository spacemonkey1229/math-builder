Case Study: Roofline Call
MS | Math | Case Study | 2026-27 | Heroes Academy

## Overview

Colton Marsh has the roof plan open on two monitors and a client meeting in ninety minutes.

He's been drafting for six months at Marsh & Iyer Design-Build, and this is the first framing set
he's allowed to sign off on himself before it goes to the truss shop. The client — a couple adding
a bedroom wing to their ranch house — keeps forwarding him photos of houses on the next street
over. "We want something that doesn't look like a box," the email says. Colton knows exactly what
that means: they want the roof to wrap the corners instead of just running straight to two gable
ends.

He pulls up both roof plans side by side. One is the simple version he drew first — two planes
meeting at a single ridge line, the kind of roof a framing crew can cut with a speed square and
not think twice about. The other wraps all four sides, the kind that photographs better from the
street and sheds rain off every wall instead of dumping it off two ends — but every corner where
those extra planes meet is a compound angle a framer has to mark by hand, one rafter at a time.

His supervisor sticks her head in. "Whichever one you send to the shop this afternoon is the one
we're framing. You've got the numbers — which one actually pencils out?"

Colton pulls up his takeoff spreadsheet and starts counting rafters.

## Background

Colton Marsh is a residential architectural drafter at Marsh & Iyer Design-Build, a small firm
that both designs and builds its own projects. He is responsible for producing the framing plan a
crew can cut directly from — which means every angle on the plan has to be one a framer can
actually mark and cut, not just something that looks right on the elevation drawing.

A framing plan is a handoff, not a picture. What Colton actually sends to the truss shop is a set
of rafter lengths and cut angles the crew can lay out on real lumber with a **speed square** — a
triangular layout tool with degree markings and a built-in rafter scale (invented by Albert
Swanson in 1925 and still standard on every job site) that lets a carpenter align a pitch number,
scribe one line, and repeat that exact mark down eighty rafters without measuring an angle twice.
A **common rafter**'s cuts — the **birdsmouth** seat cut at the eave and the plumb cut at the
ridge — are both single-bevel cuts: one angle, marked once, cut the same way on every rafter in
the run. A **hip** or **jack rafter**'s cut is a **compound angle**: two bevels laid out at once
(the plumb cut and a second "cheek" cut across the board's face), because the rafter is climbing
the roof slope *and* crossing the building corner at 45 degrees at the same time. That second
bevel can't be read off a speed square the way a common rafter's single angle can — it has to be
laid out with a hip-valley scale or a saw set to two angles simultaneously, then test-fit before
the final cut. That is the real reason a compound cut takes a crew longer per rafter than a simple
one: not that the wood is harder to cut, but that marking the cut correctly the first time takes
more setup, more checking, and more chances to have to re-mark it.

The job is a single rectangular addition, **24 feet wide by 52 feet long** — one uninterrupted
rectangle in plan, no wings, no offsets, no jogs in the exterior wall — framed at a **6-in-12
roof pitch** (a rise of 6 inches for every 12 inches of horizontal run) on both roof options under
consideration, so the comparison isn't muddied by two different slopes (SEE APPENDIX B). Two roof
plans are on the table for the same footprint.

**The Gable Line** frames the roof as two planes meeting at one long ridge that runs the full
52-foot length of the building. Every rafter is a **common rafter** — same length, same single
angle cut at the eave (a birdsmouth seat cut) and the same single angle cut at the ridge, repeated
down the whole run. No compound angles anywhere on this plan.

**The Hip Wrap** frames the roof as four planes: the two long sides still carry common rafters, but
each end of the building is capped by a hip roof — four **hip rafters** running diagonally from
each corner up to the ends of a shortened ridge, filled in by a run of **jack rafters** that get
shorter and shorter as they approach each corner. Every hip and every jack rafter needs a
**compound-angle cut** — two bevels marked at once, not one — because the rafter isn't just
climbing the roof slope, it's also crossing the building at 45 degrees in plan.

Both plans use the same **2-inch by 8-inch dimension lumber**, spaced **16 inches on center**, for
every rafter, hip, and ridge board — a stock size and spacing Colton chose because it comfortably
clears the framing span this run requires with margin to spare (SEE APPENDIX A), so lumber grade
isn't a variable between the two options, only the roof geometry is. Framing lumber is priced by
the board-foot (SEE APPENDIX C), and every individual rafter cut — simple or compound — costs the
crew labor time at the shop's carpenter wage rate (SEE APPENDIX D).

The client's email is the only real pressure driving Option B: a hip roof sheds wind and rain off
every wall instead of concentrating it at two gable ends, and it's the look they specifically asked
for. The mechanism is simple geometry, not opinion — a gable roof only slopes over its two long
walls; the two short **eave**-less end walls run straight up to the ridge with no roof plane
above them at all, so wind-driven rain hits those walls directly. A hip roof wraps all four
walls in a sloped plane, so every wall — including both ends — sits under its own eave. Whether
that's worth what the compound cuts actually cost — in lumber, and in the crew's
time — is the number Colton has to work out and defend before the truss shop cutoff. All figures in
this case are sourced in APPENDIX F, or stated as an explicit modeling assumption in APPENDIX E
where no public figure exists. The vocabulary and the "how to…" skills this case will ask of you
are listed in APPENDIX H — that is where Challenge 1 sends you. (SEE APPENDIX G for further
reading on roof framing and hip-roof geometry.)

## Dilemma

You are Colton Marsh.
Do you:
A.) Draft The Gable Line — two roof planes, one straight ridge, every rafter a simple single-angle
cut OR
B.) Draft The Hip Wrap — four roof planes, hip and jack rafters at both ends, every one of them a
compound-angle cut
