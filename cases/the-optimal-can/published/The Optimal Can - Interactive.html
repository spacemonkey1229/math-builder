Case Study: The Optimal Can

Overview

The forming press on the line punches out another shell, and Priya Anand watches it ride the star wheel toward the necker with the same rhythm it has held all shift. On her second monitor, a spreadsheet is open to a single cell she has rebuilt four times since lunch. Somewhere down the line a shell drops and rattles across the guarding before an operator scoops it up without looking. The can no one dropped costs money whether it falls or not, and that is the whole problem on her screen.

Thursday's capital review has one slot left, and her supervisor already told her it is hers if she wants it. The pitch is simple to say out loud: change the shape of the can, use less aluminum for the same twelve ounces, save money on every can Ridgeline stamps from now on. It is much less simple to defend in a room that will ask her, in front of the plant manager, exactly how many months it takes to get the retooling money back — and whether that number is one she is willing to put her name on.

Her coffee has gone cold next to the keyboard. The math itself she trusts. What she does not yet know, with the die order due to the toolroom by Friday if this is going anywhere, is whether the number on this line beats the number finance is going to hold her to.

She pulls up two folders side by side: the shape Ridgeline runs today, and the shape calculus says is the true minimum. Somewhere between them is Thursday's answer.

Background

Priya Anand is a packaging and process engineer at Ridgeline Canning, a contract beverage canner that fills and cans carbonated and still beverages under co-packing agreements for a roster of regional brands. She owns the material-cost line item for the aluminum can body on Ridgeline's lines, and any change to that line item — including a request for retooling capital — is hers to propose and defend.

Every can Ridgeline stamps on this line must hold the same fill volume it holds today: this is the brand client's own bottling specification, fixed by contract, and it is not a variable either option below is allowed to change (Appendix A). What can change is the can body's proportions — its radius and height — as long as the enclosed volume stays exactly the same.

Hold The Line keeps the can body at the industry-standard proportions Ridgeline's forming and necking tooling already cuts. Nothing on the floor changes. The retooling cost is zero (Appendix A).
The Slim Retool re-cuts the forming dies to the proportions that a calculus optimization identifies as the minimum-surface-area shape for a cylinder holding the same fixed volume — using less aluminum sheet for every can, at the cost of a one-time die-and-tooling changeover (Appendices A, B and C).

Both options are modeled as a plain right circular cylinder — a flat top, a flat bottom, and a straight sidewall — because that is the shape the classic surface-area-minimization problem solves for, and because the two designs differ from each other only in radius and height, not in the material they are cut from or the process that forms them (Appendix A). Aluminum for the can body is priced the same for either shape (Appendix B), and Ridgeline's plant leadership will not fund the retool unless the material savings pay back the die-and-tooling cost within the capital-approval window finance holds every line-level retooling request to (Appendix C).

Where a figure in this case is not a matter of public record, it is stated as an explicit modeling assumption (Appendix D) rather than guessed, and every sourced figure is listed in Appendix E. The vocabulary and the “how to…” skills this case will ask of you are listed in Appendix G — that is where Challenge 1 sends you. Real photos and further reading are gathered in Appendix F.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 · The Surface-Area Function and Its Derivative (D1)

    A closed cylinder's surface area is S = 2πr² + 2πrh. Substitute h = V/(πr²) (the fixed-volume constraint, V = 21.65625 in³) so S depends on r alone: S(r) = 2πr² + 2V/r. Differentiate using the power rule. Enter dS/dr evaluated at the standard radius, r = 1.301 in, as a check that your derivative expression is right.

    Step 2 · The Critical Point and the Classification (D2)

    Set dS/dr = 0 and solve for the critical radius r* (Appendix A gives the closed form). Then find the optimal height h* from r* and the fixed volume. Enter both.

    Now prove r* is a local minimum, not a local maximum — first derivative test or a concavity check. Which sign does dS/dr have just below r*? Just above? (This part isn't graded by the calculator; write your reasoning in your pitch.)

    Step 3 · Material-Cost Comparison (D3)

    Using Appendix A's two surface areas and Appendix B's thickness, density and price, find each shape's aluminum weight per can (lb) and material cost per can ($), then the percent reduction The Slim Retool achieves over Hold The Line.

    Step 4 · Annual Savings, Payback, and Break-Even (D4)

    Scale Step 3's per-can savings up to Appendix C's annual volume, then find the payback period on Appendix C's retooling cost, in months. Finally, find the break-even annual volume — the minimum cans/yr at which The Slim Retool would pay back exactly within Appendix C's ceiling.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before opening this — in a live discussion, this section is addressed at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    This Case Ships Without a Twist

    Research for this case considered one natural candidate for a mid-discussion twist: a change to Ridgeline's annual-volume assumption (MA3), such as a brand client doubling its order — exactly the kind of real information a plant only learns after a capital request is drafted.

    That candidate turned out to only restate the break-even calculation D4 already asks you to compute — dropping it as a "twist" mid-discussion would either leak that answer early or require inventing a second unsourced number this case's research did not turn up a defensible basis for. Rather than manufacture a number to force a twist beat, this case ships without one.

    The pressure a twist would normally add is still here — it's in the Deep Conflict discussion your facilitator runs instead: a saving that's certain per can, against a bill that's certain in dollars, and four separately stated assumptions (Appendix D) any one of which can move the answer.
