Overview

Forty minutes to doors, and Priya Nakamura is on her knees behind the House-Right speaker stack with a multimeter, watching a fault light blink where a fault light has no business blinking. The second monitor amp channel is dead. Not dim, not crackling — dead, confirmed twice, and the rental company's after-hours line rings through to a voicemail box that hasn't been checked tonight.

That leaves one working monitor circuit for the whole show, and one decision that has to be made before the house manager starts seating people: which floor wedge gets the lead vocal.

She already knows both candidate spots. The venue's crew has used the House-Left position for years out of habit. Nobody has ever measured whether it's actually the better one — they've just never had a reason to ask, because there used to be two wedges and it never mattered which was which. Tonight it matters.

Priya pulls up the stage plot on her laptop, the mic's spec sheet in a browser tab beside it, and the blocking notes from Tuesday's run-through. Somewhere upstairs, the house manager is telling early arrivals that doors are running a few minutes behind schedule. Priya doesn't look up.

She opens a blank sheet of graph paper and starts plotting.

Background

Priya Nakamura is the front-of-house sound engineer for tonight's performance at the Ridgeline Playhouse, a mid-sized community theater. At a full-size touring show, mixing what the audience hears (front-of-house) and mixing what performers hear through their own monitors are two separate jobs, usually done by two different engineers. Ridgeline is small enough that Priya runs both tonight — which is exactly why this call is hers alone to make. She owns the mix for the evening and is the person the house manager will come find if the show gets stopped by a feedback squeal mid-song. (The house manager is a different job again, and not a sound job at all: the person responsible for the audience-facing side of the building itself — seating, ushers, doors, safety. Tonight the house manager's only connection to this decision is who comes looking for Priya if it goes wrong.) With the second monitor amp channel down (SEE APPENDIX A), she has to commit the lead vocal's floor wedge to one of exactly two physical positions before the doors open.

Here is why that is a forced, single choice rather than "use both": each monitor amp channel powers exactly one floor wedge. Tonight only one channel is alive, which means only one wedge position can carry any monitor sound at all — whichever one Priya commits to gets the lead vocal's entire monitor mix for the whole show, and the other stays silent all night.

House-Left — the wedge position the crew has used by default for years, roughly in front of the mic and somewhat off to one side (SEE APPENDIX A).

House-Right — the position that opened up only because the failed amp channel used to feed a second wedge there; it sits closer to the mic but almost directly off to the side of it (SEE APPENDIX A).

The risk both positions share is the same one every live sound engineer manages: a floor monitor is a loudspeaker sitting near an open, live microphone, and if too much of the monitor's own sound finds its way back into that microphone, the system re-amplifies its own signal until it squeals — audio feedback, one of the most notorious failure modes in live sound. An uncontrolled feedback loop is not a small mistake: it is a sudden, piercing howl that can hurt performers' and audience members' ears at close range, damage speakers, and force whoever is running the board to kill the channel and stop the number cold, sometimes mid-line.

How close a mix can be pushed before that happens is called the gain before feedback, and it depends on exactly two things once the loudspeaker's own output is fixed: how far the monitor sits from the mic, and how sensitive the mic actually is in the direction the monitor sits in (SEE APPENDIX D). Picture the mic "listening" hardest in a heart-shaped zone pointing straight out in front of it, and listening less and less as a sound source moves off to the side or behind it — that heart shape is exactly what "cardioid" means. The lead vocal mic is a directional, cardioid model — most sensitive straight ahead, and built to reject sound from other angles by different amounts depending on how far off-axis that angle is (SEE APPENDIX B). A monitor that sits closer but nearly out of the mic's pickup pattern can, in principle, be safer than one that sits farther away but more in line with it — which is exactly why the two candidate positions are not an easy call by eye.

The lead vocalist, Dana Voss, sings the opening verse standing at a fixed mic stand — but during the bridge, she picks up the same mic handheld and crosses the stage to hit a new mark for the final chorus, holding the mic's aim steady throughout (SEE APPENDIX C, MA2). Whichever position Priya commits to has to stay safe not just for the static verse, but for every blocking mark Dana actually holds during the number. Where a figure in this case is not a matter of public record, it is stated as an explicit modeling assumption (SEE APPENDIX E) rather than guessed, and every sourced figure is listed in Appendix F. The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in Appendix G — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

Appendix B — The Microphone's Polar Pattern

Dana's handheld vocal mic is a cardioid dynamic microphone — picture it "listening" hardest in a heart-shaped zone pointing straight out in front, and listening less and less as a sound source moves off to the side or behind it. Its directional sensitivity, as a function of the angle θ off its on-axis heading, follows the cardioid polar equation:

g(θ) = 0.5 · (1 + cos θ), normalized so that g(0°) = 1 (full sensitivity, on-axis) [S1].

A microphone's sensitivity converts to a decibel pattern-rejection value using: Pattern rejection (dB) = −20 · log₁₀(g(θ))

Appendix D — How Gain-Before-Feedback Is Combined

Gain-before-feedback depends on several factors — directivity, mic-to-loudspeaker distance, room acoustics, and open-mic count, among others [S2]. Two of those differ between House-Left and House-Right tonight: mic-to-wedge distance and the wedge's angle off the mic's on-axis heading. The others are identical for both options tonight (MA4).

Distance term (dB) = 20 · log₁₀(r), r in feet — the inverse-square law: halving distance adds 6 dB, doubling it removes 6 dB [S2].

Pattern-rejection term is Appendix B's rejection value at the wedge's angle θ.

GBF score (dB) = distance term (dB) + pattern-rejection term (dB). A higher score means more headroom before feedback — the safer reading at that moment.
