Overview

The truck's heater is still ticking as it cools, parked on the shoulder of an unfinished road, a raw new row of houses rising out of the mud in front of the windshield. Benjamin sits with both hands on the wheel, just looking at the frames for a minute before the phone in the cupholder buzzes.

An email from his lumber supplier. He reads it twice.

Inflation. One word, and it won't leave him alone.

He and his wife have never been careless with money — that part he's sure of. What he's not sure of is what to do with what's left over at the end of each month, now that "left over" is starting to feel less safe than it used to. Friends have been telling him the same thing for a year: stop just saving it — put it to work.

He sets the phone down, pulls his laptop across the passenger seat, and opens a browser tab.

Background

Benjamin Tippets is thirty years old, a carpenter and homebuilder outside Nashville, Tennessee. He and his wife have spent a decade building a genuinely disciplined financial life: no debt, a paid-off house, a full year of emergency cash set aside, life and disability insurance in place, and their IRA and 401(k) contributions maxed out every year. Any additional investing he does now happens outside those tax-sheltered accounts, so the returns are exposed to taxes. He has two young children and wants his investing plan to be something he sets up once and only revisits twice a year — his real focus, and his real passion, stays on building houses.

After months of research and conversations with friends in finance, Benjamin has narrowed his options to two well-known strategies, each built entirely from ordinary exchange-traded funds (ETFs) he could buy today.

Warren Buffett's 90/10 Portfolio puts 90% of the money in a low-cost S&P 500 index fund and the remaining 10% in short-term government bonds (SEE APPENDIX A).

Ray Dalio's All-Weather Portfolio spreads the money across five pieces: 40% long-term bonds, 30% stocks, 15% intermediate-term bonds, 7.5% gold, and 7.5% commodities (SEE APPENDIX B).

Every figure in this case that describes how these two portfolios have actually performed — return, volatility, and worst-case loss — is drawn from a real, dated backtest of the exact fund allocations above, not from a general description of "the stock market" (SEE APPENDICES C, D and E). Benjamin openly admits he doesn't know how he'd react, emotionally, to watching a portfolio drop 40% in a single stretch — that uncertainty is part of what he's weighing.

The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in Appendix F.

Benjamin has no deadline pressuring him tonight, but he wants an answer he can commit to and stop revisiting.

    Step 3 — D4/D5: 30-year projection, with and without the Year-3 shock

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    One more thing about Buffett's 90/10 rule. It didn't come from a book on how to build wealth over a career. Buffett wrote it in his 2013 Berkshire Hathaway shareholder letter as an instruction for the trustee managing his own wife's inheritance, after his death — a single lump sum, for someone who would never add another dollar to it and would draw it down slowly for the rest of her life.

    Benjamin is thirty, actively earning, and planning to add money to this account every single year for the next three decades. Does "good enough for Buffett's own wife" still mean "good enough for Benjamin"?
