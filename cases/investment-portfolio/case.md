## Overview

The truck's heater is still ticking as it cools, parked on the shoulder of an unfinished road, a raw new row of houses rising out of the mud in front of the windshield. Benjamin sits with both hands on the wheel, just looking at the frames for a minute before the phone in the cupholder buzzes.

An email from his lumber supplier. He reads it twice.

*Inflation.* One word, and it won't leave him alone.

He and his wife have never been careless with money — that part he's sure of. What he's not sure of is what to do with what's left over at the end of each month, now that "left over" is starting to feel less safe than it used to. Friends have been telling him the same thing for a year: *stop just saving it — put it to work.*

He sets the phone down, pulls his laptop across the passenger seat, and opens a browser tab.

## Background

Benjamin Tippets is thirty years old, a carpenter and homebuilder outside Nashville, Tennessee. He and his wife have spent a decade building a genuinely disciplined financial life: no debt, a paid-off house, a full year of emergency cash set aside, life and disability insurance in place, and their IRA and 401(k) contributions maxed out every year. Any additional investing he does now happens outside those tax-sheltered accounts, so the returns are exposed to taxes. He has two young children and wants his investing plan to be something he sets up once and only revisits twice a year — his real focus, and his real passion, stays on building houses.

After months of research and conversations with friends in finance, Benjamin has narrowed his options to two well-known strategies, each built entirely from ordinary exchange-traded funds (ETFs) he could buy today.

**Warren Buffett's 90/10 Portfolio** puts 90% of the money in a low-cost S&P 500 index fund and the remaining 10% in short-term government bonds (SEE APPENDIX A).

**Ray Dalio's All-Weather Portfolio** spreads the money across five pieces: 40% long-term bonds, 30% stocks, 15% intermediate-term bonds, 7.5% gold, and 7.5% commodities (SEE APPENDIX B).

Every figure in this case that describes how these two portfolios have actually performed — return, volatility, and worst-case loss — is drawn from a real, dated backtest of the exact fund allocations above, not from a general description of "the stock market" (SEE APPENDICES C, D and E). Benjamin openly admits he doesn't know how he'd react, emotionally, to watching a portfolio drop 40% in a single stretch — that uncertainty is part of what he's weighing.

The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in APPENDIX F.

Benjamin has no deadline pressuring him tonight, but he wants an answer he can commit to and stop revisiting.

## Dilemma

You are Benjamin Tippets.

Do you:

A.) Put your excess savings in **Warren Buffett's 90/10 Portfolio** OR

B.) Put your excess savings in **Ray Dalio's All-Weather Portfolio**

## Appendix D: Modeling Assumptions

> These figures are **not sourced** — they are stated scenario assumptions chosen for this case, needed to run a projection Appendix C's real historical data alone cannot answer (nobody can cite a fact about the next 30 years). A Hero may challenge them in discussion — that is a legitimate move.

| # | Assumption | Value used | Basis | Sensitivity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MA1 | Risk-free rate, used only for the Sharpe-ratio calculation in D6 | 1.5% per year | A round approximation of average short-term U.S. Treasury / cash yields over the same trailing 20-year window Appendix C's 20-year figures cover — the standard "safe baseline" a Sharpe ratio subtracts before dividing by risk. | The Sharpe-ratio comparison in D6 is sensitive to this value — a different risk-free-rate assumption changes both portfolios' computed ratios, though not necessarily by the same amount. |
| MA2 | Forward-looking expected return, used only for the D4/D5 projections | Each portfolio's own 30-year historical annualized return from Appendix C (9.98% for Buffett 90/10, 7.40% for All-Weather), applied forward as a constant assumed annual return for all 30 projection years (except the Year-3 override in D5) | Trailing long-run historical performance is one common, transparent starting point for a forward-looking return assumption when no one can know the actual future return in advance. This case states plainly that it is only an assumption: real returns vary year to year (see Appendix C's standard-deviation rows) and past performance is not a guarantee of future results. | The 30-year projection in D4 and D5 is sensitive to this value — a different assumed forward return changes the projected dollar totals for both portfolios. |
| MA3 | Contribution schedule for the D4/D5 projections | Benjamin contributes 40% of his $100,000 annual income each year, and that income (and therefore the dollar contribution) grows by 2.6% per year — the average annual U.S. inflation rate from 2000-2026 [S4] — starting from Year 1 | Stated directly in Benjamin's own plan: "contribute 40% of income consistently," with income assumed to keep pace with inflation rather than staying fixed in nominal dollars for 30 years. | Not sensitive to a modeling choice made by the case author — this is Benjamin's own stated plan, restated here as a fixed input so every Hero projects from the same numbers. |
| MA4 | Rounding | Dollar totals round to the nearest whole dollar; percentages and ratios round to two decimal places | Keeps every table in this case readable and consistent Hero-to-Hero. | Not sensitive at this level of precision. |
