Overview

Priya Nakamura has two truss elevations pinned to the light table and a shop that stops cutting steel at four o'clock.

She's the newest P.E. at Ferrand & Cole Structural, and this is the first bridge she's allowed to seal on her own stamp. The job is small — a footbridge over Miller's Creek, replacing the rail-trail's washed-out plank crossing — but the county's purchasing agent keeps forwarding her the same photo: a pallet of salvaged steel angle in the shop yard, pulled off a footbridge the county tore out last spring. “Free steel is free steel,” his email says. “Use what we've got.”

Priya has been staring at that pallet since Tuesday. She can see, just by eye, that the pieces are short — shorter than she'd normally spec for a truss this size. Whether “short” means “won't reach” is a different question, and it's the one her supervisor just asked her, standing in her doorway with her coat already on.

“Shop's cutting new steel at four either way,” she says. “Which truss are we drawing — the one that needs new diagonals, or the one that can actually use what's sitting in the yard?”

Priya pulls the tape measure off her desk and looks at the two elevations again.

Background

Priya Nakamura is a staff structural engineer at Ferrand & Cole Structural, a small civil engineering firm under contract to the Kestrel County Parks Department. Her job is to finalize the shop drawing for a replacement pedestrian truss bridge over Miller's Creek, on a heavily used rail-trail — a drawing the fabricator's shop will cut steel from directly, so every member length on it has to be a real, buildable length, not just a number that looks right on the elevation.

A truss is rigid for a specific geometric reason: a triangle is the only polygon whose shape is fixed once its three side lengths are set — a rectangle can rack into a parallelogram under load, but a triangle cannot deform without one of its sides changing length (see Appendix H for a sourced explanation). That is why every truss, regardless of style, is built as a chain of triangles rather than any other shape, and it is why the specific side lengths available to build each triangle — not just the finished silhouette — decide whether a truss design is even physically assembleable before a single number is checked for strength.

The county's parks department wants to reuse a pallet of salvaged structural steel angle — 24 pieces, each 3.5 feet long, pulled from a smaller footbridge the county decommissioned last year — as the truss's diagonal web members, to cut the new bridge's material cost. Two truss layouts are on the table for the same 24-foot span, and the salvaged pieces do not fit both of them the same way (see Appendix B and Appendix C).

The Warren Line frames the span as three 8-foot panels with no vertical posts, at a truss depth chosen so every web triangle is equilateral — the standard configuration for a Warren-style pony truss of this panel length, because equal-length web members put every diagonal into the same alternating pattern of tension and compression. That geometry needs every diagonal to be a full 8 feet long. Two of the salvaged 3.5-foot pieces, laid end-to-corner, cannot reach across an 8-foot panel and meet — the county's steel does not fit this design at all, and every diagonal has to be cut from new stock (see Appendix D).

The Low Line frames the same 24-foot span as four shallower 6-foot panels, at a reduced truss depth required to clear the county's floodway ordinance for the walkway underside during high water. At that depth, two salvaged 3.5-foot pieces reach across a 6-foot panel with room to spare — the salvage fits, and every interior diagonal in this design can be built from the county's steel at no material cost. The shallower depth carries a real structural cost of its own: for the same span and load, a shallower truss puts more force into its top and bottom chords, so The Low Line's chord steel has to be a heavier structural section than The Warren Line's (see Appendix A) — and its four panels, versus three, mean one more pair of panel-point connections for the shop to fabricate (see Appendix E).

Structural steel is priced by the pound and gusset-plate fabrication is priced by the shop's labor rate (see Appendices D and F); every dollar figure in this case is either cited to a real source (see Appendix G) or stated as an explicit modeling assumption (see Appendix F) where no public figure exists. A shallower truss carries more force in its chords than a deeper one spanning the same distance under the same load, because the chord force in a truss scales inversely with the truss's depth (see Appendix H) — which is the real reason The Low Line's reduced depth forces a heavier chord section even though its span and loading are identical to The Warren Line's.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Truss Geometry Setup (D1)

    Read The Warren Line's repeating web triangle and The Low Line's repeating interior web triangle straight off Appendix B, and enter their three side lengths.

    Step 2 — Triangle Inequality Check (D2)

    Add the two salvaged pieces, then compare that sum to each option's panel length to see which truss the salvage steel can actually close.

    Step 3 — The Warren Line's Triangles (D3)

    Using the Warren Line's three equal side lengths from Step 1, compute the interior angle at any one vertex with the Law of Cosines, then decide acute/obtuse/right.

    Step 4 — The Low Line's Triangles (D4)

    Classify The Low Line's repeating interior triangle (name the base and the two legs, find its largest angle) and its one non-repeating end triangle (7 ft, 6 ft, 3.5 ft — Appendix C) the same way.
