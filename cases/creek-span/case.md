Case Study: Creek Span
MS | Math | Case Study | 2026-27 | Heroes Academy

## Overview

Suri Nakamura has two truss elevations pinned to the light table and a shop that stops cutting
steel at four o'clock.

She's the newest P.E. at Ferrand & Cole Structural, and this is the first bridge she's allowed to
seal on her own stamp. The job is small — a short footbridge over Miller's Creek, replacing the
rail-trail's washed-out plank crossing — but the county's purchasing agent keeps forwarding her the
same photo: a pallet of salvaged steel angle in the shop yard, pulled off a footbridge the county
tore out last spring. "Free steel is free steel," his email says. "Use what we've got."

Suri has been staring at that pallet since Tuesday. She can see, just by eye, that the pieces are
short — shorter than she'd normally spec for a truss this size. Whether "short" means "won't reach"
is a different question, and it's the one her supervisor just asked her, standing in her doorway
with her coat already on.

"Shop's cutting new steel at four either way," she says. "Which truss are we drawing — the one that
needs new diagonals, or the one that can actually use what's sitting in the yard?"

Suri pulls the tape measure off her desk and looks at the two elevations again.

## Background

Suri Nakamura is a staff structural engineer at Ferrand & Cole Structural, a small civil
engineering firm under contract to the Kestrel County Parks Department. She grew up in this town,
close enough to Miller's Creek that she crossed the old plank footbridge every school morning as a
kid, so the crossing she's now been asked to redesign is not just another job site to her — a fact
she keeps carefully separate from the engineering. She still carries a tape measure in her bag out
of old habit, pulling it out even in meetings where she'll never actually use it. Her job here is to
finalize the shop drawing for a replacement pedestrian truss bridge over Miller's Creek, on a
heavily used rail-trail — a drawing the fabricator's shop will cut steel from directly, so every
member length on it has to be a real, buildable length, not just a number that looks right on the
elevation.

A truss is rigid for a specific geometric reason: a triangle is the only polygon whose shape is
fixed once its three side lengths are set — a rectangle can rack into a parallelogram under load,
but a triangle cannot deform without one of its sides changing length (SEE APPENDIX H for a
sourced explanation). That is why every truss, regardless of style, is built as a chain of
triangles rather than any other shape, and it is why the specific **side lengths** available to
build each triangle — not just the finished silhouette — decide whether a truss design is even
physically assembleable before a single number is checked for strength.

The county's parks department wants to reuse a pallet of salvaged structural steel angle — 24
pieces, each **3.5 feet long**, pulled from a smaller footbridge the county decommissioned last
year — as the truss's diagonal **web** members, to cut the new bridge's material cost. Two truss
layouts are on the table for the same 24-foot span, and the salvaged pieces do not fit both of them
the same way (SEE APPENDIX B AND C).

**The Warren Line** frames the span as three 8-foot panels with no vertical posts, at a truss depth
chosen so every web triangle is **equilateral** — the standard configuration for a Warren-style
pony truss of this panel length, because equal-length web members put every diagonal into the same
alternating pattern of tension and compression. That geometry needs every diagonal to be a full
8 feet long. Two of the salvaged 3.5-foot pieces, laid end-to-corner, cannot reach across an 8-foot
panel and meet — the county's steel does not fit this design at all, and every diagonal has to be
cut from new stock (SEE APPENDIX D).

**The Low Line** frames the same 24-foot span as four shallower 6-foot panels, at a reduced truss
depth required to clear the county's floodway ordinance for the walkway underside during high
water. At that depth, two salvaged 3.5-foot pieces reach across a 6-foot panel with room to spare —
the salvage fits, and every interior diagonal in this design can be built from the county's steel
at no material cost. The shallower depth carries a real structural cost of its own: for the same
span and load, a shallower truss puts more force into its top and bottom chords, so The Low Line's
chord steel has to be a heavier structural section than The Warren Line's (SEE APPENDIX A) — and its
four panels, versus three, mean one more pair of gusset-plate connections for the shop to fabricate
(SEE APPENDIX E).

Structural steel is priced by the pound and gusset-plate fabrication is priced by the shop's
labor rate (SEE APPENDICES D AND F); every dollar figure in this case is either cited to a real
source (SEE APPENDIX G) or stated as an explicit modeling assumption (SEE APPENDIX F) where no
public figure exists. The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in
Appendix I, where Challenge 1 sends you. (SEE APPENDIX H for further reading on truss triangulation
and the Triangle Inequality Theorem.)

## Dilemma

You are Suri Nakamura.
Do you:
A.) Detail The Warren Line — three 8-foot equilateral-triangle panels, every diagonal cut from new
steel OR
B.) Detail The Low Line — four shallower 6-foot panels built from the county's salvaged 3.5-foot
diagonals, with heavier chord steel

## Appendix A — Structural Steel Angle Reference

| Chord size, The Warren Line (deeper truss, lower chord force) | L3×3×1/4 structural steel angle |
| Weight per linear foot, L3×3×1/4 angle | 4.89 lb/ft [S2] |
| Chord size, The Low Line (shallower truss, higher chord force) | L3×3×3/8 structural steel angle |
| Weight per linear foot, L3×3×3/8 angle | 7.17 lb/ft [S3] |

A shallower truss carries more force in its chords than a deeper one spanning the same distance
under the same load, because the chord force in a truss scales inversely with the truss's depth
(SEE APPENDIX H) — which is the real reason The Low Line's reduced depth forces a heavier chord
section even though its span and loading are identical to The Warren Line's.

## Appendix B — The Truss Geometry

Suri's own layout for this crossing — not a public bridge plan (Ferrand & Cole's project
drawings are proprietary), but built entirely from real, standard Warren-truss geometry and the
county's own floodway clearance requirement.

| | The Warren Line (A) | The Low Line (B) |
|---|---|---|
| Span | 24 ft | 24 ft |
| Panel count | 3 | 4 |
| Panel length (bottom and top chord) | 8 ft | 6 ft |
| Truss depth | 6.93 ft | 1.80 ft |
| Repeating web triangle side lengths | 8 ft, 8 ft, 8 ft | 6 ft, 3.5 ft, 3.5 ft |
| Repeating web triangle, by side length | equilateral | isosceles |
| Repeating web triangle, by angle | (SEE CHALLENGE D3) | (SEE CHALLENGE D4) |

The Warren Line's depth (6.93 ft) is set so that every diagonal equals the 8-ft panel length —
the textbook equilateral-triangle Warren configuration. The Low Line's depth (1.80 ft) is fixed
not by truss theory but by the county's floodway clearance ordinance for the walkway underside
(SEE APPENDIX H); the panel length that results from fitting the salvaged 3.5-ft diagonals across
that depth is 6 ft, which is why The Low Line uses four panels instead of three over the same
24-ft span.

## Appendix C — Salvage Steel & the End-Panel Detail

| | Value |
|---|---|
| Salvaged steel angle pieces available | 24 pieces, L2×2×1/4, 3.5 ft each |
| Source | Decommissioned footbridge, Kestrel County Parks Department yard |
| Interior web triangle diagonals needed, The Low Line | 8 pieces (2 per panel × 4 panels) |
| Bottom-chord panel length the salvage pair must span, The Warren Line | 8.0 ft |
| Bottom-chord panel length the salvage pair must span, The Low Line | 6.0 ft |

Whether two 3.5-ft salvaged pieces can actually reach across either of those panel lengths and
meet at the top-chord node — closing a real, buildable triangle — is exactly what the Triangle
Inequality Theorem in Challenge D2 is for.

The Low Line's two end panels each carry one additional detail: a diagonal **sway brace**, a
standard end-panel wind-bracing member the fabricator specs at a fixed 7-ft stock length,
running from the end bottom-chord node up to the *second* top-chord node — spanning two panels
instead of one. That brace, the 6-ft top-chord segment it lands on, and the regular 3.5-ft
salvage diagonal already framing that bay together form one triangle with three different side
lengths (7 ft, 6 ft, 3.5 ft) — the only triangle in either truss option that is not equilateral or
isosceles (SEE CHALLENGE D4).
