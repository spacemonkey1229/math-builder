Case Study: Apron Order
MS | Math | Case Study | 2026-27 | Heroes Academy

## Overview

Devon Reyes has the cutting table lights on at 6:00 in the morning, a bolt of canvas unrolled to its full length, and a boutique's rush order sitting on the clipboard: forty bib aprons, ready for pickup Thursday, no exceptions.

Two marker layouts are chalked out side by side on the table's paper backing. One uses the fold — half the pattern pieces, half the tracing, half the pinning, a shortcut Devon has used a hundred times before. The other lays every piece flat and open, packed edge to edge like a jigsaw puzzle nobody asked for, but using less cloth to do it, which is exactly the kind of number the shop owner watches.

Devon's boss sticks her head in the cutting room, coffee in hand, coat still on. "Whichever marker you cut from is the one we're stuck with. Fabric's not cheap, but neither is your morning — and I've got a client meeting in twenty minutes who's going to ask which one we ran."

Devon picks up the tailor's chalk, looks at the bolt, and does the math one more time before committing a single cut.

## Background

Devon Reyes cuts and marks fabric at a small custom-apparel shop, responsible for laying out pattern pieces on stock fabric before a single cut is made — and for knowing which layout actually costs less to run, not just which one looks more efficient on the table.

The job: **40 bib aprons**, cut from cotton canvas sold on a **44-inch-wide bolt** (SEE APPENDIX A). Every seam uses the industry-standard **⅝-inch seam allowance** (SEE APPENDIX B). Two marker layouts are on the table.

**Cut on the Fold** places the apron's body panel with one edge on a folded double layer of fabric, so tracing and pinning one half automatically produces a mirrored whole panel. It uses **1¼ yards of fabric per apron** and takes **3 minutes of marking time per apron** — fewer pieces to place and trace (SEE APPENDIX C).

**Cut Open** lays every piece fully flat, individually nested tighter against its neighbors on the bolt. It uses only **1⅙ yards of fabric per apron**, but takes **6 minutes of marking time per apron** — more pieces to place, and every one has to be matched against its mirror-image partner by hand (SEE APPENDIX C).

Every yard of fabric costs the same regardless of layout (SEE APPENDIX A). Every minute of marking labor costs the same regardless of which layout it goes toward (SEE APPENDIX D). The total job cost is yards used × fabric price, plus minutes used × labor rate — two line items that move in opposite directions as the layout changes.

Neither layout is machine-cut. Devon's shop is a small custom-apparel operation, not a factory with an automated, computerized fabric cutter — every marker here is chalked onto the fabric by hand with tailor's chalk, and every cut is made by hand with scissors or a hand-held rotary cutter (SEE APPENDIX F, MA4). That is exactly why the two layouts differ in marking time as well as yardage: more pieces to individually trace and match by hand is real labor, not a setting on a machine. The online Interactive version of this case includes a diagram comparing the two marker layouts side by side — Cut on the Fold's mirrored half-panel against the fold versus Cut Open's two separately nested mirror-image pieces — so you can see the difference, not just read about it.

Before either layout was chalked out, Devon also had to work out the apron's own real dimensions — the waist ties, the trimmed marker length, how many aprons a full bolt actually yields — all of it in the fractions and mixed numbers a tape measure and a fabric bolt actually come in, not tidy decimals.

All figures in this case are sourced in APPENDIX E, or stated as explicit modeling assumptions in APPENDIX F where no public figure exists. The vocabulary and the "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in APPENDIX H — that is where Challenge 1 sends you. If you want to go beyond the required math and learn more about the real trade this case is drawn from, SEE APPENDIX G for further reading.

## Appendix G — Learn More

Curious what marker layouts, seam allowances, and canvas fabric look like beyond this case? These
are real, currently-live resources for further reading — none of them are required for the
Challenges.

- [What is Marker, Marker Making, Marker Efficiency and How to Calculate Marker Efficiency?](https://www.onlineclothingstudy.com/2013/05/what-is-marker-marker-making-marker.html) — an industry explainer on how real garment factories lay out pattern pieces on a fabric roll and measure how tightly a marker uses the cloth, the same fold-vs-open tradeoff Devon is weighing.
- [Cotton duck — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton_duck) — background on cotton duck (canvas), the heavy plain-woven cotton fabric Devon's shop is cutting the aprons from, including its weight grades and common uses.
- [Cut and sew — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cut_and_sew) — an overview of the cut-and-sew manufacturing process that this case's cutting-room scene is drawn from, from pattern to cut fabric to finished garment.
- [How to Add Seam Allowance & Sew It, Made Simple — TREASURIE](https://blog.treasurie.com/how-to-add-seam-allowance/) — a plain-language guide to what a seam allowance is and why every garment pattern, including Devon's apron, builds one in.
- [Rotary cutter — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotary_cutter) — background on the hand-held cutting tool Devon's shop actually uses, as introduced for garment cutting in 1979; the real, low-cost alternative to the automated, computerized cutting machines large factories run.
