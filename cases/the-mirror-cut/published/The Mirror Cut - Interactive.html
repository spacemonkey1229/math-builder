Overview

Sam Delgado has the nesting file open on one monitor and the bid deadline open on the other: 9:00 AM tomorrow.

Sam runs production at Kestrel Sheet Metal, a six-person shop that has never bid on anything this big. The contract is for a regional solar installer's racking brackets — the same bracket, stamped and laser-cut from steel, year after year, mounted under every panel the company installs across the region. Whichever nesting layout the quote is built on tonight is the layout the shop runs for the life of the contract. Underquote the steel and Kestrel eats the difference on every bracket, for years. Overquote it and one of the two much bigger shops also bidding on this job wins on price alone, and Kestrel doesn't get a second chance at a contract this size.

Sam pulls up both nesting files side by side on the cutting-software screen. One lays every bracket down the sheet in the same orientation, straight rows, easy to check at a glance — the programmer's first pass, the one anybody in the shop could run without a second look. The other alternates every other bracket upside-down, tucked into the gap the first one leaves — tighter, more steel actually turned into parts, but a longer, fussier toolpath that takes the machine more time to cut.

The shop's laser tech leans into the doorway. “Whichever file you send to the machine tonight is the one we're quoting the customer on. Which one actually wins?”

Sam pulls up the takeoff spreadsheet and starts counting brackets per sheet.

Background

Sam Delgado is the production lead at Kestrel Sheet Metal, a small job shop that fabricates custom steel brackets and mounting hardware. Sam is responsible for the number that goes on tomorrow's bid: the true cost to cut one finished bracket, because that number becomes the price Kestrel quotes for the entire contract — a fixed quantity of 40,000 identical brackets every year, for three years, the largest order the shop has ever bid on.

A laser-cutting nesting file is the arrangement of parts on a steel sheet before the machine ever fires. Nesting software lays copies of a part down on a rectangular sheet using translations (sliding a copy to a new position without turning it), reflections (flipping a copy over a line, producing its mirror image), rotations (turning a copy about a fixed point), and dilations (scaling a copy up or down by a scale factor, used here to build the small checking gauges the shop verifies parts against, not the production parts themselves). Two nesting files are on the table for the same bracket.

The Straight Run. Repeats the bracket down the sheet in the same orientation every time — every copy is a plain translation of the one before it. It is the layout a new hire could run on day one: nothing to double-check, nothing that can be loaded upside-down by mistake.
The Mirror Cut. Alternates the bracket's orientation from row to row — every other copy is a reflection of its neighbor across the seam between them, tucked so its narrow end nests into the wide end of the row above. That interlocking is what turns more of each sheet into finished brackets instead of scrap — but it is a longer, more careful toolpath: more places the laser has to lift and re-pierce the metal instead of cutting one long continuous line, and a slower pass near every seam where two mirrored brackets sit close enough together that heat from one cut can warp the part waiting next to it (see Appendix E).

The bracket itself is Sam's own part drawing, not a public design: a single flat trapezoid, corners at exact coordinates on a plan-view coordinate grid, in inches (see Appendix A). Every sheet is the same commercially stocked 48 in × 120 in steel sheet, 16-gauge cold-rolled (see Appendix B), priced by the pound. Every cut — simple or mirrored — costs the shop machine time at the laser's real cutting speed and pierce time, priced at the shop's blended machine-and-labor rate (see Appendix C). The full sheet-by-sheet comparison, including how many finished brackets each layout actually yields per sheet, is in Appendix D. All figures in this case are sourced in Appendix F, or stated as an explicit modeling assumption in Appendix E where no public figure exists for this exact shop, this exact part.     Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    D1 — Build The Straight Run by translation

    The Straight Run repeats the bracket down the sheet with plain translations. Starting from vertex C(4.5, 4), translate the bracket down the sheet twice, each time by the vector (0, +4.25) — the slot height from Appendix D. Where does vertex C land after both translations?

    D2 — Reflect, translate, and verify the line of symmetry

    Reflect the bracket across the x-axis, then translate the reflected copy down by the kerf spacing (0.25 in, Appendix B) to build The Mirror Cut's interlocking pair. Where does vertex C land, and what is the line of symmetry (a horizontal line y = …) of the two-part interlocked pair?

    D3 — Rotate 180° and cross-check

    Rotate the original bracket's vertex B(6, 0) by 180° about the midpoint (3, −0.125) you found in D2. It should land on the exact same point D2's reflection-then-translation put there.

    D4 — Rotate the interlocked block's bounding box 90° CCW

    The two-part interlocked block from D2/D3 has a bounding box 6.0 in wide × 8.25 in tall. Rotate its corners 90° counterclockwise about the origin. What are the new bounding box's width and height?

    D5 — Dilation and scale factor

    The shop's existing QC gauge (Appendix A) is a dilation of the full-size bracket. Find that scale factor, then use a new scale factor of 0.75 to dilate the full-size bracket's vertex C(4.5, 4) into a second, larger gauge. Where does C land?

    D6 — Full nesting takeoff

    Using Appendix A–D's own numbers, the notebook computes net cost per finished bracket for each layout live — material cost plus machine-time cost. Flex Appendix E's two machine-time assumptions (MA1 pierce multiplier, MA2 speed factor) with the sliders and watch whether the winner ever changes.
