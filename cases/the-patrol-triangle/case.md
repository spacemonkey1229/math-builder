# Case Study: Patrol Triangle

MS | Math | Case Study | 2026-27 | Adventure

---

## Overview

Derek Osei stands at the tailgate of the patrol truck, the radio in his hand still warm from the last call. Dust hangs over the acacia scrub behind him, kicked up minutes ago by a herd of impala that bolted for no reason anyone can explain yet. He's been sector lead here for three dry seasons, long enough to know that "no reason anyone can explain yet" is usually the first sign something is wrong.

Spread across the truck's hood are two laminated maps. One is crosshatched with long triangular wedges tracing the fence line. The other is a tighter mesh of smaller triangles fanning out from three ranger outposts. Both are patrol plans. Only one of them is going into next month's operations order.

The reserve director wants a recommendation before the evening briefing, and the dry season — the season poachers wait for, when waterholes shrink and animals cluster where a rifle can find them easily — starts in four days. Derek spent the morning walking both plans with a stopwatch and a tape measure, timing how long it actually takes a truck to reach the far corner of each triangle, and he already knows the honest answer isn't clean. One plan covers more ground. The other gets there faster.

He folds both maps under one arm, keys the radio, and starts walking back toward the ops tent.

## Background

Derek Osei is the patrol-sector lead ranger at Kerio Plains Reserve, a fenced wildlife reserve modeled on the real scale and staffing density of an active rhino conservancy (SEE APPENDIX A). He reports to the reserve's director of security operations and is responsible for recommending exactly one reserve-wide patrol geometry before the dry season's known poaching spike begins.

Two patrol plans are under final review. **The Perimeter Loop** rings the reserve's fence line with long, narrow triangular sectors that hug the boundary — a small number of sectors, each covering a wide stretch of ground, but each with a vertex far from the next (SEE APPENDIX B). **The Interior Web** replaces that ring with a denser mesh of smaller, more equal-sided triangular sectors radiating out from three fixed ranger outposts positioned inside the reserve (SEE APPENDIX C). Both plans use the same truck fleet, the same radio network, and the same maximum patrol speed (SEE APPENDIX A) — the decision turns entirely on the geometry of the sectors themselves, not on equipment or headcount.

Every sector, under either plan, must be staffed to the reserve's own real patrol-team density standard (SEE APPENDIX A) — one **patrol team** (a truck and its small crew of rangers, the reserve's basic staffing unit) per that many square kilometers of sector, and a sector short even one team is a sector the director will not approve. The reserve's total area is fixed (SEE APPENDIX A); neither plan may claim more ground than the reserve actually has.

Picture the reserve as a single 360 km² block of land with a fence tracing its outer boundary. The Perimeter Loop lays six sectors end to end in a chain running just inside that fence — like six long, thin slices cut from the outer rind of the block — so together they trace a ring around the whole boundary, leaving the block's interior untouched by this plan. The Interior Web instead plants three fixed outposts somewhere inside that same block, away from the fence, and at each outpost its own sectors radiate outward as a wedge-shaped fan, all sharing that one outpost as a common vertex — the same way spokes share a wheel's hub. Because every Interior Web sector is equilateral, the angle at that shared vertex is a fixed 60° for each wedge, so it would take exactly six of them, edge to edge, to sweep all the way around one outpost and close into a full circle. The eight sectors are not divided evenly: three fan out from Outpost 1, three from Outpost 2, and two from Outpost 3 — so no outpost's fan ever closes into a full circle; each is an open wedge of un-swept ground, not a complete ring. These three partial fans sit inside the block, nowhere near its outer rind, and nowhere touching each other either: because there are only three small fans and the block is large, there is plenty of interior ground between them, and none of the fence line itself, that no Interior Web sector reaches at all — that is the "real gap" this plan leaves.

Before either plan goes into next month's operations order, the reserve's three fixed outposts also need their own reported distances checked against each other. Locating a reported sighting from multiple outposts works like this: each outpost radios in how far away (and in what compass direction — a **bearing**) it estimates the sighting to be; if you already know exactly how far apart the three outposts are *from each other*, those three separate estimates can be cross-checked against each other and combined into one trustworthy location, the way three people at three known street corners can pin down a fourth address between them just from their own distances to it. **Triangulating** a sighting is this cross-check-and-combine process, and it only works if the outposts' own distances from each other are geometrically consistent in the first place — a mapping error between any two outposts would undermine the whole method (SEE APPENDIX D). Two of the outposts also have a proposed **radio-relay** route between them: handheld radios only reach so far, or can be blocked by hills and thick bush, so a relay tower positioned between two outposts simply repeats the signal, letting them communicate even when they could not hear each other directly — surveyed along the reserve's internal grid of patrol roads (its own local map of numbered access tracks, not the public road network).

The vocabulary and the "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in **APPENDIX F** — that is where Challenge 1 sends you. Every sourced figure and stated assumption in this case is documented in **APPENDIX E** and **APPENDIX G**.

Derek radios the ops tent that he's five minutes out, and starts running the numbers on both maps one more time before he has to give his answer.

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|

| Real patrol-team density standard (one patrol team per this many km² of sector) | 14 [S2] |

| Total ranger workforce at the real conservancy this reserve is modeled on (context only) | 150+ [S4] |

| # | Assumption | Value used | Basis | Sensitivity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MA1 | The Perimeter Loop's standard sector side lengths | 18 km, 11 km, 9 km | No real reserve publishes the exact vertex coordinates of a boundary patrol sector. These lengths are a stated scenario shape, chosen to be a valid triangle (satisfying the Triangle Inequality Theorem) at a scale consistent with a boundary-hugging sector on a 360 km² reserve (Appendix A). | The case's answer is sensitive to this value — specifically to the sector's longest side, since it sets both the sector's area and its worst-case response distance. | MA2 | Number of Perimeter Loop sectors ringing the reserve | 6 | Chosen so that six sectors at MA1's scale form a plausible boundary ring around a 360 km² reserve without claiming more ground than the reserve has (Appendix A) or over-covering the interior. | Changes the Perimeter Loop's *total* coverage figure directly, but does not change the standard-sector ratio used for the primary metric. |
| MA3 | The Interior Web's standard sector side length | 5.7 km (all three sides equal) | No real reserve publishes exact interior patrol-triangle dimensions either. | MA4 | Number of Interior Web sectors radiating from the three outposts, and how they split across the outposts | 8 total (3 at Outpost 1, 3 at Outpost 2, 2 at Outpost 3) | Chosen so that eight sectors at MA3's scale leave real, un-patrolled gaps in the reserve's interior between the three outposts — a genuine trade-off, not an oversight, and consistent with the Interior Web design covering less total ground than the Perimeter Loop.
