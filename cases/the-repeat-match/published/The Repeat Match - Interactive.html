Overview

Yuki Alvarez has the CAD file open on two monitors, and neither version agrees with the other. On the left screen sits the master chevron motif — the client calls it "the arrow," and it does point one way, printed in three shades of blue across a brushed softshell that has real nap, the raised fiber running downhill toward the hem. On the right screen sits the same motif, twice: once rotated ninety degrees and then mirrored, once mirrored and then rotated the same ninety degrees. Same starting shape. Same two moves. Two different files.

She drags the little green crosshair — the registration point, the exact pixel the rotary screen locks the print to the cut line by — and watches it land in two different places depending on which order she built the transformation in. One version puts that crosshair exactly where the center-front zipper seam's chevron-match line actually sits. The other puts it somewhere the seam will never touch.

Down the hall, the engraving room is waiting on her final file before they cut screens that can't be uncut. Two doors past that, the cutting-room foreman has a marker layout pulled up on his own screen — one version lets pieces flip freely across the fabric to save yardage, the other locks every piece facing the same way so the nap never runs backward on a finished sleeve. He's asked her twice already this morning which layout to load.

Yuki minimizes the CAD window, opens the layout-comparison spreadsheet her ops lead sent over, and lines both decisions up side by side on her screen.

Background

Yuki Alvarez is the print-repeat engineer at a mid-size garment-print converter — a company that prints and finishes greige (undyed) fabric to order for apparel brands — and she owns the CAD file that a rotary-screen print run and the downstream cutting marker both depend on. Once the engraving room cuts screens from her file, the motif's position on the fabric is fixed for the entire yardage run; once the cutting room cuts fabric from a marker layout, that fabric cannot be uncut. Both decisions are hers to make and hers to defend.

The current job is a directional chevron border print for a softshell jacket line, printed on a brushed ("napped") softshell face fabric whose raised fiber surface runs in one direction. The chevron motif itself is not symmetric under reflection — it points one way, like an arrow — which means every mirrored copy of it is a genuinely different-looking image from the original, not an identical twin (SEE APPENDIX A). The jacket's front is two mirror-image panels that must meet at a center-front zipper seam with the chevron matching exactly; a separate back-yoke seam mirrors the same family of motif across a horizontal seam instead of a vertical one (SEE APPENDIX A). To build the mirrored panel's CAD artwork from the master motif, Yuki applies a rotation (to align the motif to the marker's cutting angle) and a reflection (to produce the mirror image) — and the order she applies them in is not interchangeable, because rotation and reflection do not commute in general (SEE APPENDIX A).

That geometric fact collides with a second, independent constraint. Reflecting the motif to build a mirrored panel also reverses which way the fabric's nap runs on that panel — a defect the trade's own napped-fabric guidance warns against by name (SEE APPENDIX D). The only way to guarantee every panel's nap runs the same direction is to forbid reflection across the whole marker and cut every piece facing the same way, which costs real, sourced extra yardage over a layout that is free to flip pieces (SEE APPENDICES D and E). The print house's rotary screen printer can also adjust its repeat length within a documented range, which matters once this same motif gets reused on a second printer that has a different adjustable range of its own — resizing the motif there means dilating it by a scale factor, not just moving it into place (SEE APPENDIX B). A separate, real patent on marker-alignment buffers for pattern-matched fabrics shows this is not a novelty problem unique to Yuki's shop — the same registration challenge has a documented industrial history (SEE APPENDIX C).

Yuki's recommendation must be defended on one number: total fabric cost for this production run under whichever marker layout policy she recommends (SEE APPENDICES D, E and F). Every sourced and assumed figure in this case is listed in the appendices and the Modeling Assumptions section — full source citations for every sourced figure are in APPENDIX H — and the vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in APPENDIX G — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

Appendix C — US Patent 5,333,111, Marker-Alignment Buffer Language

US Patent 5,333,111, "Garment cutting system having computer assisted pattern alignment," describes why plaid- and stripe-matching fabrics historically required a skilled worker to align patterns by hand, and states that a marker must leave a buffer around any garment segment that requires matching "often as much as half a fabric design repeat."

Appendix D — Sewing & Craft Alliance Guideline 4.120, Napped Fabrics

The Sewing & Craft Alliance's own guideline on napped/directional fabrics states that pattern pieces must be laid out so the nap runs the same direction on every piece, or the finished garment shows visibly uneven color shading, and that this same-direction requirement costs extra fabric.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Front panel: does rotate-then-reflect equal reflect-then-rotate? (D1)

    Front registration point P = (4, 6) in [MA1]. Rotate 90° counterclockwise about the origin [MA3], and reflect across the vertical front seam axis [MA2]. Compute the result BOTH ways — rotate-then-reflect, and reflect-then-rotate — and enter your two coordinate pairs.

    Step 2 — Back-yoke panel: same question, horizontal seam (D2)

    Back-yoke registration point P = (5, 3) in [MA4], back-yoke seam axis horizontal [MA5]. Using the same 90° cutting-angle rotation [MA3] but reflecting across the HORIZONTAL back-yoke seam axis instead of the front's vertical one, compute both composition orders.

    Step 3 — Dilation scale factor for the second printer (D5)

    The master motif's native repeat is 30 in [MA7]. Pick a target repeat length inside the second printer's range (20-30 in [MA8]) and compute the dilation scale factor needed to resize the motif to fit it: scale factor = target repeat ÷ native repeat.

    Step 4 — Marker-layout fabric cost comparison (D6)

    Total production-run fabric cost = base yardage/unit × fabric cost/yd × run size. Same-Grain adds a with-nap yardage premium (Appendix D's sourced 15-25% range) on top of that same base. Choose a premium value inside the sourced range and compute BOTH options' totals yourself.

    Reveal the Twist

    New information just came in from the cutting-room foreman. He ran a test cut of the Mirror-Match layout on the older of the shop's two markers this morning — and on that older marker, the cutting-angle rotation is not quite what Yuki's CAD file assumed. The registration point that was supposed to land on the seam's match line landed noticeably off it instead.

    That is the case's own geometric point turned into a live moment: the composition order that is correct for one cutting-angle assumption is not automatically correct for a different one.
