Overview

Renee Okafor has the observatory's control-room monitor split into two windows, and she still hasn't clicked either one. On the left: a live countdown to moonriseThe moment the Moon rises above the horizon. Scattered moonlight brightens the sky enough to wash out the tiny brightness dips this program measures, ending the usable observing window., the moment tonight's faint-target brightness measurements stop being usable at all. On the right: the queue console, blinking, waiting for her to submit one target assignment before the allocation committee's nightly sign-off (their final go-ahead, needed before the telescope will run either option) closes.

"Four hours," the on-call operator says from the doorway, not really a question. "Which target?"

Renee doesn't decide how many transit candidatesA star whose brightness dipped in a pattern consistent with a planet passing in front of it — flagged for follow-up, but not yet confirmed as a real planet. the survey program has flagged this month, or how bright the moon comes up. She decides what tonight's one open telescope slot actually points at. Right now there's a folder on her desk — printed, because she reads these on paper — for a single transit candidate the pipeline flagged weeks ago, with a predicted transit window that lines up almost perfectly with tonight. Point the telescope there for the full four hours, watch it happen, and either confirm it or don't.

Next to that folder is a second tab open on her laptop: a target list of twelve unexplored fields, each one a quick look for a brand-new signal nobody has ever flagged. Twelve short looks instead of one long stare. Faster science, if any of them hit. Nothing at all, if none of them do.

The operator is still standing there. The countdown clock ticks down another minute.

Renee pulls both windows to the front of her screen and does not press submit on either one.

Background

Renee Okafor is the telescope-time allocation scheduler for a university robotic observatoryA telescope that runs its nightly observing schedule under automated software control, rather than a human physically aiming it — the on-call operator still monitors and can intervene, but does not manually point the telescope.'s exoplanetA planet that orbits a star other than our Sun. program. Every clear night, she is personally responsible for deciding what the observatory's single telescope points at during its open science slots, and for defending that recommendation to the program's allocation committee before the night's assignment locks in. Tonight's slot is short — the Moon rises bright enough, partway through the night, to wash out the faint brightness dips this program is built to measure, which caps tonight's usable window at a fixed length regardless of which target Renee picks (see Appendix B).

Two options are on the table. The Candidate Watch commits the full slot to re-observing one already-flagged transit candidate — a star whose brightness-over-time record (a light curveA plot of how bright a star measures at each moment in time — the tool astronomers read a transit's dip off of.) has already shown one promising dip; because a transiting planet orbits on a fixed, repeating cycle, the timing of its next dip (its next predicted transit window) can be calculated in advance, and tonight's slot is timed to catch it. A transit candidate like this is vetted against a real, published pattern: large all-sky surveys flag many such candidates, and a well-documented fraction of them turn out to be false positivesA signal that looked like a real transiting planet at first, but turned out to have a non-planetary cause (a background eclipsing binary star — two stars orbiting each other, periodically blocking each other's light in a way that can look from far away like a planet crossing its own star — an instrument artifact (a glitch in the camera or electronics that mimics a real brightness dip), or a data-processing error) once more data came in. — rather than a genuine transiting planet (see Appendix A, Appendix C). The Twelve-Field Sweep instead splits the same slot across twelve short looks at fields the survey has never examined, each one an independent search for a new, as-yet-undetected transit signal (see Appendix C).

Either option clears the whole slot; neither can be extended once the Moon comes up — no telescope pointing time exists for anything past that boundary, for either option. Appendix C's per-field Sweep rate already represents a genuinely confirmed new detection — the same "survives the false-positive check" standard applied to The Candidate Watch — not merely a raw, unvetted flag; both options' probabilities answer the same question (did tonight's slot produce a real, confirmed signal), which is exactly how "a confirmed detection" is counted for D1/D3 (see Appendix F, MA6). What Appendix D governs works from a different, earlier stage of the SAME twelve fields' data, not a separate set of fields: (1) each 20-minute look finishes and an onboard automated test (software running on the telescope's own computer, checking in real time whether a star's brightness dipped more than random noise would explain) either flags the field or doesn't, a broader and more generous first pass than Appendix C's stricter confirmation standard; (2) after the 4-hour slot ends, whichever flags came in tonight go through the same false-positive check Appendix C describes; (3) separately, once the morning report deadline approaches, the program's small on-call analysis team can only fully write up and cross-check a limited number of tonight's flags before the deadline — this write-up step happens after the telescope slot is already over and uses no telescope time at all. Because the automated flag is deliberately generous, like Appendix A's real TESS pipeline, a typical night produces more flags than confirmed detections (see Appendix D). The real NASA Exoplanet Archive publishes transit-depth measurements for confirmed planets in a compact scientific-notation form that the program's own report template requires converting to plain decimal notation before it goes in front of the committee (see Appendix E).

Every figure in this case is either cited to a real published source or disclosed as a stated Modeling Assumption (see Appendix F, Appendix G). The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in Appendix H — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

Appendices

Appendix A — Transit Candidates and Vetting: The Real Numbers

A transit candidate is a star whose brightness dipped in a pattern consistent with a planet passing in front of it. Large sky surveys flag thousands of these, but not every flagged candidate is a real planet. NASA's TESS (Transiting Exoplanet Survey SatelliteA real NASA space telescope that monitors hundreds of thousands of stars, region by region, looking for the periodic brightness dips caused by a transiting planet.) mission publishes its full list of flagged candidates — called TESS Objects of Interest (TOIs)A candidate signal TESS's pipeline flags as possibly being a transiting planet, before follow-up observation confirms or rules it out. — and tracks which ones follow-up observation later re-classifies as false positives: a background eclipsing binary star, an instrument artifact (a glitch in the camera or electronics mimicking a real dip), or a data-processing error that only looked like a planet's dip [S1].

Outcome, TESS Prime Mission TOI catalogCountShare
Total TOIs in the catalog2,241100%
Re-classified as false positives after follow-up565≈25.2%
Remaining candidates (planet candidates or confirmed planets)1,676≈74.8%

This case does not need the specific reasons a candidate becomes a false positive — they are included here because the vetting system is real, and because "not every flagged dip is a real planet" is exactly why a second, confirming look matters.

Appendix E — Real Transit-Depth Data (NASA Exoplanet Archive)

The NASA Exoplanet Archive publishes transit depth — how much a star's brightness drops during a transit, as a fraction of its total light — for confirmed planets. Real papers on these planets often report this fraction in scientific notation.

PlanetTransit depth (scientific notation, fraction of stellar brightness)Source
TOI-700 d6.13 × 10−4Spitzer Space Telescope (a retired NASA infrared space telescope) confirmation paper [S3]
Kepler-186f4.41 × 10−4Derived from published planet-to-star radius ratio (how much smaller the planet is than its star; squaring that ratio gives the fraction of starlight it blocks) [S4]

[S5] confirms the NASA Exoplanet Archive's own Transiting Planets Table reports depth as a percentage. This case's report template requires standard (decimal) notation instead, because the committee's spreadsheet tool does not accept scientific notation as input.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 (D1) — Compound probability for The Twelve-Field Sweep

    Using Appendix C's per-field rate and 12 independent fields, find the probability that at least one field yields a confirmed new detection tonight. Use the complement rule — do not just multiply.

    Step 2 (D2) — Single-event probability for The Candidate Watch

    State the probability, from Appendix A/C, that tonight's re-observation confirms a real detection.

    Step 3 (D3) — Expected detections per hour, and the margin

    Using Step 1, Step 2, and the 4-hour slot, find each option's rate (detections per hour) and the percent margin between them.

    Step 4 (D4) — Write-up combinations and permutations

    Using Appendix D's preliminary-flag count and same-night write-up capacity, find how many possible subsets could be chosen to write up (a combination), and how many possible orders a chosen subset of that size could be written up in (a permutation).

    Step 5 (D5) — Standard notation

    Convert Appendix E's two real transit-depth figures from scientific notation to standard (decimal) notation.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    "We're partway into the slot. The pipeline sends an alert: an archival cross-match (comparing tonight's image against older sky-survey images of the same patch of sky) just flagged a faint background star sitting almost on top of the candidate's target star — so close in the sky that their light blends together in the telescope's image, precisely the kind of blended background object that has turned real-looking dips into false positives before (see Appendix A). The confirmation odds just dropped."
