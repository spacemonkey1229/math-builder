Case Study: The Custom Die
MS | Math | Case Study | 2026-27 | Heroes Academy

## Overview

Marcus Feld has forty minutes before the die shop's quote expires.

He's standing at the cutting table in the sample room with two mockup boxes side by side, both
holding the same triangular pastry slice the internet has decided it can't stop watching someone
eat. The plain one is a plain box — square corners, flaps that fold shut in ten seconds, and a
wedge of dead air above the slice that Marcus can feel every time he lifts the lid. The other box
is nothing but flat cardboard right now: a die-cut sheet on the table next to it, cut and scored
into a shape that folds up snug around the slice's actual triangular cross-section, corner to
corner, no gap.

Six months ago this bakery shipped forty boxes a week and Marcus taped them shut himself. Then one
video crossed a million views and the order queue hasn't stopped since. The fulfillment center's
invoice is sitting on the desk with a line item circled in yellow marker — they bill by the cubic
inch now, not by the box — and packaging has quietly become the second-biggest cost on the sheet,
right behind flour and butter.

His phone buzzes face-down on the cutting table. The die shop, confirming: whichever shape he
approves tonight is the one they start cutting steel for tomorrow morning, and there's no second
quote before the next production run has to ship.

Marcus picks up the plain box, then the flat die-cut sheet, turning each one over in his hands.

## Background

Marcus Feld is the packaging engineer at a bakery that grew, almost overnight, from a handful of
local weekly orders to a shipping operation moving thousands of boxes a week to addresses it had
never shipped to before. He is the person responsible for choosing what every product ships in —
which means every box he approves has to survive a conveyor belt (the automated belts and chutes a
warehouse uses to sort and move packages — real equipment that jostles, drops, and squeezes boxes
from every side, not a gentle ride), hold its shape in transit, and cost the company as little as
possible per order, because at this new volume a few cents saved or lost on a single box repeats
itself thousands of times a week.

The product driving this decision is a triangular pastry slice the bakery calls **The Wedge** — a
baked good with a right-triangular cross-section, not a rectangular one, that the fulfillment
center's (a company Marcus's bakery pays to warehouse, pack, and ship its orders instead of doing
it in-house) new billing policy makes expensive to ship carelessly: the invoice is no longer priced
per box, it is priced by the cubic inch the box actually occupies on the truck — a practice
sometimes called dimensional-weight billing, charging by the space a package takes up rather than
its physical weight (SEE APPENDIX G) — whether or not that space holds any pastry (SEE APPENDIX B
for The Wedge's exact dimensions).

Before settling on a box shape, Marcus's packaging team spent an afternoon at the whiteboard
sketching every container shape they could think of for The Wedge, from shapes borrowed off other
suppliers' catalogs to shapes somebody just liked the look of (SEE APPENDIX A). Most of those
sketches don't survive five minutes of scrutiny once you actually look at how each shape is built
— but the exercise narrowed the real field down to exactly two serious candidates, and those are
the two Marcus has mockups of on the table tonight.

**Option A — The Standard Box** is a simple rectangular prism, sized to the outside dimensions of
The Wedge with a small clearance (packaging term for the deliberate small gap left between a
product and the inside of its box, so the product slides in and out without the box being cut so
tight it jams or tears) so the slice slides in and out cleanly (SEE APPENDIX C). Every face is a
flat rectangle, every fold is a straight line, and the die (a custom-built, hardened-steel cutting
and creasing tool — see below) that cuts its **net** (a 3-D shape's flat, unfolded pattern: every
face laid out flat and connected, in the exact layout you would cut from one sheet before folding
it up into the finished box) out of a sheet of cardboard is about as simple as die-cutting gets —
cheap to make, fast to approve. The tradeoff is built into the shape itself: a triangular slice
does not fill a rectangular box, so every Standard Box ships with a wedge of empty air above the
pastry, air the fulfillment center bills for exactly as if it were more pastry.

**Option B — The Fitted Wedge** is a triangular-prism box, its interior cross-section built to
match The Wedge's own triangular shape with the same small clearance (SEE APPENDIX C). It wastes
almost none of the space it ships. But its net is not six simple rectangles — it is two triangles
and three rectangles of different widths, meeting at angles a straight-rule die does not cut as
cheaply, and Marcus's die-shop (the outside vendor that owns the steel-cutting equipment and
builds a custom die — the physical steel tool that presses through a flat cardboard sheet in one
stroke to cut and score it into a box's exact net — to a customer's specifications) quote reflects
that (SEE APPENDIX D and APPENDIX E).

Both options use the same single-wall corrugated cardboard stock (cardboard built from one wavy
layer of paper, called the medium, glued between two flat liner sheets — plain, smooth sheets of
paper facing outward on either side, giving the board its flat outer surfaces — "single-wall" means
just one such wavy layer between two liners, the lightest common corrugated construction), priced
by the square inch (SEE
APPENDIX D), so material cost is driven entirely by how much flat cardboard each box's *net*
actually uses — not by how much air or pastry sits inside the folded box once it's assembled. The
one-time cost of cutting steel for either die — a cost sometimes called **tooling** (the setup
investment a factory or die shop makes in a custom tool before it can produce a single unit) — only
gets paid once, then spreads (this spreading is called **amortizing** a cost) across every box the
bakery orders from that die before the next **re-tooling** decision — the next point where the team
could choose to invest in cutting a different die — (SEE APPENDIX E). All figures in this case are
sourced in APPENDIX F, or stated as an explicit modeling assumption in APPENDIX E where no public
figure exists for a decision this specific to one company. The vocabulary and the "how to…" skills
this case will ask of you are listed in APPENDIX H — that is where Challenge 1 sends you. (SEE
APPENDIX G for further reading on packaging geometry and die-cutting.)

## Dilemma

You are Marcus Feld.
Do you:
A.) Approve The Standard Box — a simple rectangular-prism box, cheap die, wasted space around every
slice OR
B.) Approve The Fitted Wedge — a triangular-prism box fitted to the slice's real shape, pricier die,
almost no wasted space

## Appendix A — Packaging Team Whiteboard Log

Six shapes came off the whiteboard before the team narrowed the field to Options A and B. Nobody
labeled these with their formal names on the whiteboard — just descriptions. Naming them correctly
is part of D1.

| Candidate | Description as sketched |
|---|---|
| Candidate 1 | One curved lateral surface running between two flat, circular, congruent, parallel ends. No edges where the curved surface meets flat surface at either flat face's rim, and no vertices anywhere on the shape. |
| Candidate 2 | One flat circular base and one continuous curved surface that tapers up from the base's edge to a single point directly above the base's center. One edge (the base's circular rim), one vertex (the point at the top). |
| Candidate 3 | Six flat rectangular faces. Every pair of opposite faces is congruent and parallel to each other. Twelve edges, eight vertices. |
| Candidate 4 | One flat square base and four flat triangular faces, each one sharing an edge with the base and meeting all the others at a single point directly above the base. Eight edges, five vertices. |
| Candidate 5 | A single continuous curved surface with no flat faces anywhere, no edges, and no vertices. |
| Candidate 6 | Two flat triangular faces, congruent and parallel to each other, connected by three flat rectangular faces. Nine edges, six vertices. |

The team rejected four of these within minutes once someone actually checked how each shape's
interior space lines up against a triangular cross-section — but the case does not tell you which
four, or why. That's D1's job.

## Appendix D — Material Cost Reference

| | Value |
|---|---|
| Corrugated stock | Single-wall, 200 lb test, white/kraft (a common corrugated finish: one side coated smooth white for printing, the other left in its natural brown "kraft" paper — a cosmetic finish, not something that affects any calculation in this case), 5/32 in thick (the board's total thickness once glued together — a rating of the sheet itself, not a dimension you compute with) |
| Sheet size priced | 24 in × 36 in (864 sq in) |
| Price per individual sheet | $2.37 [S1] |
| Price per square inch | $0.0027431 (derived: $2.37 ÷ 864 sq in) |

"200 lb test" is the industry rating for how much force the flat sheet resists before it punctures
— a measure of the stock's strength, not a physical shipping weight. **$2.37 is the price of one
24 in × 36 in sheet at the supplier's 50-sheet bundle tier**, not the price of the whole bundle;
the supplier only sells this sheet in bundles of 50 or more, so a 50-sheet bundle costs
$2.37 × 50 = $118.50, but every calculation in this case (and the derived per-square-inch price
above) uses the $2.37 single-sheet price.

This is the same raw material both options are cut from. The die shape changes how much of the
sheet ends up as box versus scrap, but not the price of the sheet itself.

## Appendix E — Modeling Assumptions

> These figures are **not sourced** to a single public price, because a die-shop quote for one
> specific company's one specific box is not a published figure anywhere. They are stated scenario
> assumptions chosen by the case author, each with a real basis named below. A Hero may challenge
> them in discussion — that is a legitimate move, and Appendix H names the skills a Hero would need
> to test one.

In the Sensitivity column below, "the margin" means how far apart Option A's and Option B's final
D5 scores end up, as a percent — the same "how big was the gap between the winner and the loser"
idea D5 already asks you to state. "The margin is sensitive to this value" means: change this one
assumption and that percent gap changes noticeably, possibly enough to flip who wins.

| # | Assumption | Value used | Basis | Sensitivity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MA1 | One-time steel-rule (a hardened steel blade, bent to the exact shape of every cut and crease line the box's net needs, then mounted in a wooden or steel base) die cost, Option A (simple rectangular net — six straight-line panels, all 90-degree folds) | **$450** | Industry pricing pages consistently describe die cost as scaling with total **rule footage** (the total length of that steel blade a die uses — more footage generally means a more complex, more expensive die) and design complexity, but published dollar figures for specific dies vary widely across sources with no single primary methodology behind them (a straight-line structural die was found quoted as low as the low hundreds by some suppliers, while more decorated or multi-piece dies run into the thousands). This case fixes a representative value for the simplest possible die style — one continuous run of straight cutting and creasing rule, no angled cuts. **No published dollar-per-foot rate exists to compute this from rule footage directly, so $450 and $1,450 (MA2) are fixed scenario values chosen by the case, not figures you derive from a formula.** | The margin is sensitive to this value — see D5. |
| MA2 | One-time steel-rule die cost, Option B (triangular-prism net — two triangular panels plus three angled rectangular panels) | **$1,450** | Same sourcing limitation as MA1. This case fixes a value roughly 3.2x MA1's, reflecting the additional rule footage from the angled triangular panels and the extra setup a non-90-degree fold pattern requires, consistent with the qualitative pattern (more cuts, more angles, more cost) found across every industry source checked, even though no source gave a defensible exact multiplier. | The margin is sensitive to this value — see D5. |
| MA3 | Expected order volume for The Wedge before the next re-tooling decision point | **12,000 units** | Tied directly to the bakery's own current nationwide order volume (SEE Background) — the packaging team's working forecast is that The Wedge will account for roughly one week's worth of the bakery's present order volume before the next production run reaches the point where a die change could even be considered again (the die shop needs six weeks of lead time — advance notice before delivering a finished die — measured against an eight-week production window, the stretch of time this production run is expected to take). **This case defines the re-tooling horizon as whatever order volume Marcus's team can actually count on with confidence at the moment of the decision** — for a brand-new, unproven product with no purchase commitment from any buyer, that confidently-known volume is only the near-term production window described above. If a buyer instead signs a purchase order (a buyer's own written, binding commitment to buy a specific quantity — stronger than a forecast, because the buyer is contractually obligated to it) that guarantees a specific volume over a stated contract term (how long that written agreement runs — for example, one year), the confidently-known volume becomes the full guaranteed volume over that contract's own term — not a smaller number obtained by squeezing that guarantee back down into the original 8-week window, since the whole reason the original window was short was the absence of any such guarantee. This is a business forecast, not a public fact, and a Hero may reasonably argue for a different number. | The margin is sensitive to this value — see D5. |
