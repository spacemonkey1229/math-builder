Overview

Marcus Feld has forty minutes before the die shop's quote expires.

He's standing at the cutting table in the sample room with two mockup boxes side by side, both holding the same triangular pastry slice the internet has decided it can't stop watching someone eat. The plain one is a plain box — square corners, flaps that fold shut in ten seconds, and a wedge of dead air above the slice that Marcus can feel every time he lifts the lid. The other box is nothing but flat cardboard right now: a die-cut sheet on the table next to it, cut and scored into a shape that folds up snug around the slice's actual triangular cross-section, corner to corner, no gap.

Six months ago this bakery shipped forty boxes a week and Marcus taped them shut himself. Then one video crossed a million views and the order queue hasn't stopped since. The fulfillment center's invoice is sitting on the desk with a line item circled in yellow marker — they bill by the cubic inch now, not by the box — and packaging has quietly become the second-biggest cost on the sheet, right behind flour and butter.

His phone buzzes face-down on the cutting table. The die shop, confirming: whichever shape he approves tonight is the one they start cutting steel for tomorrow morning, and there's no second quote before the next production run has to ship.

Marcus picks up the plain box, then the flat die-cut sheet, turning each one over in his hands.

Background

Marcus Feld is the packaging engineer at a bakery that grew, almost overnight, from a handful of local weekly orders to a shipping operation moving thousands of boxes a week to addresses it had never shipped to before. He is the person responsible for choosing what every product ships in — which means every box he approves has to survive a conveyor belt, hold its shape in transit, and cost the company as little as possible per order, because at this new volume a few cents saved or lost on a single box repeats itself thousands of times a week.

The product driving this decision is a triangular pastry slice the bakery calls The Wedge — a baked good with a right-triangular cross-section, not a rectangular one, that the fulfillment center's new billing policy makes expensive to ship carelessly: the invoice is no longer priced per box, it is priced by the cubic inch the box actually occupies on the truck — a practice sometimes called dimensional-weight billing — whether or not that space holds any pastry (SEE APPENDIX B for The Wedge's exact dimensions).

Before settling on a box shape, Marcus's packaging team spent an afternoon at the whiteboard sketching every container shape they could think of for The Wedge, from shapes borrowed off other suppliers' catalogs to shapes somebody just liked the look of (SEE APPENDIX A). Most of those sketches don't survive five minutes of scrutiny once you actually look at how each shape is built — but the exercise narrowed the real field down to exactly two serious candidates, and those are the two Marcus has mockups of on the table tonight.

Option A — The Standard Box is a simple rectangular prism, sized to the outside dimensions of The Wedge with a small clearance so the slice slides in and out cleanly (SEE APPENDIX C). Every face is a flat rectangle, every fold is a straight line, and the die that cuts its net out of a sheet of cardboard is about as simple as die-cutting gets — cheap to make, fast to approve. The tradeoff is built into the shape itself: a triangular slice does not fill a rectangular box, so every Standard Box ships with a wedge of empty air above the pastry, air the fulfillment center bills for exactly as if it were more pastry.

Option B — The Fitted Wedge is a triangular-prism box, its interior cross-section built to match The Wedge's own triangular shape with the same small clearance (SEE APPENDIX C). It wastes almost none of the space it ships. But its net is not six simple rectangles — it is two triangles and three rectangles of different widths, meeting at angles a straight-rule die does not cut as cheaply, and Marcus's die shop's quote reflects that (SEE APPENDIX D and APPENDIX E).

Both options use the same single-wall corrugated cardboard stock, priced by the square inch (SEE APPENDIX D), so material cost is driven entirely by how much flat cardboard each box's net actually uses — not by how much air or pastry sits inside the folded box once it's assembled. The one-time cost of cutting steel for either die — a cost sometimes called tooling — only gets paid once, then spreads (this spreading is called amortizing a cost) across every box the bakery orders from that die before the next re-tooling decision (SEE APPENDIX E). All figures in this case are sourced in APPENDIX F, or stated as an explicit modeling assumption in APPENDIX E where no public figure exists for a decision this specific to one company. The vocabulary and the "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in APPENDIX H — that is where Challenge 1 sends you. (SEE APPENDIX G for further reading on packaging geometry and die-cutting.)

Appendix A — Packaging Team Whiteboard Log

Six shapes came off the whiteboard before the team narrowed the field to Options A and B. Nobody labeled these with their formal names on the whiteboard — just descriptions. Naming them correctly is part of D1.

CandidateDescription as sketched
Candidate 1One curved lateral surface running between two flat, circular, congruent, parallel ends. No edges where the curved surface meets flat surface at either flat face's rim, and no vertices anywhere on the shape.
Candidate 2One flat circular base and one continuous curved surface that tapers up from the base's edge to a single point directly above the base's center. One edge (the base's circular rim), one vertex (the point at the top).
Candidate 3Six flat rectangular faces. Every pair of opposite faces is congruent and parallel to each other. Twelve edges, eight vertices.
Candidate 4One flat square base and four flat triangular faces, each one sharing an edge with the base and meeting all the others at a single point directly above the base. Eight edges, five vertices.
Candidate 5A single continuous curved surface with no flat faces anywhere, no edges, and no vertices.
Candidate 6Two flat triangular faces, congruent and parallel to each other, connected by three flat rectangular faces. Nine edges, six vertices.

The team rejected four of these within minutes once someone actually checked how each shape's interior space lines up against a triangular cross-section — but the case does not tell you which four, or why. That's D1's job.

Appendix D — Material Cost Reference
Value
Corrugated stockSingle-wall, 200 lb test, white/kraft, 5/32 in thick
Sheet size priced24 in × 36 in (864 sq in)
Price per individual sheet$2.37 [S1]
Price per square inch$0.0027431 (derived: $2.37 ÷ 864 sq in)

"200 lb test" is the industry rating for how much force the flat sheet resists before it punctures — a measure of the stock's strength, not a physical shipping weight. $2.37 is the price of one 24 in × 36 in sheet at the supplier's 50-sheet bundle tier, not the price of the whole bundle; the supplier only sells this sheet in bundles of 50 or more, so a 50-sheet bundle costs $2.37 × 50 = $118.50, but every calculation in this case (and the derived per-square-inch price above) uses the $2.37 single-sheet price. This is the same raw material both options are cut from — the die shape changes how much of the sheet ends up as box versus scrap, but not the price of the sheet itself.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Dimensioned Net (D2)

    Option B's cross-section is a right triangle with the same two legs as Option A's width and depth. Compute the hypotenuse of that cross-section (with clearance already added), for the net you draw in D2.

    Step 2 — Net-Area Material Table (D3)

    Using your labeled nets from D2 (not either box's volume), compute the total square inches of cardboard each option's net actually uses.

    Step 3 — Wasted-Space Table (D4)

    Compute the volume of The Wedge itself and the interior volume of each box option, then find how many cubic inches (and what percent) of each box's interior is wasted space.

    Step 4 — Tooling-Amortized Cost Comparison (D5)

    Combine your D3 net area with each option's one-time die cost (Appendix E), spread across the expected 12,000-unit order volume (Appendix E, MA3), then express each option's total as square inches of cardboard per cubic inch of Wedge actually shipped.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    Marcus's manager comes back from the retail buyer's office and drops a signed contract on the table. "It's not a trial order. They want forty thousand units a year, guaranteed."

    "And it changes something else, too," the manager adds. "A trial order only ever bought us until the next production run — that's why we only ever counted twelve thousand units. A signed annual contract is a different animal: they're not going to make us re-open the tooling conversation every eight weeks anymore. The next real re-tooling decision isn't the next production run. It's the next time this contract comes up for renewal — a full year out."

    Does that change what Marcus should approve tonight?
