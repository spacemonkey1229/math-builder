## Overview

Chalk dust hangs in the air beneath the towering speed walls at The Summit Climbing Center, where a room full of nervous laughter and stretching bodies waits for Coach Carlos Chen to post two lists on the whiteboard. Every girl in the gym has given up birthdays, weekends, and summer mornings for this. Every one of them believes she's earned a spot.

Ava tightens her climbing shoes without looking up. As co-captain, she's been to Nationals before, and she wants one more run before she ages out of the program for good. Across the room, Gertrude closes her eyes and breathes, the way she does before every climb, competition or not. Rebecca paces near the mats, certain she's one of the fastest climbers in the building even though she's never once competed. In the corner, Kylie stretches alone, trying to keep her mind on the wall and off the empty side of a bed her dad doesn't sleep in anymore.

Coach Chen watches all of it — the chalk, the nerves, the quiet competitiveness — and thinks about the three envelopes sitting closed on his desk in the back office. He hasn't opened them yet. He already knows, roughly, what each one is going to say, and he knows that whichever way he leans, somebody in this room is going to hear a name that isn't hers tomorrow morning.

He picks up his clipboard and heads for the office.

## Background

Four years ago, Carlos Chen retired from international speed climbing competition after winning a World Championship, and opened The Summit Climbing Center in St. George with a simple stated mission: *develop champions, not just champion climbers.* Today The Summit's U18 girls team is one of the most competitive youth speed-climbing programs in the western United States (SEE APPENDIX E for real-world context on the sport itself).

A dozen athletes, ages 11 to 17, have spent the past year training for the U18 National Championships. The Summit will send two national teams — each made up of three competing climbers and one alternate (SEE APPENDIX D for the exact rules officials use to score a team, and the rule The Summit uses to assign climbers to a team). Coach Chen has to fill every one of those roster spots from the twelve athletes in the gym today; everyone he doesn't select stays home. In front of him: every climber's timed results from today's final selection trial, her practice and competition history, and a full year of his own coaching notes on who she is as a teammate (SEE APPENDICES A, B, and C).

The afternoon before he has to post the roster, Coach Ivy hands him three letters. The gym's owner wants the team built to win — a national title would grow The Summit's membership and reputation, and "our athletes deserve every opportunity." Team co-captains Ava and Kylie want it built to win too — they see Nationals as a stage that should reward performance, and they're asking Coach Chen to pick "the athletes who give us the best chance." A group of parents wants something else: they're asking him to weigh leadership, growth, and teamwork alongside raw speed, "even if it costs some raw speed" — trusting him to build not just a winning team, but a lasting program.

Both philosophies are real, and both already point to a specific, buildable roster. Selecting purely on speed and selecting for character overlap on most of the twelve names — but not all of them (SEE APPENDIX D). The climbers who land on the wrong side of that line are the ones who will notice tomorrow morning. Every figure in this case that isn't a direct citation of real-world speed climbing (SEE APPENDIX E) is either given scenario data or a disclosed, stated Modeling Assumption (SEE APPENDIX F) — none is invented quietly. The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in APPENDIX G — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

You are Carlos Chen.

Do you:

### Appendix D — Team Selection & Scoring Rules

These are the fixed rules of the competition and of The Summit's own internal process — not something to guess at.

**How Nationals officials score a team.** Each athlete climbs twice at Nationals; only her fastest of the two counts as her official Nationals time. A team's score is:

> **team score = team's mean climb time + team's median climb time**

computed across the **three competing members only** (the alternate's time doesn't count unless she replaces a teammate). **Lower score wins** — a team is not judged by its single fastest individual climber, but by this combined figure.

**Official selection time.** For the purposes of building your roster today, this case uses each climber's **Best Practice Climb Time** (Appendix B) — her most recent recorded monthly time — as her official time. (See Appendix F, MA1, for why this case uses "most recent" rather than "fastest-ever" and what that choice costs.)

**How The Summit splits eight climbers into two teams.** Once you've selected your 8 athletes (by whatever philosophy you're pitching), rank all 8 by official selection time, fastest to slowest. Assign them to teams by alternating seed: 1st fastest → Team 1, 2nd fastest → Team 2, 3rd fastest → Team 1, 4th fastest → Team 2, and so on through all 8. Within each 4-athlete team, the **slowest of that team's four** becomes the alternate; the other three are the team's competitors. This keeps both teams evenly seeded instead of stacking every fast climber onto one team — it is not a choice you get to make; it's the rule the gym uses every year.

### Appendix E — About Speed Climbing

Speed climbing is a real, Olympic sport with a wall and route standardized worldwide: the IFSC (International Federation of Sport Climbing) requires the official speed wall to be **15,000 mm (15 meters) tall**, continuously overhanging at a fixed **5° angle**, with identical holds at every sanctioned competition [S1]. Every athlete on an official wall, anywhere in the world, climbs the exact same route.

Because the route never changes, speed climbing times keep dropping as training and technique improve. As of this case's writing:

- The women's world record is **5.99 seconds**, set by American climber Emma Hunt on July 4, 2026, at the World Climbing Series stop in Kraków, Poland — the first time any woman has broken the six-second mark [S2].
- The men's world record is **4.58 seconds**, set by 16-year-old Zhao Yicheng of China on April 28, 2026, at the Asian Beach Games in Sanya [S3].

In the United States, USA Climbing runs a real Speed discipline — alongside Boulder and Lead/TR — at its annual Youth National Championships [S4]. The Summit's own "U18" team category is the gym's own name for its oldest girls' division, not an official USA Climbing division name.
