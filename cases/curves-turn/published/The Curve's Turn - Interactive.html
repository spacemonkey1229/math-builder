Overview

Dr. Renata Vance has the projector remote in one hand and her phone face-down in the other, and the school board is due in this room in a matter of minutes. On the screen behind her is a chart she has looked at so many times this week that she has stopped seeing it — a jagged line of weekly case counts climbing out of the autumn, dipping, climbing again. Someone from facilities has stacked the folding chairs against the wall, and the room smells like floor wax and cold coffee. Her deputy has printed two memos and left them squared on the table, one laid on top of the other, the way you leave a choice for somebody else to make.

The superintendent stops in the doorway with his coat already on. "They're going to ask you for a date," he says. "Not a range. A date." Then he is gone down the corridor, and Renata is alone with the hum of the projector fan.

She knows what the smooth curve drawn through those points says about where this is going. She also knows the line underneath it is real data — reported, revised, ragged — and that every epidemiologist she trained with has watched a curve exactly like this one flatten into nothing the week after everybody panicked. Down the hall a custodian is running a floor buffer. Renata turns the top memo over, turns it back, and reaches for the remote.

Background

Dr. Renata Vance is director of health services for Birchmont Unified School District. Under district policy she does not order a closure herself; she brings the board a single recommendation, and the board has never once voted against one of hers. She is accountable for what happens to instruction and for what happens to the people inside the buildings, and this week those two accountabilities pull in opposite directions.

Birchmont Unified is an invented district. The county it sits in is not, and neither is a single case count in this case: the weekly reported series in Appendix A and Appendix B is the actual published county series for the county named there, and the population denominator, the reference case-rate tiers and the test-performance figures are cited in Appendix E. Nothing about the decision is real — no health officer named Vance made this call and no board voted on it. Every figure that is not cited is stated openly in Appendix F.

Two plans are on the table, and one standing board rule constrains both. The board has authorised exactly one closure, of one fixed length, for the whole term — a closure budget, not a closure policy (Appendix C). Whatever Vance recommends, the district spends that budget once, or not at all. Both plans therefore cost the district the same instructional days if they fire; they differ only in when the budget is spent, and in what else runs alongside it.

The Early Brake closes every school in the district for two weeks starting immediately, on the strength of the curve's turn. The smooth model fitted to the county's current wave (Appendix B) has been concave up for weeks — the weekly case count is not merely rising, it is rising faster each week. That is the earliest defensible signal available, and it is a signal about a rate, not a level.
The Tripwire keeps every school open, runs twice-weekly on-site rapid antigen testing for consenting students and staff, and holds the closure budget in reserve until the reported case rate crosses a fixed level the board has already written down (Appendix C). A level is unambiguous to read off a table. It is also structurally late: a level can only be crossed after the curve has already turned.

Vance is judged on one number, and the board has written that down too: chain-weighted in-school transmissions per one thousand students across the board's stated evaluation horizon (Appendix D). The prevention model, the transmission coefficient and the chain multiplier that turn a county case rate into that number are all set out there.

In the real world, no single number tells every district when to close. The U.S. CDC's own 2020 framework for school operating-status decisions sets several risk-tiered ranges at once — for case rate and for test positivity — and states plainly that these ranges are guideposts, not fixed cutoffs, and that the actual call is made locally, by health officials and school administrators working with their own community, not handed down by CDC itself. Real thresholds also vary by jurisdiction and by how severe the disease in question is — a rule written for one county in one outbreak is not a rule for every county in every outbreak. The board's own closure threshold in Appendix C works the same way: it is this case's own stated scenario constraint for a fictional board (Appendix F, MA2), not a published real-world standard.

The shape behind both plans — cases rising, then rising faster, then turning and falling away — is not unique to this county either. Epidemiologists call a chart like Appendices A and B an epidemic curve, and outbreaks of this kind typically climb steeply and decline more gradually, for reasons epidemiology itself explains: transmission chains usually wane after a few generations, either because the number of people left to infect falls below what is needed to sustain spread, or because the response measures already under way start working. Finding the week a real curve like this stops accelerating — the week its concavity flips from turning-upward to turning-over — is exactly the technique real epidemiologists and public-health analysts use to spot a wave's turn before its peak, which is what Appendix D's model and Challenge D4 ask you to do here.

Where a figure in this case is not a matter of public record, it is stated as an explicit modeling assumption (Appendix F) rather than guessed, and every sourced figure is listed in Appendix E. Appendix D — The Prevention Model and the Metric

Every quantity below is either cited in Appendix E or stated in Appendix F. Nothing else enters the arithmetic, and no step is hidden: you can reproduce the whole decision table from this appendix, Appendix B and Appendix C in one pass.

Step 1 — case rate. For any week t in the horizon, the reported weekly case rate per 100,000 is R(t) = D(t) / POP × 100,000, where D(t) is the current-wave model of record (Appendix B) and POP is the population denominator (Appendix C).

Step 2 — the growth rate the district plans on. g = D'(t_dec) / D(t_dec), the model's own relative rate of change at the decision week t_dec = 11. This is a derived number, not a stated one: you compute it from the derivative found in D1.

Step 3 — the chain multiplier. A transmission prevented in week t also prevents the transmissions it would have gone on to cause before the horizon closes. The board's rule credits that chain as a finite geometric series at the planning growth rate:

w(t) = ( (1 + g)^(H - t + 1) - 1 ) / g

where H is the last week of the horizon. w(H) = 1 — a transmission prevented in the last week prevents only itself.

Step 4 — chain-weighted in-school transmissions. For each week t in the horizon, X(t) = k × R(t) / 100 × w(t), in chain-weighted transmissions per one thousand students, where k is the in-school transmission coefficient below.

InputValue
In-school transmission coefficient k0.5 in-school transmissions per 1,000 students in one open week when the community reported rate is 100 per 100,000 that week — see Appendix F, MA6, which reasons this down from the real school secondary attack rates in [S6]
Rapid antigen test performance in asymptomatic screeningaverage sensitivity 55.0% (95% CI 50.9%–59.0%) against RT-PCR in asymptomatic people, average specificity 99.5% [S5]
Programme effectiveness e of twice-weekly on-site testing0.10 — the share of in-school transmission the screening programme removes in an open week; see Appendix F, MA7, which builds it down from [S5]

Step 5 — the two plans.

Total       = sum of X(t) over every week t in the horizon        (no action at all)
Early Brake = Total - [ X(t0) + X(t0+1) ]                          t0 = first week of the horizon
Tripwire    = Total - [ X(tB) + X(tB+1) + e × sum of X(t) over every horizon week outside the closure ]
              where tB is the first horizon week with R(t) >= the board's written level

The primary metric is the number each plan leaves behind — chain-weighted in-school transmissions per one thousand students over the horizon. Lower is better. Both plans spend the same closure budget, so the metric compares what the budget buys, not what it costs.

    Find the equation of the tangent line to D(t) at the decision week t = 11: y = (slope)(t − 11) + D(11), i.e. slope and intercept in y = slope·t + intercept form.     Reveal the Twist

    Six weeks on, the county's own reported series is in front of you. Week twelve came in far higher than the fitted model said. Week thirteen higher still. Week fourteen — more than four times what your cubic predicted, and the crest, after which it fell away.

    The board's rule doesn't care what a model expected. It only reads the reported rate. Run the real numbers back through Appendix D's formula and see what happens to the decision table.
