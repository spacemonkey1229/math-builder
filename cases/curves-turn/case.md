Case Study: The Curve's Turn
MS | Math | Case Study | 2026 | Adventure

## Overview

Dr. Renata Vance has the projector remote in one hand and her phone face-down in the other, and the school board is due in this room in a matter of minutes. On the screen behind her is a chart she has looked at so many times this week that she has stopped seeing it — a jagged line of weekly case counts climbing out of the autumn, dipping, climbing again. Someone from facilities has stacked the folding chairs against the wall, and the room smells like floor wax and cold coffee. Her deputy has printed two memos and left them squared on the table, one laid on top of the other, the way you leave a choice for somebody else to make.

The superintendent stops in the doorway with his coat already on. "They're going to ask you for a date," he says. "Not a range. A date." Then he is gone down the corridor, and Renata is alone with the hum of the projector fan.

She knows what the smooth curve drawn through those points says about where this is going. She also knows the line underneath it is real data — reported, revised, ragged — and that every epidemiologist she trained with has watched a curve exactly like this one flatten into nothing the week after everybody panicked. Down the hall a custodian is running a floor buffer. Renata turns the top memo over, turns it back, and reaches for the remote.

## Background

Dr. Renata Vance is director of health services for Birchmont Unified School District. Under district policy she does not order a closure herself; she brings the board a single recommendation, and the board has never once voted against one of hers. She is accountable for what happens to instruction and for what happens to the people inside the buildings, and this week those two accountabilities pull in opposite directions.

Birchmont Unified is an invented district. The county it sits in is not, and neither is a single case count in this case: the weekly reported series in (SEE APPENDIX A) and (SEE APPENDIX B) is the actual published county series for the county named there, and the population denominator, the reference case-rate tiers and the test-performance figures are cited in (SEE APPENDIX E). Nothing about the decision is real — no health officer named Vance made this call and no board voted on it. Every figure that is not cited is stated openly in (SEE APPENDIX F).

Two plans are on the table, and one standing board rule constrains both. The board has authorised exactly one closure, of one fixed length, for the whole term — a closure budget, not a closure policy (SEE APPENDIX C). Whatever Vance recommends, the district spends that budget once, or not at all. Both plans therefore cost the district the same instructional days if they fire; they differ only in **when** the budget is spent, and in what else runs alongside it.

**The Early Brake** closes every school in the district for two weeks starting immediately, on the strength of the curve's turn. The smooth model fitted to the county's current wave (SEE APPENDIX B) has been concave up for weeks — the weekly case count is not merely rising, it is rising faster each week. That is the earliest defensible signal available, and it is a signal about a *rate*, not a level.

**The Tripwire** keeps every school open, runs twice-weekly on-site rapid antigen testing for consenting students and staff, and holds the closure budget in reserve until the reported case rate crosses a fixed level the board has already written down (SEE APPENDIX C). A level is unambiguous to read off a table. It is also structurally late: a level can only be crossed after the curve has already turned.

Vance is judged on one number, and the board has written that down too: chain-weighted in-school transmissions per one thousand students across the board's stated evaluation horizon (SEE APPENDIX D). The prevention model, the transmission coefficient and the chain multiplier that turn a county case rate into that number are all set out there. The vocabulary and the "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in (SEE APPENDIX H) — that is where Challenge 1 sends you. For real, freely available background reading on epidemic curves, school testing programmes and the county itself, beyond what the case requires, (SEE APPENDIX G).

## Dilemma

You are Dr. Renata Vance.
Do you:
A.) Recommend The Early Brake — close every school in the district for two weeks starting now, because the fitted curve has been turning upward for weeks OR
B.) Recommend The Tripwire — stay open with twice-weekly on-site rapid testing, and hold the closure until the reported case rate crosses the board's written level

## Appendix D — The Prevention Model and the Metric

Every quantity below is either cited in APPENDIX E or stated in APPENDIX F. Nothing else enters
the arithmetic, and no step is hidden: a Hero can reproduce the whole decision table from this
appendix, Appendix B and Appendix C in one pass.

**Step 1 — case rate.** For any week `t` in the horizon, the reported weekly case rate per 100,000
is `R(t) = D(t) / POP × 100,000`, where `D(t)` is the current-wave model of record (Appendix B) and
`POP` is the population denominator (Appendix C).

**Step 2 — the growth rate the district plans on.** `g = D'(t_dec) / D(t_dec)`, the model's own
relative rate of change at the decision week `t_dec = 11`. This is a derived number, not a stated
one: a Hero computes it from the derivative found in D1.

**Step 3 — the chain multiplier.** A transmission prevented in week `t` also prevents the
transmissions it would have gone on to cause before the horizon closes. The board's rule credits
that chain as a finite geometric series at the planning growth rate:

```
w(t) = ( (1 + g)^(H - t + 1) - 1 ) / g
```

where `H` is the last week of the horizon. `w(H) = 1` — a transmission prevented in the last week
prevents only itself.

**Step 4 — chain-weighted in-school transmissions.** For each week `t` in the horizon,

```
X(t) = k × R(t) / 100 × w(t)
```

in chain-weighted transmissions per one thousand students, where `k` is the in-school transmission
coefficient below.

| Input | Value |
|---|---|
| In-school transmission coefficient `k` | **0.5** in-school transmissions per 1,000 students in one open week when the community reported rate is 100 per 100,000 that week — see APPENDIX F, MA6, which reasons this down from the real school secondary attack rates in [S6] |
| Rapid antigen test performance in asymptomatic screening | average sensitivity **55.0%** (95% CI 50.9%–59.0%) against RT-PCR in asymptomatic people, average specificity 99.5% [S5] |
| Programme effectiveness `e` of twice-weekly on-site testing | **0.10** — the share of in-school transmission the screening programme removes in an open week; see APPENDIX F, MA7, which builds it down from [S5] |

**Step 5 — the two plans.**

```
Total       = sum of X(t) over every week t in the horizon        (no action at all)
Early Brake = Total - [ X(t0) + X(t0+1) ]                          t0 = first week of the horizon
Tripwire    = Total - [ X(tB) + X(tB+1) + e × sum of X(t) over every horizon week outside the closure ]
              where tB is the first horizon week with R(t) >= the board's written level
```

**The primary metric is the number each plan leaves behind** — chain-weighted in-school
transmissions per one thousand students over the horizon. Lower is better. Both plans spend the
same closure budget, so the metric compares what the budget buys, not what it costs.
