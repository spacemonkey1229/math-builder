# Guide Notes: Nuclear Waste

**Case:** Nuclear Waste · **Tier:** T3 · **Band:** MS-stretch/HS-entry · **Studio:** Heroes Academy
**Standards:** see `case.json.standards[]` for the exact ids (12 skills — cylinder/prism volume,
circle/rectangle area, cylinder surface area, percent, and comparison)

Legacy conversion, 2026-08-01, reformatted from the original 2021 Guide Notes
(`source/Y3/Session 5/Nuclear Waste/Guide Notes.docx`) onto the current 11-anchor ladder. The
original's own Lessons Learned section admits the cost numbers were "very one sided" — this
conversion answers that by declaring safety rating (not cost) the graded primary metric, keeping
the real, dramatic cost gap as disclosed context instead of forcing it into an artificial balance.

## Intro

Frame the format: this is a real federal safety decision, not a worksheet. Name the opener — "Two
companies. Two ways of storing something that stays dangerous for hundreds of thousands of years.
One contract."

## Launch

Read both companies' safety ratings aloud — 4.8 and 4.5 — with no cost figures mentioned yet. Ask:
"Just from the safety ratings, does this look like a close call or a clear one?" Do not confirm or
correct answers here.

## Anchor 1 — Take a stand and vote

**TAKE A STAND AND VOTE. No reasoning yet, just commit.**

"Who awards Isolation International? Who awards NuStorage?" Show of hands, no discussion. Record
the count — Close will ask them to vote again.

## Anchor 2 — What is the problem we're solving?

**"Is this the right question?" · "What do we need to consider in order to decide?"** (the
original Guide Notes' own language, preserved)

Let confusion grow here, deliberately. Push on the commission's actual job: "This isn't a company
picking the cheapest vendor — it's a safety regulator. Does that change what 'the right factor to
focus on' should be?"

## Anchor 3 — What is the process?

**"Do we consider all of these factors, focus on 2-3, or focus on only one? Which one(s)?"**
(also the original's own language)

Once the room has picked its real deciding factor(s), ask them to name the actual steps: find rods
per container (a volume comparison), total cost for the full order, max facility capacity, and the
safety-rating gap as a percentage. Re-ask: "Do we agree that's the right process for the factor we
picked?"

## Anchor 4 — What don't we know?

**"Do we need to make assumptions? Which ones are critical?"**

Surface the two real conventions: rods-per-container assumes no wasted space (a real simplification
of a much harder packing problem), and facility max capacity assumes the whole usable area is
filled edge-to-edge. Ask which one actually matters more to the final numbers.

## Data Deep Dive

Now, and only now, the numbers. Walk D1's volume comparison and D2's total-cost calculation
together as a room, cold-calling for the cylinder-volume step specifically — this is where a Hero
who used the wrong radius (diameter instead of radius) will get a real, visibly wrong rod count.

**D6, once the group is comfortable with volume.** Surface area is a genuinely different
calculation from volume, and the case gives Heroes a real reason to need it: each Isolation barrel
is fully sealed and lined on the inside, at a real, disclosed $12.50/sq ft. Cold-call for whether a
Hero remembered BOTH circular ends, not just the curved side wall — that's the same
radius-vs-diameter trap as D1, applied to a different formula. One barrel: ~80.11 sq ft, $1,001.38
to line. All 273 barrels this contract needs: $273,376.74 — a real number that adds to Isolation's
already-large cost disadvantage (Deep Conflict thread 1), without changing the graded safety-rating
comparison at all.

## Twist — drop this at ~15 minutes into the discussion

Deliver slowly, with pauses (the original Guide Notes' own twist, preserved):

"Transportation from the reactor sites to whichever facility you chose is done by rail. That
railroad passes through two major urban centers along the way."

**Facilitator-only.** This twist is deliberately qualitative, not a new number to plug in — it asks
Heroes to weigh a real transportation-safety factor that neither company's bid quantifies. Ask: "Does
this change your recommendation, or does it just add a factor you'd want the winning company to
address separately?" A Hero who already picked based on safety rating has a real, harder question:
does population-density-along-the-route belong in "safety rating" at all, or is it a genuinely
separate risk the rating doesn't capture?

Do not hand over a "correct" answer — this is a real, unresolved policy question, not a computation.

## Immediate recommit

Re-vote immediately. Ask by name: "Did the rail route change your recommendation, or did it just
make you want more information before finalizing?"

## Deep conflict — three threads (20-30 min)

1. **Safety rating vs. total cost.** Isolation is safer by a real 6.67% margin; NuStorage is
   cheaper by a real 268%. For a public-safety regulator, is any cost difference big enough to
   outweigh a safety-rating edge, or is safety supposed to be the tiebreaker only when cost is
   close?
2. **What a safety rating doesn't capture.** Above-ground inland vs. below-ground near a Great
   Lake; rail route through cities vs. not. Are these already priced into the 4.8/4.5 numbers, or
   are they real, separate risks the rating doesn't show?
3. **Who bears the decision, decades later.** The Overview's own closing line says someone will
   live with this decision long after the commissioners retire. How should that change how much
   weight today's numbers get against tomorrow's unknowns?

## The trap

A Hero computes the dramatic cost difference, recommends NuStorage on price alone, and never
engages with the fact that the deciding body is a safety regulator, not a cost-minimizing business.
The case's real tension is that the numbers point hard one way (cost) while the commission's actual
job points toward a different, closer number (safety). Watch for a pitch that treats "cheaper" as
automatically "better" for this particular decision-maker.

## Socratic questions

1. Isolation's safety rating is 6.67% higher than NuStorage's — how would you explain to the commission why that gap is or isn't decisive on its own?
2. NuStorage costs 268% less than Isolation for the same 250,000-rod order — does a cost gap that large ever get to override a safety-rating difference for a body whose whole mandate is public safety?
3. The rod-per-container calculation assumes zero wasted space — how would a real packing inefficiency (say, 15% wasted space) change each company's cost and capacity numbers?
4. NuStorage's facility sits below ground near Lake Huron; Isolation's sits above ground inland. Is that difference already reflected in their safety ratings, or is it a separate risk factor?
5. The original case's own numbers were flagged as "very one-sided" by the people who wrote it. What does it mean for a case's design that the same math, viewed through a different lens (safety instead of cost), turns out much closer?

## Close

**VOTE AGAIN.** "Did we ask the right question at the start?" "What math was new to you tonight, and
how did you actually attack it?" Compare the closing vote to Anchor 1's — a room that didn't move at
all after the twist is worth asking about directly: did the rail route not feel like a real factor,
or did the room already treat "safety" as bigger than what the rating number alone captures?

## Facilitator note — sensitivity

Cases touching public-health-and-safety regulatory decisions (here, radioactive waste storage) get
a one-line note per SPEC §6, even without a literal trigger word — the real-world stakes are
serious, though the case's own math never asks students to optimize casualty counts or treat harm
as an abstract quantity to minimize.
