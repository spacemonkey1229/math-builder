## Overview

"Moving on to our next order of business," states the Chairman, presiding over the weekly commission hearing. "We have before us two bids for the off-site, long-term storage of high-level radioactive waste from Region Three."

Two CEOs sit at the witness table, each representing a company that wants the contract. For the next two and a half hours, the commissioners work through the bids line by line — storage method, safety record, cost — while the room listens.

As the questioning wraps up, the Chairman looks down the row of commissioners. "Thank you both for your time today. We'll review these bids and announce our decision at the next session."

The room empties. The commissioners are left with two folders, two very different storage plans, and a decision that has to hold up for decades. Whatever they sign today, someone will still be living with it long after every person in this room has retired.

## Background

The commission is an independent federal agency tasked with protecting public health and safety related to nuclear energy — overseeing reactor safety, licensing, and the storage and disposal of spent fuel. It is awarding a contract to store spent Uranium-235 reactor rods from 15 different sites across Region Three. After 7-10 years cooling onsite at their original reactors, the rods are cool enough to transport, though still highly radioactive and requiring secure, specialized storage. The contract covers **250,000 spent fuel rods**, each a cylindrical container (SEE APPENDIX C).

**Isolation International**, headquartered in Decatur, Illinois, stores waste above ground in cylindrical barrels, laid out in rows and never stacked (SEE APPENDIX A). The company has operated its facility since 1984 and holds a **4.8 out of 5** safety rating. Every barrel Isolation uses is fully sealed and lined on the inside — top, bottom, and the curved side wall — with a radiation-shielding coating, at a real, disclosed cost per square foot of surface area (SEE APPENDIX A).

**NuStorage, LLC**, headquartered in Grand Rapids, Michigan, stores waste below ground in cube-shaped crates, stacked up to three high, at a facility just off the shore of Lake Huron built in 2011 (SEE APPENDIX B). NuStorage holds a **4.5 out of 5** safety rating.

Both companies are experienced, licensed, and highly regarded. The commission's job is to weigh what each one actually offers. Exactly how to count rods per container and how to size each facility's maximum capacity are fixed in **APPENDIX D**; use those conventions, or your numbers won't match anyone else's.

The vocabulary and the "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in **Appendix E** — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

## Appendix A: Isolation International Storage

Above-ground storage in cylindrical barrels: **7 feet tall, 3 feet in diameter**, stored in rows, never stacked. The facility sits on 37 acres, of which **16.8 acres** can actually be used for barrel storage. Transport cost: **$164.92 per barrel**. Storage cost: **$1,419.43 per barrel per year**. Safety rating: **4.8 out of 5**. Interior radiation-shielding lining cost: **$12.50 per square foot** of surface area (top, bottom, and curved side wall — the barrel is fully sealed).

## Appendix B: NuStorage, LLC Storage

Below-ground storage in cube-shaped crates (a rectangular prism with all sides equal): **5 feet on each side**, stacked up to **3 crates high**. The storage area totals **168,539 square feet**. Transport cost: **$103.57 per crate**. Storage cost: **$983.69 per crate per year**. Safety rating: **4.5 out of 5**.

## Appendix C: Fuel Rod Information

Each spent fuel rod is a metal cylindrical container measuring **19 inches long and 2.5 inches in diameter**.

## Appendix D: Modeling Assumptions

**Assumption 1 — Rods per container, by volume.** Rods-per-container is found by dividing container volume by rod volume, rounded down to the nearest whole rod. No packing inefficiency is modeled — a real simplification of a much harder 3-D packing problem, stated openly rather than hidden.

**Assumption 2 — Facility maximum capacity.** A facility's maximum container count is found by dividing its usable site area by one container's footprint area (times the stacking factor for NuStorage), rounded down. This treats the entire usable area as filled edge-to-edge, with no access aisles or safety buffers.
