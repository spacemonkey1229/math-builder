## Intro

Frame the format: this is not a "which number is bigger" problem in the way it first looks. Full
Stop actually removes *more* dollars than the $25,000 target requires — the room's real job is not
"which option saves more money" but who bears the cost, and whether the numerically "cleanest"
answer is actually the soundest one once you look past the dollar figure alone.

## Launch

Read Appendix A's five employee bios aloud, cold, before any dollar figures. Ask: "If you had to
guess, before you've seen a single number — who feels like the person Francesca lets go?" Do not
confirm or correct answers. Note who the room gravitates toward; it will matter at Anchor 4 and at
the Twist.

## Anchor 1 — Take a stand and vote

**TAKE A STAND AND VOTE. No reasoning yet, just commit.**

"Who stands with Full Stop? Who stands with Even Keel?" Show of hands, no discussion. Record the
count — Close will ask them to vote again.

## Anchor 2 — What is the problem we're solving?

**"Is this the right question?" · "What do we mean by 'closing the gap'?"**

Push on what "closing the gap" actually requires: exactly $25,000, or at least $25,000? For the
rest of 2019 only, or with an eye on 2020 too (D5)? Let it feel unresolved for a minute before
moving on — the room hasn't yet noticed that the two options don't remove the same dollar amount.

## Anchor 3 — What is the process?

**"If we go through this process and find the data, how will we decide?"**

Once the room has picked its deciding factor(s), ask them to name the actual steps: for Full Stop,
check each of the five employees' remaining pay against $25,000 one at a time; for Even Keel, find
the flat percent that removes exactly $25,000 from the five-employee pool. Re-ask: "Is that still
the right process for the factor we picked?"

## Anchor 4 — What don't we know?

**"Do we need to make assumptions? Which ones are critical?"**

Surface the real conventions this case states: (1) Francesca's own remaining pay is excluded from
the $25,000 target and from Even Keel's percentage base, because the Dilemma is about the team she
built, not about herself; (2) second-half 2019 revenue and expenses are assumed to continue at the
same rate as the first half, since no seasonal pattern is given; (3) the employee-level 2019 H1
figures in Appendix C don't sum to Appendix B's company-wide 2019 H1 payroll total — a real
inconsistency in the source data, not an error to "fix." Ask which one the room would push back on
if they could ask Francesca a follow-up question.

## Data Deep Dive

Now, and only now, the numbers. Walk D1 (bar graph) together as a room, then cold-call for D2's
five-employee shortfall check specifically — this is where a Hero who picked an employee by story
alone (Martha, Paul) discovers their remaining pay doesn't actually close $25,000 on its own. Then
work D3 (Even Keel's percent) and D4 (the full-year profit projection) before D5's trend line.

## Twist — drop this at ~15 minutes into the discussion

Deliver slowly, with pauses:

"A local trade-show organizer emails Francesca: they'd love to have Grampoline back next year,
specifically because of 'the sales rep who really understood what our members needed' — unmistakably
Christina, who ran the booth alone this spring."

**Facilitator-only.** This is a disclosed scenario assumption for the Twist, not a real, sourced
figure (see `case.json.modeling_assumptions` MA4): Appendix A states Christina personally accounted
for 41% of Grampoline's new sales in 2018, more than double Paul's 17%. Assume that share is also
representative of her share of *revenue growth* specifically — Grampoline's revenue grew $14,000
from 2017 to 2018 (Appendix B: $275,000 − $261,000). At a 41% attribution, roughly **$5,740** of
that growth traces to Christina, and losing her puts a comparable share of continued 2020 growth at
risk of not materializing.

If any Hero picked Full Stop *and specifically chose Christina* — the only single-employee cut that
cleanly closes the $25,000 target — ask them to recompute live: $26,450 payroll saved, minus $5,740
of at-risk future revenue, is a net benefit of only **$20,710** — which no longer actually clears
the $25,000 target. The "cleanest" number on paper isn't clean at all once the thing that made her
number large in the first place (she is very good at selling) is weighed against removing her. This
does not make Full Stop wrong for everyone — a different single employee is untouched by this twist
and still under-shoots the target the same way it did before — and it does not make Even Keel
automatically right either; it still cuts five real paychecks. It invalidates exactly one popular
reasoning path: "pick whichever employee's number is biggest."

**Soundness check (required before running this case live):** verified this twist holds under both
a sequential reading (state MA1, then MA2, then MA3, then the Twist's $5,740 figure, one at a time —
none contradicts an earlier one) and a simultaneous reading (apply all four at once: Francesca's own
pay is still excluded from both the $25,000 target and Even Keel's percentage base; the H1-to-H2
scaling convention for D4 is untouched by the Twist; the Appendix C footnote's employee-vs-company
payroll mismatch is unrelated to either). No combination produces a contradiction or a double-counted
dollar, and the Twist's $5,740 figure depends only on Appendix B's given 2017/2018 revenue and
Appendix A's given 41% figure — neither is affected by any other Modeling Assumption.

## Immediate recommit

Re-vote immediately. Ask by name: "Did this change your position — or does it just mean Full Stop
needs a second, smaller cut alongside whichever employee you pick?"

## Deep conflict — three threads (20–30 min)

1. **The "clean" number vs. the real number.** Full Stop with Christina removes $1,450 more than
   the target on paper — but the Twist shows her real net benefit is $4,290 *short* of the target.
   Is a dollar figure that requires a hidden assumption to be trustworthy still the "clean" answer?
2. **Concentrated vs. distributed pain.** Full Stop puts the entire hit on one household; Even Keel
   spreads a smaller hit across five. Is it more defensible to remove exactly the target from five
   people, or to remove slightly more than the target from one?
3. **Who gets picked, and on what basis.** If the room leans Full Stop, push on *why* that person
   and not another — tenure, family circumstance, sales performance, or the Twist's revenue-risk
   finding are all real, disclosed factors in Appendix A, and they don't all point the same way.

## The trap

A Hero picks an employee to let go based on their personal story before checking whether the number
actually solves the stated problem. Martha's caregiving hardship and Paul's missed sales targets are
the two most emotionally obvious candidates for Full Stop — but their remaining pay falls $1,600 and
$4,500 short of the $25,000 target, respectively. Only Christina's remaining pay works alone — and
the Twist shows that even her "clean" number isn't as clean as it looks once her value to the
company (not just her paycheck) is priced in.

## Socratic questions

1. You've said Full Stop removes more money than the $25,000 target requires — a real margin of 5.8%. Why would removing *more* money than necessary not automatically make Full Stop the better financial choice?
2. If your reason for choosing an employee under Full Stop is their personal story — Martha's caregiving, Paul's missed targets — what happens to your argument once you check whether their remaining pay actually closes the $25,000 gap on its own?
3. Even Keel cuts five paychecks instead of one. How would you defend that as the *more* compassionate option, not just the less painful one to calculate?
4. Your D4 profit projection assumes the second half of 2019 looks like the first half. What happens to your year-end profit number if that assumption is wrong in either direction?
5. Now that you know about the revenue risk tied to Christina, does the math actually favor letting her go less than it first appeared — and by how much, in dollars?

## Close

**VOTE AGAIN.** "Did we ask the right question at the start?" "What math was new to you tonight —
the trend line, the shortfall check, something else — and how did you actually attack it?" Compare
the closing vote to Anchor 1's — a room that gravitated toward Christina at Launch and stayed there
after the Twist is worth asking about directly: did the $5,740 at-risk-revenue figure not feel real
to them, or did they weigh it and still land on Christina for other reasons?

## Lessons Learned

- Whether Heroes checked that their chosen Full Stop employee's remaining pay actually closes
  $25,000 (only Christina's does, and even then it overshoots by $1,450), or assumed any single
  firing works because "the number looked big enough."
- Whether Heroes computed Even Keel's percent on the correct $112,250 base (five employees only) or
  mistakenly included Francesca's own remaining pay.
- Whether the Twist's $5,740 at-risk-revenue figure changed anyone's chosen employee, or just
  prompted a values discussion about whether that risk should matter at all.
- Whether Heroes noticed Appendix C's footnote (the employee-level payroll total doesn't match the
  company-wide total) and correctly used each table for the deliverable it's meant for, rather than
  trying to force the two figures to agree.

## Facilitator note — sensitivity

This case touches money stress and family hardship directly (Francesca going without pay, Martha's
caregiving crisis, a real possibility of job loss) — SPEC.md §6 flags this for the discussion lead.
Keep the frame on the case's own disclosed numbers and each employee's stated circumstances in
Appendix A; steer away from personal financial-hardship anecdotes from the room, which can turn a
math discussion into something a facilitator isn't equipped to hold.

in Appendix B/C is the source workbook's own given data, not swept by probe 5 by design
