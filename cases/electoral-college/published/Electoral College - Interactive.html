Overview

The conference room smells like burnt coffee and dry-erase marker. Six screens glow along the back wall, each one locked on a different cable-news poll tracker, and none of them agree with each other. A map of the United States takes up the whole far wall, half of it already shaded in — the easy half.

"Alright, folks, listen up." Jane Earl doesn't raise her voice. She doesn't have to. "Let's focus on the battleground states. Mark, give us the breakdown."

Mark Onerog clicks to the next slide without looking away from it. Sarah Theopolis is already talking over him about the ads running in Florida. James Beeson cuts back in about the Rust Belt. Oscar Newsom's phone buzzes twice on the table and he ignores it both times, still describing what the volunteers are hearing at the doors.

Jane lets it run for a few seconds — the noise is useful, it's how she finds out what she doesn't know yet — and then holds up one hand. The room quiets.

She looks at the map. Then at the spreadsheet open in front of her, the one with a single cell highlighted in yellow: a dollar figure, and next to it, a countdown that reads in days, not weeks.

Someone has to tell Paul Peaken where to fly first.

Jane pulls the spreadsheet closer and reaches for her laptop.

Background

Jane Earl is the campaign manager for Paul Peaken, the Democratic candidate for President of the United States in the 2044 election, running against Republican governor Harrison Farnsworth. Jane has managed three presidential campaigns, including the two prior wins by Peaken's former running mate — but this race is closer than either of those. Election Day is 6 days away.

Jane's job for these final days is narrow and specific: decide which states, if any, are worth sending Paul Peaken to visit in person before voters cast their ballots. From past campaigns, Jane's team has found that an in-person visit — a factory floor, a diner, a door-knocking event, all of it covered by local press — measurably moves undecided voters in a candidate's favor in the days that follow. It does not move voters who have already committed to the opponent.

The campaign has $19,000,000 left overall, but $16,000,000 of that is already committed to advertising and cannot be redirected — leaving a fixed travel budget for the final week (SEE APPENDIX E). Every state visit draws from that same fixed pool, and once it's spent, it's spent: there is no second trip to make later.

Jane can schedule Paul into a state for a half day or a full day, and every visit — regardless of length — carries a flat per-state travel setup cost on top of the per-day cost (SEE APPENDIX E). There are only 6 days left on the calendar, and each day can be spent as one full-day visit to a single state, or split into two half-day visits to two different states (SEE APPENDIX E).

The most recent, most reliable polling (taken November 1st) is in from every state (SEE APPENDIX B and APPENDIX C). Nine states are already locked in for Peaken and five are already locked in for Farnsworth — each polling above 90% for its candidate, meaning no realistic amount of campaigning changes the outcome there (SEE APPENDIX B). The other 37 states are genuinely contested, each with its own mix of committed Peaken voters, committed Farnsworth voters, and undecided voters (SEE APPENDIX C). To win the presidency, Peaken needs at least 270 of the 538 total electoral votes (SEE APPENDIX A).

The campaign's data analysts have found that each half day a candidate spends campaigning in a state shifts a fixed share of that state's undecided voters — and only the undecided ones — into the candidate's column (SEE APPENDIX E). A campaign visit cannot flip a voter who already told a pollster they support the other candidate.

Whichever states Jane picks, and however many days she sends Paul to each, she needs a real plan: a defensible answer for exactly which states, how long in each, what share of the vote it wins, and which voters within those states the campaign should actually spend its limited time talking to (SEE APPENDIX D). Broadly, Jane sees two ways to spend the travel budget: a Big-Prize Strategy that puts the largest possible number of electoral votes into play by targeting the biggest swing states, or a Margin Strategy that chases the narrowest polling gaps regardless of a state's size, betting that winning several separate close races is worth more than winning one or two big ones. Every figure in this case that isn't a direct citation is a disclosed, stated Modeling Assumption (SEE APPENDIX F), backed where possible by real sourcing (SEE APPENDIX G) — none is invented quietly. The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in Appendix H — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

Scheduling rule: each of the 6 remaining days can be spent as one full-day visit to a single state, or split into two half-day visits to two different states. The flat per-state travel cost applies once per state visited, no matter how many half- or full-day visits are scheduled there across the week. A campaign visit can only move a state's undecided voters — it cannot be assumed to flip a voter already committed to the opposing candidate, and it cannot move more voters than a state actually has left undecided (SEE APPENDIX C, "Undecided %" column, for each state's ceiling).

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Visit Cost Calculator (D1)

    Goal: given how many half-day and full-day visits you schedule in ONE state, compute the total cost (flat per-state fee + per-visit cost), using Appendix E's figures.

    Step 2 — Vote-Share Equation Solver (D2)

    Goal: pick a contested state from Appendix C and solve the one-step equation for the minimum number of half-days needed for Peaken's projected share to strictly EXCEED Farnsworth's (a tie is not a win). Watch the state's own Undecided % ceiling.

    Step 3 — Electoral Vote % Calculator (D3)

    Goal: given a total electoral-vote count your plan reaches (locked + newly flipped), compute what percent of all 538 electoral votes that represents.

    Step 4 — Cost-Efficiency Ratio (D4)

    Goal: for a state you're evaluating, compute cost per electoral vote and cost per percentage point flipped — the two ratios D4 asks you to rank all 37 contested states by.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    "Indiana just released a corrected poll." A follow-up verification round found that 4 points of Indiana's previously counted "undecided" voters had already committed to Farnsworth before November 1 — undecided isn't 23%, it's 19%, and Farnsworth's share rises from 48% to 52%. (Peaken's 29% is unchanged — Indiana's row still sums to 100%.)

    Does this change your plan? You have 60 seconds — are you changing your states, or not?
