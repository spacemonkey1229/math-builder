Case Study: The One-Pass Route

Overview

Renata Kwan has the district’s paper street map taped across the hood of the sweeper truck, its corners lifting in the diesel exhaust, and a red grease pencil already chewed flat on one end. Leaf season starts soon, and the route she inherited last spring has her trucks doubling back down Larkspur over and over before the shift ever reaches Birchwood. The radio on her hip crackles — the division director wants her routing memo by end of day, the one that decides whether the department pays for a short stretch of new pavement or just keeps eating the extra diesel every single week. Behind her, the sweeper idles, gutter broom folded up, waiting on her to draw the line. She traces Larkspur with the pencil, then Birchwood, then the short dead-end nub off Persimmon that has bothered her for two sweeps running. Somewhere on this map is the exact reason none of this ever closes into one clean loop, and she is fairly sure she has finally found it — not a guess this time, a number. She uncaps the pencil, looks up at the idling truck, and starts counting corners.

Background

Renata Kwan is the Route Supervisor for the Streets and Sanitation Division of the Department of Public Works in the city of Talbrook. She is responsible for sweeper-truck routing across Talbrook’s residential neighborhoods and is accountable to the division director for the fuel and labor cost of every route she designs — including every “deadhead” mile her crews drive over a street segment they have already swept that same shift (SEE APPENDIX A). She does not control the city’s road-construction budget, but her annual routing memo is what the division director uses to decide whether that budget funds a new residential connector street this cycle.

This year’s memo covers Larkspur Hollow, a real residential neighborhood whose full street network — every block, every intersection, every dead end — has been mapped as a graph: each intersection or dead end a vertex, each street segment between two of them a path. The real network contains 58 vertices connected by 86 edges, totaling 3.96 miles of street (SEE APPENDIX A). A sweeper truck that starts and ends at the same depot corner and drives every street segment exactly once, with nothing repeated, closing into one continuous cycle, is only possible if every vertex in that graph touches an even number of street segments — Euler’s theorem, applied literally to pavement (SEE APPENDIX B). Larkspur Hollow’s real intersection layout does not have that property: 48 of its 58 vertices touch an odd number of segments, which means a true single-pass route is not merely hard to find — on the network as it exists today, it is mathematically impossible (SEE APPENDIX B).

Larkspur Hollow’s graph is modeled on a real neighborhood’s real street layout, not an invented grid — building it honestly required a few disclosed modeling choices about where the district’s boundary is drawn and how often a route like this really runs (SEE APPENDIX E).

Two responses are on Renata’s desk before the memo is due, and the division can only fund one this cycle. The As-Is Loop keeps Larkspur Hollow’s street network exactly as it is and instead computes the specific, calculable minimum amount of already-swept street the route must drive a second time to close into one continuous loop — no capital request, a fixed and predictable increase in fuel cost every single week the crew sweeps (SEE APPENDIX C). The Persimmon Connector asks the division to fund a short new stretch of pavement joining two of the network’s vertices, at a one-time construction cost of roughly $1,600 per linear foot for an estimated 302-foot connector — a change to the graph itself that reduces, though it does not eliminate, how much repeat mileage every future sweep needs (SEE APPENDICES C AND D). Every sourced figure in this case is listed in APPENDIX F; the vocabulary and “how to…” skills the case will ask of you are listed in APPENDIX G — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

How Appendix A was built

[S3] Larkspur Hollow's street network is modeled on the real street layout of the town of Seaside, Florida, pulled directly from OpenStreetMap (SEE APPENDIX E, MA1). The raw geometry was collapsed into a graph the same way a real GIS routing tool does: every node where two or more differently-named streets meet, and every dead end, becomes a vertex; every uninterrupted run of the same-named street between two such vertices, however many shape points it takes to draw its curve, becomes a single edge whose length is the summed real-world distance along that curve (computed with the great-circle/haversine formula from the raw GPS coordinates, not a straight-line guess). Three OSM way segments that leave Seaside's built area and run for over a fifth of a mile toward neighboring, unmapped communities were excluded as outside Renata's assigned route district (SEE APPENDIX E, MA2); one small, disconnected two-node fragment with no path back to the rest of the network was also excluded.

Euler's theorem outcome for this networkNeither an Euler circuit nor an Euler path exists. A graph has an Euler circuit only if every vertex has even degree (0 odd vertices), and an Euler path (but no circuit) only if exactly 2 vertices have odd degree. Larkspur Hollow has 48 odd-degree vertices — far more than 2 — so neither special case applies, and *some* repeated driving is unavoidable no matter how the route is planned [S3, derived].

    Twist

    No scripted twist for this case.
