Overview

Priya Anand has the reticle-order screen open on her laptop, the cursor parked on a field marked DIE SIZE (MM²) that will not let her submit until it is filled. Behind her, the small conference room's speakerphone is muted but blinking — the board is already dialed in, waiting on the number she promised them an hour ago. Someone taped a printout of two chip floorplans to the whiteboard this morning, side by side, one noticeably smaller than the other, both labeled only “Option A” and “Option B” in marker.

TSMC's wafer-start slot for their process quarter closes today. Once Priya submits this field and the mask set is cut, there is no redo — the company's entire first tape-out budget rides on whichever number goes in that box next. Her phone buzzes face-down on the table: the CEO, again, asking if she's decided.

She has run each option's numbers twice already and gotten two different confident answers depending on which sheet she was reading. The engineer at her elbow, headset still around his neck from the last stand-up, taps the smaller floorplan. “More chips per wafer. It's not close.”

Priya doesn't reach for the field yet. More chips is not the same question as more money, and she has not yet run both all the way through. She opens a blank spreadsheet instead of typing the number.

Background

Priya Anand is the director of silicon engineering at a fabless AI-chip startup preparing its first commercial tape-out with TSMC, the foundry that manufactures the company's chip design onto physical silicon wafers. Priya has final sign-off on one irreversible decision before the mask set (the physical stencil used to pattern every wafer) is cut: how large, in square millimeters, to make the die — the single rectangle of silicon that becomes one finished chip once the wafer is cut apart. Two floorplans are ready to go, labeled The Compact Die and The Full Die (SEE APPENDIX B).

A wafer is a large, thin, circular disc of silicon; TSMC patterns many identical copies of a die onto one wafer, then cuts the wafer apart into individual chips (SEE APPENDIX B). A smaller die means more copies fit on each wafer — but it is not the only thing that changes. Wafers are not manufactured perfectly: microscopic manufacturing flaws, called defects, land at random across the wafer's surface, and any die unlucky enough to contain one at all normally fails testing and cannot be sold. A larger die area is more likely to contain at least one defect than a smaller die area is, so the two options do not just differ in how many dies fit on a wafer — they differ in what fraction of those dies actually work (SEE APPENDIX D). The two options also do not carry the same chip: the Full Die packs proportionally more transistors — and therefore more compute capability — onto its larger area, which the company can sell for a proportionally higher price per chip (SEE APPENDIX C, APPENDIX E).

The company has committed to exactly one wafer lot with TSMC this quarter — the budget will not stretch to run both options — so Priya's decision is final for this product generation, and the mask order must be placed before today's reticle lock deadline (SEE APPENDIX A). Every number in this case, from the wafer's own area down to the fraction of a defect per square centimeter, is large or small enough that it is normally written in scientific notation rather than standard notation; TSMC's own engineering documentation does the same (SEE APPENDIX F). The vocabulary and the “how to…” skills this case will ask you to research are listed in the final appendix — that is where Challenge 1 sends you (SEE APPENDIX F). All figures in this case are sourced (SEE APPENDIX G). Real-world industry terms used throughout this case are defined in plain language (SEE APPENDIX H).

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 · Scientific-Notation Conversions (D1)

    Convert Appendix B's wafer diameter (cm) and both die areas (cm²) into scientific notation, entering each as a coefficient (1–10) and a base-10 exponent.

    Step 2 · Transistor Count (D2)

    Multiply Appendix C's transistor density by each option's die area (both in scientific notation) to find each option's total transistor count.

    Step 3 · Gross Dies Per Wafer (D3)

    Apply Appendix B's formula for both options: Gross Dies = (Wafer Area ÷ Die Area) − (π × Wafer Diameter) ÷ √(2 × Die Area). Give each as a whole number of dies.

    Step 4 · Yield and Net Good Dies (D4)

    Apply Appendix D's Seeds model, DY = 1 ÷ (1 + A × D₀), to both options at the same defect density, then multiply by each option's gross die count.

    Step 5 · Revenue Per Wafer and Recommendation (D5)

    Compute net good dies × price per die (Appendix E) for each option, then write your recommendation. There is no single "correct" recommendation to check — the Check button only verifies your arithmetic.
