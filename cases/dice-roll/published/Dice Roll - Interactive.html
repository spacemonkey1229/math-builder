Overview

Same 12-county roster, two different selection processes. The Statewide Draw's actual 5-county pick (top) reaches every region; The Front Range Loop (bottom) never leaves the Front Range column, no matter how many cycles run.

Twenty ten-sided dice sit in a shallow tray under the meeting-room camera, and the livestream chat is already scrolling faster than Marisol Ibarra can read it. A staffer in a blazer picks up the first die, rolls it across the felt, and reads the digit into the microphone. Four. Another staffer types it into a form on the projector screen. Nineteen more to go.

Marisol watches from the second row with a legal pad on her knee, half her attention on the dice and half on the seating chart of Colorado's sixty-four counties taped to the wall behind the podium. In eleven minutes, that twenty-digit number the dice are building — random, public, impossible to have picked in advance — will seed the software that tells every county in the state exactly which ballots its bipartisan audit board has to pull and recount by hand.

The audit itself isn't Marisol's call. State law already requires it, in all sixty-four counties, every time. What's on her legal pad is a narrower question, and it's the one her director wants answered before the seating chart comes down off that wall: with a travel budget that covers five site visits this cycle, which five counties does the compliance-monitoring team actually go stand inside, in person, to watch the audit boards work?

The last die lands. Six. Her director leans over. "You said you'd have the visit list by end of day."

Marisol looks down at the two lists already sketched on her legal pad.

Background

Here is the concrete question, stripped of the ceremony: Marisol's director wants a list of exactly 5 counties — out of this case's 12-county working roster standing in for Colorado's full 64 — where the Division's own compliance-monitoring team will show up in person this cycle to watch a county audit board work.

The one distinction to hold onto: the risk-limiting audit itself is required in all 64 counties, every cycle, no matter what Marisol decides. Nothing about her choice changes whether a county gets audited. What she's actually deciding is narrower — which counties a Division observer shows up to watch in person.

The dice matter because they are where this case's Statistics-and-Probability content actually lives: they are the real mechanism Colorado's own risk-limiting audit uses to turn "random" from a promise into a number anyone can check and reproduce (SEE APPENDIX B) [S4]. Marisol's own visit-plan choice runs on that identical idea — a public, reproducible chance process — just applied one level up: not which ballots get recounted, but which counties a Division observer actually walks into.

Two concrete plans sit on her legal pad: The Statewide Draw, which lets a chance process pick both the counties and the sessions inside them, and The Front Range Loop, which simply sends the team to the 5 counties closest to Denver every time, no draw involved.

Simple random sampling, systematic sampling, cluster sampling, and multistage sampling — the four tools this case asks a Hero to actually use — are not exotic or invented for this exercise: OpenStax's Introductory Statistics textbook lists the stratified, cluster, and systematic sample as the standard "well-known" alternatives taught alongside simple random sampling in any first statistics course [S9], and pairing a random county draw with a random within-county subsample — exactly what The Statewide Draw does — is the textbook definition of a multistage sample.

What genuinely isn't public record is whether Colorado's real Division uses one of these methods, or something else entirely, to pick which counties its own compliance-monitoring team visits in person; no such policy is published (SEE APPENDIX D), so this case models that specific choice as the open, unresolved question it actually is, not a foregone conclusion dressed up as one.

Colorado has 64 counties (SEE APPENDIX A) [S1]. Since 2009, Colorado Revised Statutes §1-7-515 has required that, starting with the 2017 coordinated election and after every primary, general, coordinated, or congressional vacancy election held since, every county that uses ballot-counting equipment must conduct a risk-limiting audit (RLA) — a statistically designed manual recount of a randomly selected set of ballots, run by a bipartisan county audit board, that continues until it either confirms the reported outcome to a fixed confidence level or triggers a full hand count [S2] [S3]. That requirement does not change based on anything in this case: all 64 counties run their own RLA, every cycle, regardless of which counties Marisol picks.

Marisol Ibarra is the Compliance Audit Coordinator in the Colorado Department of State's Elections Division. Her job is not to run the county audits — it is to make sure the Department can stand behind them. Beyond the paperwork every county already files, the Division sends a small compliance-monitoring team to a limited number of counties each cycle to observe the audit board's work in person: watching chain of custody handling, confirming the audit follows the seed-driven ballot selection exactly as designed, and catching the kind of procedural slip that never shows up in a written report (SEE APPENDIX C). This cycle, the team's travel budget and staff-days cover exactly 5 in-person site visits (SEE APPENDIX C) — out of a 12-county working roster [MA4] this case uses to represent Colorado's full geographic spread, from the Front Range to the Eastern Plains, the San Luis Valley, and the Western Slope (SEE APPENDIX A).

Marisol worked as a county poll worker herself for three elections before she ever crossed over to the compliance side, so she knows what a real polling place feels like from the inside — the folding tables, the long lines of forms to sign, the exact moment in the evening when a tired precinct judge starts making small procedural shortcuts nobody meant any harm by. That ground-level memory is part of why she reads a county's audit floor so well now, and part of why she doesn't rattle when a county clerk pushes back hard on a finding — she's stood on the other side of that conversation. A single six-sided die sits on the corner of her desk, worn smooth at the edges; an instructor in her very first audit-methods training years ago used a handful of ordinary dice to walk the room through what a random sample actually is, and she kept one as a reminder that the math in front of her — 5 of 12 counties, sessions picked on an interval — is the same simple idea, just scaled up.

Marisol has narrowed her visit plan to two options, using the same kind of public, reproducible random-seed process the statewide RLA itself uses (SEE APPENDIX B) [S4]:

The Statewide Draw selects the 5 visited counties by simple random sampling from the full 12-county roster, then, inside each selected county, uses systematic sampling to pick which of that county's scheduled audit sessions the team actually sits in on (SEE APPENDIX B). Every county on the roster — Front Range, plains, valley, or Western Slope — has a real, calculable chance of a Division observer walking in the door.

The Front Range Loop sends the team to the 5 counties nearest the Denver office — a convenience sample (SEE APPENDIX A). It costs less to run and needs no overnight travel. It also means the Division's own eyes never once reach the Eastern Plains, the San Luis Valley, or the Western Slope, no matter how many cycles run, no matter how the travel budget is spent.

Appendix B — This Cycle's Sampling Mechanics

How Colorado's own RLA sets its random seed (for context — this is the state's real, statutory process, not something Marisol controls): at a public meeting, staff roll 20 ten-sided dice one at a time on camera while the meeting livestreams; each roll becomes one digit of a 20-digit random seed, entered into the Department of State's open-source RLA software to select the specific ballots each county's audit board must recount [S4]. The process is designed to be public, unpredictable in advance, and exactly reproducible afterward from the recorded seed.

The Statewide Draw's realized county selection, this cycle (one specific simple-random draw of 5 of the 12 counties in Appendix A, using the Division's own public-seed selection procedure): Jefferson, Boulder, Pueblo, Kit Carson, Alamosa.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Population, sample, and percent (D1)

    This case's working roster has 12 counties (Appendix A); the state actually has 64. This cycle's visit sample is 5 counties. What percent of the 12-county working roster does the sample represent? What percent of the full 64-county state would that scale to?

    Step 2 — Selection probability, one cycle and three cycles (D4)

    Under The Statewide Draw, 5 of 12 roster counties are chosen each cycle by simple random sampling. What is the probability a specific county (say, a Western Slope or San Luis Valley county) is selected in a single cycle? What is the probability it is selected at least once across three independent cycles? (Hint: find the probability it is never selected in three cycles first, then subtract from 1.) Compare both numbers to what The Front Range Loop gives that same county, every cycle, forever.

    What is that same county's probability of ever being visited under The Front Range Loop, no matter how many cycles run?

    Step 3 — Total program cost and the margin (D5)

    Using Appendix C's rates and Appendix A's distances, compute each option's total program cost (mileage + lodging + M&IE + staff time) for its 5-county visit list, then the percent margin between them.

    Step 4 — The cluster cost check (D3)

    Appendix B lists how many audit sessions are scheduled this cycle in each Statewide Draw county, and the target of 2 sessions observed per county. At $25.00 per session reviewed (Appendix C), what would it cost to review every scheduled session in those 5 counties (the full cluster), versus the systematic subsample of 2 per county?

    Step 5 — The Statewide Draw's systematic-sampling interval (D2)

    Appendix B's realized draw selected Jefferson, Boulder, Pueblo, Kit Carson, and Alamosa, with scheduled audit sessions of 18, 14, 9, 3, and 4 respectively. For each county, the systematic-sampling interval k is that county's scheduled sessions divided by the target of 2 sessions observed, rounded down to a whole number. Then pick a starting session at random between 1 and k — enter any whole number in that valid range; the checker confirms it falls inside it, since the actual starting point is your own random pick, not a fixed answer.

    Reminder: k is always rounded down (Pueblo's 9 scheduled sessions ÷ 2 target does not divide evenly, so it rounds down to 4, not up to 5) — that's what makes this "systematic," not "even."

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    Colorado's own real risk-limiting audit -- the one that already happens in all 64 counties regardless of Marisol's visit plan -- sets its random seed at a public meeting where staff roll 20 ten-sided dice on camera while the meeting livestreams, and anyone can verify the resulting seed and recompute the ballot selection afterward (Appendix B, Appendix E S4).

    Suppose a county clerk pushes back on The Statewide Draw: "If a county's own audit already uses a fully public, verifiable random process, why does it matter whether the Division's observers get there by a random draw or by convenience? The audit itself is equally random either way." Using your D4 probability numbers, what is the flaw in that argument -- what, specifically, is being sampled by Marisol's visit plan that is not the same thing the county-level RLA already randomizes?
