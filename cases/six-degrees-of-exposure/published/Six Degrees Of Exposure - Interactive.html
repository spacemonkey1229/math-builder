Overview

Dr. Yolanda Marsh has the county's outbreak-response line open in one ear and a spreadsheet of contact-tracing interviews open on both monitors. Calloway State's health center called it in nine days ago: mumps, confirmed, then confirmed again, then confirmed a third time in a different residence hall. Her team has spent the week doing what contact tracers do — sitting across from sick and exposed students, asking who they eat with, who they study with, whose room they crashed in after a party — and logging every name into the same interaction graph the university's own platform already keeps. The graph is open on her second monitor now, a mess of dots and lines she can no longer read by eye.

Her regional director is blunter on the phone than usual. The county's rapid-response vaccination clinic can go live tomorrow morning, but the dose allocation that just cleared the state warehouse is fixed, and so is the outreach staff assigned to administer it — not a dose or a labor-hour more. Two operational plans sit in her inbox, both built to spend that exact same allocation and neither one a dollar more. One plan sends her contact-tracing team back out to confirm and vaccinate the small number of students its own graph says are the most connected people on campus. The other skips that confirmation work entirely and vaccinates a much larger number of students chosen at random from the same traced population.

Her director wants an answer before the clinic opens its doors. Dr. Marsh minimizes the spreadsheet, opens both plans side by side, and starts reading.

Background

Dr. Yolanda Marsh is the lead epidemiologist for the Calloway County Department of Public Health. She is responsible for the county's outbreak-response operations and accountable to the county health director for how the fixed, one-time state dose allocation is spent (see Appendix D). She does not set the size of that allocation and cannot request more once the clinic opens, but she authors the operational plan the clinic runs on.

The outbreak is centered on Calloway State University. Over nine days, the county's contact-tracing team built a traced-contact network of 1,899 students and staff, connected by 13,838 confirmed contact pairs (see Appendix A; see Appendix E, MA1). Every pair in the network carries a weight equal to the number of separate contact events logged between those two people during the surveillance window (see Appendix A) — two people who crossed paths once carry a light edge; roommates or lab partners who were logged together dozens of times carry a heavy one. Like every real contact-tracing graph, this one is badly uneven: most people in it have only a handful of traced contacts, a small number have dozens, and a very small number are traced to far more contacts than a typical person in the graph (see Appendix A).

Quick facts (Appendix A)
1,899 traced individuals 13,838 confirmed contact pairs 59,835 total contact-weight 193-day surveillance window

Two operational plans are under consideration. Both are sized to draw down the exact same fixed dose-and-outreach budget (see Appendix D) — neither can run over it, and neither is required to run under it:

Operation Hub Sweep

Sends the contact-tracing team back out to confirm and vaccinate the 50 individuals the traced network ranks highest by degree — the number of distinct people each one is confirmed to have been in contact with (see Appendix B). Locating, re-confirming, and physically reaching each of these 50 people costs more per dose, because it requires the same case-investigation fieldwork the tracing team has been doing all week (see Appendix D).

Operation Wide Net

Skips that confirmation fieldwork and vaccinates 500 individuals selected uniformly at random from the same traced population, at the lower per-dose cost of a standard outreach-clinic invitation — a postcard, a text, a scheduled slot, no case investigation required (see Appendix D).

The two operations are judged on a single number: the total weight of traced-contact connections eliminated from further spread once vaccination takes effect, per dollar of the shared budget (see Appendices A, B and D). Because both operations spend the identical budget, the comparison reduces to a direct question — which operation's vaccinated set removes more of the network's total contact-weight? Dr. Marsh's team also needs a fast way to warn newly-exposed contacts down a chain of confirmed contacts once a case is found (see Appendix C). Every sourced figure in this case is listed in Appendix F, and the vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in Appendix G — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

Sum of these 50 degrees: 6,241 (double-counts any edge with both endpoints in this list). Sum of these 50 weighted degrees: 30,224 (double-counts the same way, but in contact-weight instead of edge count).

Of the 6,241 counted edges, exactly 382 connect two of these same 50 individuals to each other, with a combined weight of 2,581. Each of those 382 is counted twice above (once at each endpoint) and needs to be subtracted back out exactly once — not left in at full double-weight, and not removed entirely — to reach the true, non-double-counted total. Appendix C — Sample Weighted Contact Subgraph

A small, real excerpt of the traced network, kept to a size a person can trace by hand. Used for D1 and D4.

EdgeWeight (recorded contact events)

Notification-cost rule (for D4). A pair recorded in contact more times has a faster, more reliable line for passing along an exposure notification. Define the notification cost of an edge as 1 ÷ (that edge's weight). The fastest way to notify one traced individual starting from another is the path — direct or through intermediate people — that minimizes the total summed notification cost, not necessarily the path with the fewest steps. D4 asks you to find the fastest notification path from Contact 47 to Contact 105.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 · Degree of a vertex (D1/D2 warm-up)

    Appendix C's subgraph has an edge between Contact 47 and every one of: Contact 46, 78, 91, 567, 814, 1142, 1299, 1540. What is the of Contact 47 in this subgraph — the count of distinct edges touching it?

    Step 2 · Weighted edges at one vertex (D1 support)

    Using Appendix C's edge-weight table, add up the weight of every edge that touches Contact 814. (Contact 814 appears in three edges — find them and sum their weights.)

    Step 3 · The counting argument for a random sample (D3)

    Wide Net's 500 people can't be listed by hand, so its expected weight eliminated comes from a counting argument: for a network of n_total people, P(a given edge survives an n_wide-person random draw untouched) = [(n_total−n_wide)(n_total−n_wide−1)] ÷ [n_total(n_total−1)]. Slide n_wide and compute the expected weight eliminated = total_weight × (1 − that survival probability). Enter your own answer for the slider's current position, then check it.
    500

    This step recomputes the counting argument live for whatever n_wide you choose — it does not print a single fixed answer.

    Step 4 · Budget-consistency check (D3, Appendix D)

    500 Wide Net doses at $38.00 each draw down $19,000.00 of the shared budget. At Hub Sweep's fully-loaded rate of $213.94 per dose, how many Hub Sweep doses would that same $19,000.00 actually buy? (Divide; you do not need to round to a whole number.)

    Step 5 · Shortest notification path (D4)

    Using Appendix C's edge weights and the notification-cost rule (cost of an edge = 1 ÷ weight), compute the total notification cost of two candidate paths from Contact 47 to Contact 105, then say which is the faster (lower-cost) notification chain.

    The Trap

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion it lands as a specific beat, not something you read in advance.

    Reveal the trap

    Most Heroes compute one number first: weight eliminated per person vaccinated. On that number, Hub Sweep looks like a landslide.

    But Dr. Marsh isn't being handed a fixed number of syringes to point at whoever she likes. She's being handed a fixed dollar budget — and the two operations spend very different amounts per dose. The 50 and the 500 aren't independent choices; they're both just what that one shared budget happens to buy at each operation's own per-dose cost.

    Recompute the same comparison per dollar of the shared budget instead of per head, using Step 3's expected-value tool above at n_wide = 500 against your own D3 result for Hub Sweep's total weight eliminated (Appendix B gives you the weighted-degree sum and the internal-edge double-count correction you need to reach it yourself). The race looks very different once the denominator is the one thing that's actually fixed.
