## Overview

Priya Desai has the rack catalog open in one tab and the competitor's proposal open in the other,
and she has been toggling between them for twenty minutes. The Reyes family's south-facing roof is
still up on her second monitor, the drone photo she took herself, panels sketched over it in the
design software as translucent blue rectangles.

Her phone buzzes face-down on the desk. She doesn't turn it over yet.

The other installer's quote promises a bigger number on the cover page — more total annual
kilowatt-hours, printed bold, circled probably. Mrs. Reyes mentioned it on the phone yesterday,
not accusingly, just: *"Their sheet says more power. Yours says less. Why would we pay you more to
get less?"*

Priya has an answer. She is not sure yet that it is the *right* answer, which is a different
problem than not having one. Two tilt angles are open in the modeling tool, same roof, same panels,
same racking hardware — only the angle iron is set to a different number of degrees in each. One
of them wins on the number the competitor is printing. She has a hunch the other one wins on the
number that actually shows up on the Reyes family's utility bill each August, when the rate meter
tips into its most expensive hours of the year.

A hunch is not a bid. She pulls both models onto one screen, side by side, and reaches for the
angle iron spec sheet.

The phone buzzes again.

## Background

Priya Desai is a solar installation design engineer at Solterra Home Solar, a residential solar
contractor. She is responsible for the technical design behind every bid the sales team submits —
the panel layout, the racking configuration, and the projected annual production figure the
company puts its name behind. If that figure is wrong, it is Priya's design, not the salesperson's
pitch, that the homeowner remembers when the first year's bill arrives.

The proposed installation is a fixed, non-tracking rooftop array on the Reyes family's home. Both
options under consideration use the **identical number of panels, the identical panel model, and
the identical racking system** (SEE APPENDIX A) — the racking hardware permits the installed tilt
angle to be locked at any value within its supported range at the time of mounting, and cannot be
adjusted afterward without a service call. The two options differ in exactly one setting: the
angle at which the installer locks the rack before the panels go on.

**Latitude Lock** sets the array's fixed tilt angle equal to the site's latitude — the angle a
generation of solar-design rules of thumb call optimal, because it is the single fixed angle that
minimizes the sun's average angle of incidence across a full year and therefore maximizes total
annual energy collected (SEE APPENDICES A and D).

**Summer Shift** sets the array to a shallower fixed tilt angle, chosen so the panel surface sits
closer to perpendicular to the sun during the high-elevation summer months, at the cost of a worse
angle of incidence — and therefore lower output — during the low-elevation winter months (SEE
APPENDICES A and D).

Both options are modeled against the same published solar-production tool and the same site
location (SEE APPENDIX B), so the two proposals differ only in their month-by-month output curve,
never in panel count, panel wattage, or hardware cost. The racking system also imposes a hard
physical limit on how steep or shallow a tilt it can hold (SEE APPENDIX A); either option's angle
must fall inside that range or it is not buildable at all.

The homeowner's utility does not pay the same price for every kilowatt-hour: its published
residential time-of-use rate schedule charges a higher rate during summer on-peak hours than the
rest of the year (SEE APPENDIX C), the same schedule the Reyes family will actually be billed and
credited under. Priya's bid has to state a single production figure comparable to the competitor's,
and the two installers are not required to define that figure the same way — which is exactly what
has put two different "winning" numbers in front of the Reyes family. Where a figure here is not a
matter of public record, it is stated as an explicit modeling assumption (SEE APPENDIX E) rather
than guessed, and every sourced figure is listed in (SEE APPENDIX F). The vocabulary and "how to…"
skills this case asks you to research are listed in **APPENDIX G** — where Challenge 1 sends you.

## Dilemma

You are Priya Desai.
Do you:
A.) Recommend Latitude Lock — the fixed tilt angle that maximizes total annual energy collected OR
B.) Recommend Summer Shift — the shallower fixed tilt angle that favors the utility's highest-rate summer months
