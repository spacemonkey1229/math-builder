Overview

Priya Desai has the rack catalog open in one tab and the competitor's proposal open in the other, and she has been toggling between them for twenty minutes. The Reyes family's south-facing roof is still up on her second monitor, the drone photo she took herself, panels sketched over it in the design software as translucent blue rectangles.

Her phone buzzes face-down on the desk. She doesn't turn it over yet.

The other installer's quote promises a bigger number on the cover page — more total annual kilowatt-hours, printed bold, circled probably. Mrs. Reyes mentioned it on the phone yesterday, not accusingly, just: “Their sheet says more power. Yours says less. Why would we pay you more to get less?”

Priya has an answer. She is not sure yet that it is the right answer, which is a different problem than not having one. Two tilt angles are open in the modeling tool, same roof, same panels, same racking hardware — only the angle iron is set to a different number of degrees in each. One of them wins on the number the competitor is printing. She has a hunch the other one wins on the number that actually shows up on the Reyes family's utility bill each August, when the rate meter tips into its most expensive hours of the year.

A hunch is not a bid. She pulls both models onto one screen, side by side, and reaches for the angle iron spec sheet.

The phone buzzes again.

Background

Priya Desai is a solar installation design engineer at Solterra Home Solar, a residential solar contractor. She is responsible for the technical design behind every bid the sales team submits — the panel layout, the racking configuration, and the projected annual production figure the company puts its name behind. If that figure is wrong, it is Priya's design, not the salesperson's pitch, that the homeowner remembers when the first year's bill arrives.

The proposed installation is a fixed, non-tracking rooftop array on the Reyes family's home. Both options under consideration use the identical number of panels, the identical panel model, and the identical racking system (see Appendix A) — the racking hardware permits the installed tilt angle to be locked at any value within its supported range at the time of mounting, and cannot be adjusted afterward without a service call. The two options differ in exactly one setting: the angle at which the installer locks the rack before the panels go on.

Latitude Lock

Sets the array's fixed tilt angle equal to the site's latitude — the angle a generation of solar-design rules of thumb call optimal, because it is the single fixed angle that minimizes the sun's average angle of incidence across a full year and therefore maximizes total annual energy collected (see Appendices A and D).

Summer Shift

Sets the array to a shallower fixed tilt angle, chosen so the panel surface sits closer to perpendicular to the sun during the high-elevation summer months, at the cost of a worse angle of incidence — and therefore lower output — during the low-elevation winter months (see Appendices A and D).

Both options are modeled against the same published solar-production tool and the same site location (see Appendix B), so the two proposals differ only in their month-by-month output curve, never in panel count, panel wattage, or hardware cost. The racking system also imposes a hard physical limit on how steep or shallow a tilt it can hold (see Appendix A); either option's angle must fall inside that range or it is not buildable at all.

The homeowner's utility does not pay the same price for every kilowatt-hour: its published residential time-of-use rate schedule charges a higher rate during summer on-peak hours than the rest of the year (see Appendix C), the same schedule the Reyes family will actually be billed and credited under. Priya's bid has to state a single production figure comparable to the competitor's, and the two installers are not required to define that figure the same way — which is exactly what has put two different “winning” numbers in front of the Reyes family. Where a figure here is not a matter of public record, it is stated as an explicit modeling assumption (see Appendix E) rather than guessed, and every sourced figure is listed in Appendix F. The vocabulary and “how to…” skills this case asks you to research are listed in Appendix G — where Challenge 1 sends you.

All figures in this case are sourced (Appendix F) or stated as a modeling assumption (Appendix E).

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 · Degrees-to-radians conversion (D1)

    Pick a representative date and convert its solar-noon elevation angle from degrees to radians: radians = degrees × (π ÷ 180).

    Worked example: multiply the Spring equinox's 51.5° solar-noon elevation by (π ÷ 180) to convert it to radians. Use that same formula and arithmetic for whichever date you picked above.

    Step 2 · Solar-position table (D2)

    Pick a sun position from Appendix D. Convert its published azimuth (clockwise from true north) into a standard-position angle (counterclockwise from due east): standard angle = (90° − azimuth), adjusted so it falls within a single full turn. Then identify the reference angle and find sine, cosine, and tangent of the standard-position angle using the unit circle.

    Reasoning template: find the reference angle from the standard-position angle's quadrant, look up sine/cosine on the unit circle for that reference angle, then apply the sign the quadrant calls for (positive/negative) before reporting sine, cosine, and tangent of the full standard-position angle.

    Step 3 · Amplitude & period of the year-long model (D3)

    Using Appendix D's twelve mid-month solar-noon elevation values, identify the sinusoidal model's amplitude (half the distance from crest to trough) and period (the time to complete one full cycle).

    Step 4a · Angle-of-incidence check (D4)

    For a south-facing fixed tilt β at solar noon, the angle of incidence between the sun's rays and the panel's normal is θ = |90° − elevation − β|, and relative efficiency = cos(θ). Compute both for June and December, both tilt options, using Appendix D's mid-month elevations (June 74.8°, December 28.1°) and Appendix A's tilt angles.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    “Appendix C's non-summer rate — $0.1285/kWh — is the off-peak non-summer rate. SMUD's own rate page also publishes a non-summer on-peak rate: $0.1776/kWh, for the same 5–8pm window the summer on-peak rate applies to, just charged less because it's not summer. Appendix C picked the off-peak figure because it's what most non-summer hours are actually billed at — but if the Reyes family runs their evening routine (dinner, laundry, the dishwasher) inside that 5–8pm window year-round, not just in summer, a meaningful share of their non-summer usage is actually billed at $0.1776, not $0.1285.”

    “If the non-summer rate you should be weighting against is really $0.1776, not $0.1285 — does your recommendation change?”
