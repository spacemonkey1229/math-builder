# Case Study: Payroll

MS | Math | Case Study | 2026 | Heroes Academy

## Overview

Francesca Molinari stops at the corner before she reaches her own front door. Grampoline's sign — hand-painted the week she opened, gold letters over navy blue — catches the first light of the morning. She doesn't go in yet. In her hand is a paper cup of coffee she made at home instead of buying, and tucked in her back pocket is her phone, still open to the spreadsheet she fell asleep looking at last night.

Inside, her team is due in an hour: Martha at the front desk, Paul on the sales floor, Christina working the register, Tiago at his design bench, Irene already posting to the store's social accounts before she's even parked her car. Francesca has watched every one of them build a life around this storefront. She has built her own life around it too — longer than most marriages last.

Her phone buzzes again. It's the same message as yesterday: the number the bookkeeper needs to see by month's end, the number that doesn't move no matter how many times Francesca re-checks the math. She has already stopped taking her own paycheck. She has already called every vendor, every landlord, every lender who might give her more time. None of them can close the gap that's left.

There is exactly one lever left to pull, and it runs straight through the people standing on the other side of that door. Francesca finishes the coffee, tucks the phone away, and reaches for her keys.

## Background

Francesca Molinari founded Grampoline LLC in 2011 after recognizing an underserved market — exercise equipment designed for older adults — and opened a storefront in Orlando, Florida. She grew the company one hire at a time over the following years, eventually building a team of five employees who handle accounting and operations, sales, product design, and marketing (SEE APPENDIX A). For most of that history, revenue grew every year.

Over the past two years, new online competitors have taken market share with lower prices and a wider online reach, and Grampoline's revenue has fallen. The company's own year-by-year financial history — revenue, expenses, payroll, and profit, dating back to its founding — shows the trend plainly (SEE APPENDIX B). Francesca has already taken every non-payroll cost-cutting step available to her, and has gone without her own paycheck for the past month just to help cover rent.

To close its 2019 fiscal year without carrying a shortfall into the following year, Francesca must remove **$20,000** from the payroll still owed to her five employees for the rest of 2019 (SEE APPENDIX C). If whatever she chooses does not fully close that $20,000 gap, the difference becomes a second, unplanned round of cuts before the year is out — Francesca does not want to make this decision twice.

The exact figures used throughout this case, and the modeling conventions applied to them, are fixed in **APPENDIX D** — use those conventions, or your numbers won't match anyone else's.

Two paths are open to her. **Operation Clean Break** closes the shortfall by ending one employee's position entirely: their full remaining 2019 pay comes off the books at once, while the other four keep every dollar they were counting on. **Operation Shared Weight** closes the same shortfall by cutting every remaining employee's pay by the same percentage: no one loses their job, but everyone's paycheck gets smaller for the rest of the year. Each of Grampoline's five employees has their own history, role, and recent performance at the company (SEE APPENDIX A).

---

## Dilemma

You are Francesca.

Do you:

**A.)** Pursue Operation Clean Break — let one employee go, closing the $20,000 shortfall in a single cut **OR**

**B.)** Pursue Operation Shared Weight — cut every remaining employee's pay by the same percentage, closing the same $20,000 shortfall

## Facilitator-Only: Balance Argument (do not share with students before discussion)

**Argument for A (Operation Clean Break).** Letting go of either Tiago or Irene removes exactly $20,000 from the remaining payroll budget in a single, clean cut — the target is closed exactly, on the first try, with zero risk of a second round of cuts from a rounding error. The other four employees keep 100% of the pay they were counting on for the rest of the year. Martha, in particular, is already stretched thin caring for her niece; a pay cut on top of that would be a second hardship layered onto a family already in crisis.

**Argument for B (Operation Shared Weight).** A 17.94% pay cut, applied to all five employees, closes the same $20,000 gap without anyone losing their job or their health insurance, their identity as "the person who does X at Grampoline," or their income entirely. It spreads a real hardship five ways instead of concentrating 100% of it onto one person and their household. No single employee — including Tiago, two months into fatherhood, or Irene, who just signed a year-long lease she can't easily break — is asked to absorb the whole shock alone.

**The margin.** Both options are constructed to close the identical $20,000 target (Operation Clean Break via Tiago or Irene's exact remaining pay; Operation Shared Weight via 17.94% applied to the $111,500 payroll pool). `margin_pct = 0.0` — the two options are, by design, financially identical on the graded primary metric. The real tension this case forces is not "which option saves more money" (they don't differ), but who bears a $20,000 hit and how concentrated that hit is: one household absorbing all of it, or five households absorbing a slice each. See `case.json.balance` for the full computed figures.

## The Twist (facilitator delivers at ~15 minutes)

Francesca's phone rings. It's Grampoline's largest customer — a regional chain of senior-living communities — calling to say how thrilled they are with Irene's latest social media posts, which drove a wave of new signups at their newest location. "I had no idea Irene was this good," the customer says. "Honestly, she's a big part of why we keep coming back to Grampoline instead of the bigger guys."

**Facilitator-only, disclosed scenario assumption (not a real, sourced figure — see `case.json.modeling_assumptions`):** assume this customer's account represents 15% of Grampoline's 2019 H1 revenue ($127,000 × 15% = **$19,050**), and that losing Irene puts that account at real risk of leaving within the following year.

If a Hero has picked Operation Clean Break *and specifically chosen to let Irene go*, ask them to recompute: the $20,000 payroll saved this year is nearly wiped out by $19,050 of revenue put at risk next year — a net benefit of only $950, not $20,000. This does not make Operation Clean Break wrong (Tiago is untouched by this twist, and still closes the gap cleanly), and it does not make Operation Shared Weight automatically right (it still cuts five real paychecks). It invalidates exactly one popular reasoning path — "let go of the newest, least-tenured employee" — without resolving the case.

**Twist recompute, if Irene is the Clean Break choice.** Payroll saved is $20,000; revenue put at risk (Modeling Assumption MA3) is $19,050; net first-year benefit is $20,000 − $19,050 = **$950**, meaning 95.25% of the "savings" disappears once the customer-relationship risk is priced in.

Ask: "Does this change your position — or does it just mean if you pick Operation Clean Break, you pick Tiago instead of Irene?"
