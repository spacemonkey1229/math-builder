Overview

Francesca Molinari stops at the corner before she reaches her own front door. Grampoline's sign — hand-painted the week she opened, gold letters over navy blue — catches the first light of the morning. She doesn't go in yet. In her hand is a paper cup of coffee she made at home instead of buying, and tucked in her back pocket is her phone, still open to the spreadsheet she fell asleep looking at last night.

Inside, her team is due in an hour: Martha at the front desk, Paul on the sales floor, Christina working the register, Tiago at his design bench, Irene already posting to the store's social accounts before she's even parked her car. Francesca has watched every one of them build a life around this storefront. She has built her own life around it too — longer than most marriages last.

Her phone buzzes again. It's the same message as yesterday: the number the bookkeeper needs to see by month's end, the number that doesn't move no matter how many times Francesca re-checks the math. She has already stopped taking her own paycheck. She has already called every vendor, every landlord, every lender who might give her more time. None of them can close the gap that's left.

There is exactly one lever left to pull, and it runs straight through the people standing on the other side of that door. Francesca finishes the coffee, tucks the phone away, and reaches for her keys.

Background

Francesca Molinari founded Grampoline LLC in 2011 after recognizing an underserved market — exercise equipment designed for older adults — and opened a storefront in Orlando, Florida. She grew the company one hire at a time over the following years, eventually building a team of five employees who handle accounting and operations, sales, product design, and marketing (see Appendix A). For most of that history, revenue grew every year.

Over the past two years, new online competitors have taken market share with lower prices and a wider online reach, and Grampoline's revenue has fallen. The company's own year-by-year financial history — revenue, expenses, payroll, and profit, dating back to its founding — shows the trend plainly (see Appendix B). Francesca has already taken every non-payroll cost-cutting step available to her, and has gone without her own paycheck for the past month just to help cover rent.

To close its 2019 fiscal year without carrying a shortfall into the following year, Francesca must remove $20,000 from the payroll still owed to her five employees for the rest of 2019 (see Appendix C). If whatever she chooses does not fully close that $20,000 gap, the difference becomes a second, unplanned round of cuts before the year is out — Francesca does not want to make this decision twice.

Two paths are open to her. Operation Clean Break closes the shortfall by ending one employee's position entirely: their full remaining 2019 pay comes off the books at once, while the other four keep every dollar they were counting on. Operation Shared Weight closes the same shortfall by cutting every remaining employee's pay by the same percentage: no one loses their job, but everyone's paycheck gets smaller for the rest of the year. Each of Grampoline's five employees has their own history, role, and recent performance at the company (see Appendix A).

Appendix A — Grampoline's Team

Francesca Molinari, 45. Founder and owner. Grew up working in her family's Italian restaurant in Boston, which she later ran herself until it closed in 2010. Started Grampoline the following year and ran it alone out of her garage for six months before opening the storefront. Has not taken a paycheck for the past month.

Martha, 56. Hired in 2012 as Grampoline's first employee — manages accounting, the supply chain, and customer orders. Formerly a school counselor for 22 years before a district furlough. Since December, has been the primary caregiver for her 15-year-old niece, who was injured in the car accident that killed Martha's sister; Martha now frequently leaves early or misses full days to care for her.

Paul Chio, 48. Hired in 2013 to run sales; helped nearly double revenue by 2015. In 2016 his role expanded to include customer support and training new hires, and his own sales targets have not been met in over a year since. Supporting his oldest daughter through her first year of college.

Christina Redding, 34. Hired in 2017 as a second sales rep, trained by Paul. Had no prior sales experience but matched Paul's targets within a year; by 2018, her sales made up 62% of the company's new business against Paul's 38%. This is the first full-time job she has ever held.

Tiago Richman, 28. Hired in 2017 to redesign Grampoline's core product line; his work helped grow 2017 revenue. Recently, competitors outside Florida have introduced feature-richer products that have cut into Grampoline's share. Became a father two months ago; his fiancee is still recovering from pregnancy complications.

Irene Villalobos, 23. Hired in March 2019 as Grampoline's first social media manager. Sales rose 2% in April, 5% in May, and 8% in June compared with the same months the year before, since she started. First-generation college graduate carrying $54,000 in student loan debt; just signed a year-long apartment lease near the storefront.

See Appendix B for the company's financial history and Appendix C for what each employee is still owed for the rest of 2019.
