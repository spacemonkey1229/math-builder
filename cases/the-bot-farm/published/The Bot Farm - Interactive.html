Overview

Aisha Bello has two browser tabs open, and only one of them is about to matter.

The left tab shows a graph she has stared at for twenty minutes: a tight, near-solid block of accounts, thick with connecting lines, all following and messaging each other in a pattern she has flagged a dozen times before — a bot ring, or something that looks exactly like one. The right tab shows a sparser graph, but underneath it a scrolling feed of raw posting timestamps that make her stomach drop every time she refreshes: accounts posting faster than anyone sitting at a keyboard plausibly could.

The countdown clock pinned to the top of Aisha's second monitor reads six hours. In six hours, Verve's biggest live event of the year goes up, and with it the single largest traffic spike Trust & Safety sees all year — real fans discovering the platform for the first time, riding the same trending tag as whatever is trying to ride it with them.

Aisha's team lead already gave her the rule tonight: one cluster, one suspension, before the surge hits. The abuse tooling can pull a cluster this size safely exactly once — anything past that needs a slower, human-reviewed rollout Verve does not have six hours for.

She has narrowed it to two clusters. She cannot suspend both.

Her cursor sits over the button for the first one, hovering.

Background

Aisha Bello is a Fraud Analyst on the Trust & Safety team at Verve, a mid-size social platform. Six hours from now, Verve's annual live showcase — the single highest-traffic weekend of its year — begins, and Trust & Safety's abuse-detection tooling has flagged two account clusters riding the early buzz around the event's trending tag (SEE APPENDIX A). Verve's incident policy lets Aisha recommend exactly one cluster for suspension before the surge; suspending a cluster this size safely, without a slower human-reviewed rollout Verve does not have time for tonight, can only be done once per incident (SEE APPENDIX A).

Ring Alpha is the first cluster: a set of accounts identified mainly by how densely they connect to each other — heavy mutual following and messaging, visible as a dense block once the connections are laid out in an adjacency matrix (SEE APPENDIX B). Dense mutual connectivity like this is a textbook bot-ring signature. It is also, real published social-network data confirms, close to what an ordinary tight-knit real friend group's own local connections typically look like (SEE APPENDIX D) — so a dense block by itself does not settle the question.

Wave Nine is the second cluster: accounts whose defining signal is posting volume, not connectivity — each one posting far more often than a real person typically could sustain, especially with the event's surge still hours away (SEE APPENDIX C, APPENDIX F). Wave Nine's accounts mostly interact with real users rather than each other, so their adjacency matrix is comparatively sparse (SEE APPENDIX C).

Real published bot-detection research gives both signals a track record: connectivity-based detection and volume-based detection are both established, real techniques, but neither performs identically to the other, and neither performs identically under ordinary traffic conditions and under a real traffic surge (SEE APPENDIX D). Aisha's actual decision has to weigh how many confirmed bots each cluster's suspension would remove against how many real Verve accounts would be wrongly caught in it (SEE APPENDIX E) — a fraction Verve's own event weekend makes more consequential than usual, since some of the accounts a wrong call could catch are the exact mid-size creators whose posts are about to carry Verve's highest-visibility traffic of the year. Full source citations for every figure in this case are in APPENDIX G.

Note: Verve is a scenario construction for this case — there is no real platform by this name. The full standards bridge for this case is in Appendix H.

    Step 1 — False positives and confirmed bots for one cluster (D3–D4)

    Using Appendix A's flagged-account count and Appendix E's modeled false-positive rate for a cluster, compute the number of false positives (flagged × FP rate) and confirmed bots (flagged × (1 − FP rate)).

    Step 2 — The primary metric: confirmed bots per false positive (D4–D5)

    For BOTH clusters, compute the ratio of confirmed bots to false positives. A higher ratio means fewer innocent accounts get suspended per confirmed bot removed.

    Notice which numbers from Appendix A cancel out of this ratio entirely. Does cluster size matter to your recommendation, or only the false-positive rate?
