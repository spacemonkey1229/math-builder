Overview

The index card is zip-tied to Dara Voss's handlebars, laminated against the rain, and by 9:00 p.m. on a Tuesday it is covered in Sharpie numbers nobody else would bother to write down. Four deliveries in the last forty minutes. Battery running low. A pothole on 9th Avenue she has now hit twice this month and needs to remember to route around.

She used to enter numbers into other people's spreadsheets for a living — three years at a claims office before it downsized her out — and the habit never left. Every Sunday night she totals the card: miles ridden, weeks on the job, what she paid Zoomo for the bike she's been renting since her first shift.

Her phone buzzes against the handlebar mount. It's the email she's been waiting for all week: she's been accepted into a paralegal certificate program, evenings and weekends, starting in three weeks — a set, fixed length she already knows by heart. She can keep dashing the whole way through it — the schedule works — but the acceptance email has a tuition deadline attached, and tuition deadlines make a person look hard at every other number in their life.

Dara pulls over under an awning, phone in one hand, the rain-spotted index card in the other, and opens the calculator app. Two numbers are about to tell her something the card alone can't: whether the smarter move, for as long as the certificate program runs, is to keep paying Zoomo every week — or to walk into the shop three blocks from her apartment and just buy the bike outright.

Background

Dara Voss delivers full-time for two food-delivery apps in New York City, riding an e-bike six days a week. She is not an employee of either app — she is an independent contractor who supplies her own transportation, the same as roughly 80,000 other app-based delivery workers citywide (SEE APPENDIX D). Every e-bike and battery she rides has to carry UL Certification to be used for app-based delivery in NYC as of a rule that took effect January 26, 2026 (SEE APPENDIX D) — a requirement both options in this case already satisfy.

Since her first shift, Dara has rented her e-bike week to week from Zoomo, a delivery-e-bike rental company. Zoomo's current Standard Plan costs $39 per week and includes the bike, a charger, a U-lock, GPS tracking, and — critically — full maintenance and wear-and-tear coverage (SEE APPENDIX A). Dara has never owned an e-bike before and has never had to budget for her own repairs.

The certificate-program acceptance email puts a fixed horizon on the question for the first time: Dara plans to keep dashing for 21 months (91 weeks) while she completes the program, and to stop once it's done (SEE APPENDIX C). That horizon — not "forever," not "one more week" — is what she has to plan against.

The Ownership Path. Buy a Rad Power Bikes RadRunner 3 Plus outright for a one-time price, then pay her own electricity and maintenance out of pocket for as long as she owns it (SEE APPENDICES A AND C).
The Subscription Path. Keep paying Zoomo's $39-per-week Standard Plan for the full 21 months, maintenance and the bike itself already included (SEE APPENDIX A).

Every figure in this case is either cited to a real, named source or disclosed as a stated Modeling Assumption (SEE APPENDICES C AND D) — never invented quietly. Appendix B carries a plan from a different e-bike rental company, Whizz, that Dara is not choosing between — real pricing, deliberately included so you have to decide for yourself whether it belongs in your answer.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Weekly cost functions (D1)

    Using Appendix A's rate components and Appendix C's assumptions, find each option's weekly rate (the slope) after the one-time purchase cost. Combine charging cost and maintenance/subscription into a single weekly figure.

    Step 2 — Break-even system (D2)

    Set C_A(w) = C_B(w) and solve for w. Round to a sensible whole number of weeks.

    Step 3 — Net-savings intercepts (D3)

    Define S(w) = C_B(w) − C_A(w). What is its x-intercept (in weeks)? What is S(0), its y-intercept (in dollars)?

    Step 4 — Read the distractor graph (D4)

    From Appendix B's graph of the Whizz Storm-2 plan, identify its slope and y-intercept.

    Why doesn't this plan belong in Dara's Ownership-vs-Subscription comparison? Answer in your pitch, not here.

    Step 5 — Worked example: how to graph two lines and mark an intersection (D5)

    Fictional numbers, not this case's data — a model for the technique only. Plan Mercury: y = 200 + 10x. Plan Vesta: y = 25x.

    The two lines cross exactly once — that crossing point is the break-even x-value, the same idea D5 asks you to find and mark for Dara's real two options.

    Step 6 — Total cost at Dara's horizon and the margin (D6)

    Evaluate both cost functions at Dara's 91-week horizon (Appendix C, MA4). Find the dollar difference, then the percent margin of the more expensive option.
