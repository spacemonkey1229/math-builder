Case Study: The Weekly Rate

MS | Math | Case Study | 2026-27 | Adventure

## Overview

The index card is zip-tied to Dara Voss's handlebars, laminated against the rain, and by 9:00 p.m. on a Tuesday it is covered in Sharpie numbers nobody else would bother to write down. Four deliveries in the last forty minutes. Battery running low. A pothole on 9th Avenue she has now hit twice this month and needs to remember to route around.

She used to enter numbers into other people's spreadsheets for a living — three years at a claims office before it downsized her out — and the habit never left. Every Sunday night she totals the card: miles ridden, weeks on the job, what she paid Zoomo for the bike she's been renting since her first shift.

Her phone buzzes against the handlebar mount. It's the email she's been waiting for all week: she's been accepted into a paralegal certificate program, evenings and weekends, starting in three weeks — a set, fixed length she already knows by heart. She can keep dashing the whole way through it — the schedule works — but the acceptance email has a tuition deadline attached, and tuition deadlines make a person look hard at every other number in their life.

Dara pulls over under an awning, phone in one hand, the rain-spotted index card in the other, and opens the calculator app. Two numbers are about to tell her something the card alone can't: whether the smarter move, for as long as the certificate program runs, is to keep paying Zoomo every week — or to walk into the shop three blocks from her apartment and just buy the bike outright.

---

## Background

Dara Voss delivers full-time for two food-delivery apps in New York City, riding an e-bike six days a week. She is not an employee of either app — she is an independent contractor who supplies her own transportation, the same as roughly 80,000 other app-based delivery workers citywide (SEE APPENDIX D) [S6]. Every e-bike and battery she rides has to carry UL Certification to be used for app-based delivery in NYC as of a rule that took effect January 26, 2026 (SEE APPENDIX D) [S6] — a requirement both options in this case already satisfy, since Dara only ever considers bikes from established, certified sellers.

Since her first shift, Dara has rented her e-bike week to week from **Zoomo**, a delivery-e-bike rental company. Zoomo's current Standard Plan costs **$39 per week** and includes the bike, a charger, a U-lock, GPS tracking, and — critically — full maintenance and wear-and-tear coverage: if anything on the bike breaks from ordinary use, Zoomo fixes it at no extra charge (SEE APPENDIX A) [S1]. Dara has never owned an e-bike before and has never had to budget for her own repairs.

The certificate-program acceptance email puts a fixed horizon on the question for the first time: Dara plans to keep dashing for **21 months (91 weeks)** while she completes the program, and to stop dashing once it's done (SEE APPENDIX C). That horizon — not "forever," not "one more week" — is what she has to plan against.

Her two options:

**The Ownership Path.** Buy a Rad Power Bikes RadRunner 3 Plus outright from the shop near her apartment for a one-time price, then pay her own electricity to charge it and her own money for maintenance and repairs out of pocket for as long as she owns it (SEE APPENDICES A AND C).

**The Subscription Path.** Keep paying Zoomo's $39-per-week Standard Plan for the full 21 months, with maintenance, repairs, and the bike itself already included in that weekly price (SEE APPENDIX A).

(A labeled, not-to-scale illustration of the kind of step-through delivery e-bike both options put Dara on — battery, rear cargo rack, and callouts to both prices — is rendered in the published Interactive HTML, right after this section.)

Every figure in this case is either cited to a real, named source or disclosed as a stated Modeling Assumption (SEE APPENDICES C AND D) — never invented quietly. Appendix B carries a plan from a different e-bike rental company, Whizz, that Dara is **not** choosing between — real pricing, deliberately included so you have to decide for yourself whether it belongs in your answer. The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask you to research are listed in Appendix E — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

## Dilemma

You are Dara Voss.

Do you:

A.) Take **The Ownership Path** — buy the RadRunner 3 Plus outright now, and pay your own electricity and maintenance costs out of pocket for the next 21 months OR

B.) Take **The Subscription Path** — keep paying Zoomo's $39-per-week Standard Plan, maintenance and repairs fully included, for the same 21 months

## Challenges

1.) Research and find an online resource that answers one of the "how to…" questions from the case study launch. Post a link to the resource as your submission.

2.) Post 250 or more "excellent" words pitching a firm stance to your audience on which you would choose and why. Your pitch must include:
    a.) Use of the "how to…" resource you researched in Challenge 1.
    b.) At least one original visual display of the data.

3.) Your pitch must include:

D1. **A weekly cost-function derivation.** Using the itemized rate components in Appendix A, write each option's total cost as a linear equation in simplified slope-intercept form, as a function of the number of weeks, *w*: `C(w) = mw + b`. Show the distributive-property step that combines Option A's separate per-week charges into a single weekly rate, and show any like terms you combine along the way. *Why this step first:* each option needs its own independent, comparable cost function before you can compare them at all — you can't ask which is cheaper until both are expressed the same way.

D2. **A break-even system.** Set the two cost functions from D1 equal to each other as a system of two linear equations, and solve algebraically for the break-even number of weeks — the point where both options have cost Dara the same total amount. Round your answer to a sensible whole number of weeks and show your work. *Why this matters:* the break-even week is the pivot point of the whole decision — before it one option is ahead, after it the other is, and knowing which side of that week Dara's own horizon falls on is what actually drives a recommendation.

D3. **A net-savings x-intercept.** Define a net-savings function, `S(w)`, as Option B's cost function minus Option A's cost function, simplify it to a single weekly term (combining like terms), and solve algebraically for its x-intercept. In your own words, explain what the x-intercept represents in Dara's decision, and separately what `S(0)` — the y-intercept — represents. *Why this matters:* S(w)'s x-intercept should land on the same week as D2's break-even, found a second, independent way — a genuine check that both answers agree, not a repeat of the same math.

D4. **A distractor read.** Appendix B graphs a real weekly-plus-deposit pricing plan from Whizz, a different e-bike rental company Dara is not actually choosing between. From the graph, identify its slope and y-intercept, write its linear equation, and explain in 2–3 sentences why this plan is not actually relevant to Dara's Ownership-vs-Subscription decision. *Why this matters:* not every real number in an appendix belongs in your model — Whizz's plan is the same shape as the two real options, so reading it correctly and then explaining why it still doesn't belong is the actual skill being tested, not just running the numbers.

D5. **A graph of both real options.** On one set of labeled axes, graph Option A's and Option B's cost functions from D1 for *w* = 0 to at least 130 weeks. Mark and label the break-even intersection point from D2, and mark which option is cheaper on each side of it. *Why this matters:* a graph makes the break-even point visible instead of only algebraic — where your two lines actually cross, and which sits lower before versus after, is a sanity check on your D2 answer.

D6. **A total-cost-at-horizon comparison.** Evaluate both cost functions at Dara's actual 21-month (91-week) horizon, find the dollar difference between the two totals, and convert that difference to a percent margin of the more expensive option. State which option you recommend and why. *Why this matters:* a raw dollar difference alone doesn't say how big that gap really is relative to what's being spent — a percent margin is what lets this decision's stakes be compared to any other percent-based decision.
