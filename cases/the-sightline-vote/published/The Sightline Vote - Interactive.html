Overview

Dana Whitcombe kneels on the shoulder of Birch Hollow Road, sketching the final skid mark into a police diagram while a tow truck winches a crushed sedan onto its flatbed.

This is the second car she has watched get T-boned at this exact corner in a month — same blind approach, same crumpled driver's door, same statement from the other driver: “I never saw it coming until it was already there.” The smell of hot brake dust still hangs over the intersection.

Dana is the city's traffic engineer. She flagged this corner in a memo two years ago — Kellner Avenue and Birch Hollow Road have never met at a square angle, not since the road was laid out along an old farm-lot line generations before either street had a name — and the memo went into a budget queue that never got funded. Now the local paper has run both crashes on its front page, under the same headline font, eleven days apart.

Her phone buzzes face-down on the hood of her city truck. It's her department head: the council has put emergency funding for the intersection on the agenda, and they vote in ten days — one option, whichever one Dana recommends, no time to build a mockup of either first. Two contractors have already called, each swearing their own fix clears the state's minimum sightline standard. Neither number has actually been checked.

Dana pulls two folders out of her truck — one thick with land-purchase paperwork, one thin and ready to break ground next week — and opens both on the hood, next to the chalk outline of where the Civic used to be.

Background

Dana Whitcombe is the City of Caldwell Falls's sole traffic engineer, responsible for signing off on any intersection redesign before public works can put it out to bid. She reports directly to the city council, and her professional judgment is the only technical review this project will get before the vote.

Kellner Avenue (the four-lane through street) and Birch Hollow Road (the two-lane residential street) cross at a skewed angle — the two roads have never met at 90 degrees, because Birch Hollow was originally laid out along an 1880s farm-lot boundary that predates the street grid (see Appendix A). A driver stopped on Birch Hollow has to turn sharply in the seat to see traffic coming down Kellner, and that skew is the specific defect named in both crash reports.

Two fixes are on the table, and only two — the council will fund exactly one of them before the vote closes:

The Squared Corner reconstructs the intersection so Kellner Avenue and Birch Hollow Road cross at a true 90-degree angle. This is the redesign both crash reports recommend outright, and it requires the city to first buy two narrow slivers of the northeast and southeast corner lots — land the city does not currently own — before public works can even start moving curb (see Appendix C).

The Offset Bypass adds a short new connector lane rather than rebuilding the corner itself. The new lane is built parallel to the platted 1880s lot line that already runs along the adjoining parcel (see Appendix A) — which means public works can build entirely inside the city's existing right-of-way, with no land purchase and no title work, starting as soon as a crew is free (see Appendix C).

Both contractors claim their design clears the state's minimum sightline-safety angle for a public intersection (see Appendix B), a standard neither number has actually been checked against. The case's own sightline-safety scoring method, and the real research behind why intersection angle matters for crash risk at all, are in Appendix B. All figures in this case are sourced in Appendix E, or stated as an explicit modeling assumption in Appendix D where no public figure exists for this specific, one-of-a-kind intersection. The vocabulary and the “how to…” skills this case will ask of you are listed in Appendix G — that is where Challenge 1 sends you. Appendix B — Sightline Safety Score

Real research backs why intersection angle matters for crash risk at all: FHWA's own research report Impact of Intersection Angle on Highway Safety [S2] found a measurable, quantifiable relationship between an intersection's angle and its crash rate, and recommended agencies revisit the "critical minimum angle" policies they design to. Separately, California's own Highway Design Manual [S1] sets a real, binding minimum: an intersection's interior angle "should not be less than 75 degrees."

This case scores each redesign's sightline safety on a 0–1 scale defined as:

Safety score = sin(intersection angle), where "intersection angle" is the acute angle formed where the two roads cross, and the angle is measured in degrees.

A full 90-degree, perfectly perpendicular crossing scores a perfect 1.0. This is a modeling simplification of the real relationship named above (see Appendix D, MA3) — not FHWA's own crash-modification formula, which this case does not reproduce.

The state's minimum sightline-safety standard both contractors claim to clear is 75 degrees [S1] — the same real state design-manual standard named above.
