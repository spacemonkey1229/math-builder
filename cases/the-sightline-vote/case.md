Case Study: The Sightline Vote
MS | Math | Case Study | 2026-27 | Heroes Academy

## Overview

Dana Whitcombe kneels on the shoulder of Birch Hollow Road, sketching the final skid mark into
a police diagram while a tow truck winches a crushed sedan onto its flatbed. This is the second
car she has watched get T-boned at this exact corner in a month — same blind approach, same
crumpled driver's door, same statement from the other driver: "I never saw it coming until it was
already there." The smell of hot brake dust still hangs over the intersection.

Dana is the city's traffic engineer. She flagged this corner in a memo two years ago — Kellner
Avenue and Birch Hollow Road have never met at a square angle, not since the road was laid out
along an old farm-lot line generations before either street had a name — and the memo went into a
budget queue that never got funded. Now the local paper has run both crashes on its front page,
under the same headline font, eleven days apart.

Her phone buzzes face-down on the hood of her city truck. It's her department head: the council
has put emergency funding for the intersection on the agenda, and they vote in ten days — one
option, whichever one Dana recommends, no time to build a mockup of either first. Two contractors
have already called, each swearing their own fix clears the state's minimum sightline standard.
Neither number has actually been checked.

Dana pulls two folders out of her truck — one thick with land-purchase paperwork, one thin and
ready to break ground next week — and opens both on the hood, next to the chalk outline of where
the Civic used to be.

## Background

Dana Whitcombe is the City of Caldwell Falls's sole traffic engineer, responsible for signing off
on any intersection redesign before public works can put it out to bid. She reports directly to
the city council, and her professional judgment is the only technical review this project will get
before the vote.

Kellner Avenue (the four-lane through street) and Birch Hollow Road (the two-lane residential
street) cross at a **skewed** angle — the two roads have never met at 90 degrees, because Birch
Hollow was originally laid out along an 1880s farm-lot boundary that predates the street grid (SEE
APPENDIX A). A driver stopped on Birch Hollow has to turn sharply in the seat to see traffic
coming down Kellner, and that skew is the specific defect named in both crash reports.

Two fixes are on the table, and only two — the council will fund exactly one of them before the
vote closes:

**The Squared Corner** reconstructs the intersection so Kellner Avenue and Birch Hollow Road cross
at a true 90-degree angle. This is the redesign both crash reports recommend outright, and it
requires the city to first buy two narrow slivers of the northeast and southeast corner lots — land
the city does not currently own — before public works can even start moving curb (SEE APPENDIX C).

**The Offset Bypass** adds a short new connector lane rather than rebuilding the corner itself. The
new lane is built parallel to the platted 1880s lot line that already runs along the adjoining
parcel (SEE APPENDIX A) — which means public works can build entirely inside the city's existing
right-of-way, with no land purchase and no title work, starting as soon as a crew is free (SEE
APPENDIX C).

Both contractors claim their design clears the state's minimum sightline-safety angle for a public
intersection (SEE APPENDIX B), a standard neither number has actually been checked against. The
case's own sightline-safety scoring method, and the real research behind why intersection angle
matters for crash risk at all, are in Appendix B. All figures in this case are sourced in APPENDIX E, or stated as an explicit modeling assumption in APPENDIX D where no public figure exists for
this specific, one-of-a-kind intersection. The vocabulary and the "how to…" skills this case will
ask of you are listed in APPENDIX G — that is where Challenge 1 sends you. (SEE APPENDIX F for
further reading on intersection geometry and sight-distance design.)

## Dilemma

You are Dana Whitcombe.
Do you:
A.) Recommend The Squared Corner — buy the two corner-lot slivers and reconstruct the intersection
at a full 90-degree angle OR
B.) Recommend The Offset Bypass — build the new connector lane parallel to the existing platted lot
line, at the intersection's existing skew angle, with no land purchase

## Appendix A — Intersection Geometry Reference

Dana's own site survey for this intersection — not a public GIS record (the city's as-built survey
is held internally pending the redesign), but built entirely from real, standard intersection
geometry.

| | Value |
|---|---|
| Kellner Avenue (major street) width | 36 ft, two lanes each direction |
| Birch Hollow Road (minor street) width | 30 ft, two lanes |
| Current intersection angle, acute pair | 50° |
| Current intersection angle, obtuse pair | 130° |
| Platted 1880s lot line angle to Kellner Avenue (given angle for The Offset Bypass) | 75° |
| ADA curb ramp angle to the Kellner Avenue curb, northeast corner (The Squared Corner design) | 35° |
| Corner curb-return radius, both options | 25 ft |

The current 50°/130° skew and the 75° platted lot-line angle are two separate, independent facts
about this site: the lot line is a legal boundary that has existed since the 1880s survey, while
the 50°/130° skew is simply how the two roads happen to cross today. The Offset Bypass's own
resulting angle (D4) is *derived from* the lot line's 75°, not from the current 50°/130° skew.

## Appendix B — Sightline Safety Score

Real research backs why intersection angle matters for crash risk at all: FHWA's own research
report *Impact of Intersection Angle on Highway Safety* [S2] found a measurable, quantifiable
relationship between an intersection's angle and its crash rate, and recommended agencies revisit
the "critical minimum angle" policies they design to. Separately, California's own Highway Design
Manual [S1] sets a real, binding minimum: an intersection's interior angle "should not be less than
75 degrees."

This case scores each redesign's sightline safety on a 0–1 scale defined as:

> **Safety score = sin(intersection angle)**, where "intersection angle" is the acute angle formed
> where the two roads cross, and the angle is measured in degrees.

A full 90-degree, perfectly perpendicular crossing scores a perfect **1.0**. This is a modeling
simplification of the real relationship named above (SEE APPENDIX D, MA3) — not FHWA's own
crash-modification formula, which this case does not reproduce.

The state's minimum sightline-safety standard both contractors claim to clear is **75 degrees**
[S1] — the same real state design-manual standard named above.
