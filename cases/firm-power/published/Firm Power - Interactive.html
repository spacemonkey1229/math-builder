Overview

Priya Anand has the board packet open on the conference table, untouched, because she's still staring at the wall monitor instead. It's showing tonight's live system curve — the same shape it makes every single evening, a low shallow bowl all afternoon that suddenly rears up into a spike right around dinnertime and holds there for hours before it finally lets go. She has watched that exact shape happen enough times now that she doesn't need the monitor to know when it's coming. What she needs is a number.

Two folders sit in front of her, each one a signed-ready capacity contract her legal team finalized this week. One locks in a gas plant. The other locks in solar paired with batteries, plus a second charge nobody outside this industry has ever heard of, that exists specifically to make an intermittent resource promise to show up anyway. Both contracts cost real money the district doesn't get to spend twice. Both get voted on Thursday.

Her intern taps on the glass door, holds up two fingers — two minutes until the pre-board call. Priya doesn't look away from the monitor. The spike is already climbing.

She pulls both folders toward her.

Background

Priya Anand is the resource adequacy planner for Solano Bay Community Power, a community choice aggregatorA real kind of public agency California law allows a city or group of cities to form: it buys and contracts electricity supply on residents' behalf while the existing utility still delivers it over its own wires.Open a reference on this → (SEE APPENDIX F) that buys and contracts electricity supply on behalf of its member cities within the California ISO (CAISO) grid. Her job is to recommend, to the district's board, exactly one new capacity contract that closes the gap between what Solano Bay already has under contract and what its customers actually demand during the hours that matter most — and to defend that recommendation with real numbers, not a general sense of which technology "feels" more modern.

Two contracts are on the table, and Priya has already nicknamed them the way the board will hear them read into the record:

  • The Peaker Contract — new natural-gas peaking capacity, fully dispatchable on demand at any hour.
  • The Firmed Solar Contract — solar-plus-storage capacity, paired with a real, published capacity-accreditation charge (SEE APPENDIX D) that pays to bring its reliability up to the same standard as a gas plant's.

Solano Bay's own load is a small, stated share of CAISO's total system load (SEE APPENDIX E). CAISO itself publishes its real, system-wide hourly demand data (SEE APPENDIX A) — and Priya's district's own demand rises and falls with that same real system-wide curve, following the well-documented "duck curve"The evening-peak shape California grid operators have publicly documented for over a decade: solar output drops off right as residential demand rises, stacking two effects into one sharp climb.Open a reference on this → pattern, because Solano Bay's customers are part of the same grid, subject to the same heat and the same evening routines as everyone else on it.

Whichever contract Priya recommends has to cover Solano Bay's own peak — the hours each evening when demand climbs above what the district's existing contracts already reliably cover (SEE APPENDIX A, APPENDIX B). Both technologies price out in levelized costA standard way to compare different power sources on the same basis: the average total lifetime cost of a resource divided by the total energy it's expected to produce, in dollars per megawatt-hour.Open a reference on this → dollars per megawatt-hour, but only once solar-plus-storage's own raw cost is combined with the real firming chargeA real, published capacity payment (this case's own Appendix D) that pays a fully-dispatchable backstop resource to cover the gap between an intermittent resource's real reliability score and 100%.Open a reference on this → that brings it to full reliability (SEE APPENDIX C, APPENDIX D) — comparing solar's cost alone against gas would not be a fair fight, and Priya knows the board will catch it if she tries.

The board also authorized a fixed monthly budget for this specific contract tranche (SEE APPENDIX E) — not enough, on its own, to fully explain either contract, but enough to be a real constraint Priya has to reason through.

CAISO's real curve for this day is a shallow bowl through the morning and early afternoon, then a sharp evening climb, matching the well-documented "duck curve" pattern California's grid operators discuss publicly: solar generation drops off in the evening at the same time residential demand rises, and the two effects stack. Appendix D — Firmed Solar: Levelized Cost Plus Firming Charge

Grid operators including CAISO use a real, published measure called effective load-carrying capability (ELCC) to score how much of an intermittent resource's nameplate capacity can be counted on during the hours of highest system need — computed from historical performance: how often, in practice, a resource like this one was actually still producing power during the system's worst hours. A resource with ELCC below 100% has to be "firmed" — its contract requires the developer to also pay a real, published monthly capacity charge ("Net CONE," below) to a separate, fully-dispatchable backstop resource (in practice, usually an existing gas plant with spare capacity), so that if the batteries run low during an extended peak, that backup resource is contractually obligated to cover the shortfall. Concretely, in three steps: (1) Solano Bay pays the Net CONE rate every month to whichever backstop plant CAISO designates, whether or not that plant ever actually runs; (2) that standing payment is what obligates the backstop plant to be available and ready; (3) if an evening ever comes when the batteries run down before demand does, CAISO calls on that already-paid-for backstop plant to physically generate the remaining power, the same way an insurance premium is paid every month whether or not a claim is ever filed. It is not solar-plus-storage getting physically better at showing up — it is the contract paying someone else to guarantee it, the same way a band might pay a backup singer who almost never has to sing, just to guarantee the show never gets cancelled. This lets the combined package reach the same reliability as a plant like a gas peaker, which is scored at essentially 100% on its own and needs no such backup payment.

Appendix F — What a Community Choice Aggregator Is

A community choice aggregator (CCA) is a real kind of public agency that California law allows a city or group of cities to form. The split works like this: the CCA (Solano Bay, here) signs the actual supply contracts and decides where the electrons come from — a gas plant, a solar farm, a battery. The existing investor-owned utility (in Solano Bay's real-world region, this would be Pacific Gas & Electric) keeps owning and running the physical wires, poles, and meters, and keeps billing and repairing outages, exactly as before. A household doesn't notice the switch in daily life; the change happens entirely in who is contractually responsible for lining up the power supply. Dozens of real CCAs currently operate across California, ranging from a few hundred thousand customers to several million — Solano Bay Community Power in this case is a fictional, mid-size example of a real kind of organization.
