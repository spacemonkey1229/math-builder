Overview

Priya Anand has the mold vendor on hold and a sample box in each hand.

Six weeks ago, Thistle & Fern Bath Co. got the email every small bath-and-body brand hopes for: a national retailer wants the company's mini fizzer bath bombs as a holiday gift-set add-on, and the buyer needs a packaging decision locked before the mold shop can start cutting. Priya has spent the last two days at her bench with a scale, a ruler, and two hand-built prototypes — one a small paper pyramid tied shut at the peak, the other a small paper cone tied shut the same way — each one holding a handful of the little round fizzers that gave the product its name.

She sets both prototypes down next to the stack of spec sheets she's built over the past two days and picks the pyramid back up, turning it over so the peak catches the light. Behind her, the production calendar on the wall has a red circle around a single date: the day the mold shop's quote locks in and the tooling gets cut. Whichever shape she signs off on today is the shape Thistle & Fern ships for the whole holiday run.

Her phone, still on hold, crackles.

Priya sets the pyramid down next to the cone and reaches for the phone.

Background

Priya Anand is the packaging engineer at Thistle & Fern Bath Co., a small bath-and-body brand that makes and sells its own bath bombs, soaps, and lotions. She is responsible for every container the company's products ship in, and she is the one who has to sign off before the company commits real money to a custom mold or die.

The product driving this decision is the company's Mini Fizzer — a small, round bath bomb about the size of a quarter (SEE APPENDIX B for its real, sourced diameter) — which Thistle & Fern sells in small gift boxes as an impulse add-on. A national retail buyer wants to carry the Mini Fizzer as a holiday gift-set item, and has asked Priya to lock in a single container shape before the company's mold shop can start cutting tooling for the season. Priya's team sketched several container shapes on the whiteboard before narrowing the field to the two she actually built prototypes of.

Option A — The Peak Box is a small square pyramid, cut and scored from a single flat sheet of cardstock (SEE APPENDIX D for its real, sourced price) and tied shut at the peak with a short length of ribbon, the same way a party favor box is finished. Option B — The Twist Cone is a small cone, cut and scored from the same cardstock stock, rolled into shape, and twisted shut at the point the same way a candy cone is finished. Both options are cut from identical material at an identical price per square inch (SEE APPENDIX D) — so the only thing that can make one option cost more or less material per box is the geometry of the shape itself.

Both shapes are loose-filled with Mini Fizzers by hand on the packing line — nobody arranges the little spheres one at a time, they're poured in and the box is closed — so neither box's full interior volume actually fills with product; some fraction of that volume is always air between the spheres (SEE APPENDIX C for the real, sourced figure this case uses for that fraction).

Priya's mold shop can only cut tooling for one shape before the season, and there is no time to change course once the order is placed (SEE APPENDIX E). The buyer has not specified case-pack counts for the shipping cartons yet, but Priya knows from experience that a shape that wastes space inside a standard shipping carton (SEE APPENDIX F) costs the company in freight even when it wins on the price of the box itself — so she has pulled that number too, even though it isn't the number the buyer's contract is actually priced on. All figures in this case are sourced in Appendix G, or stated as an explicit modeling assumption in Appendix E where no public figure exists for a decision this specific to one company. Appendices

Appendix A — Whiteboard Log: Shapes Considered

Priya's team sketched several shapes before narrowing the field to Options A and B. A cylinder (too tall to sit flat on a retail shelf endcap at the size needed), a rectangular prism (too much dead corner space around round spheres), and a full sphere-shaped box (a mold shop quote came back more than four times the cost of either finalist, because a curved die is far more expensive to cut than a straight-line or cone die) were all ruled out at the whiteboard stage. Only the pyramid and the cone survived as real, buildable candidates at a defensible cost.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Surface Area (D1)

    Compute the total square inches of cardstock each container's net uses.

    Step 2 — Interior Volume (D2)

    Compute the interior volume, in cubic inches, of each container using its own volume formula.

    Step 3 — Usable Capacity & Cost Per Fizzer (D3 / D4)

    Find a single Mini Fizzer's volume, apply Appendix C's 64% packing figure to each container's volume, and report the whole number of Mini Fizzers each one can actually hold — then divide each option's material cost by its count.

    Step 4 — Shipping-Carton Footprint (D5)

    Using Appendix F's carton, estimate how many of each box fit in one layer in a simple grid, and what percent of that layer's floor area each shape's footprint covers.
