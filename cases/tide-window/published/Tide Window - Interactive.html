Overview

The radio in Amara Osei's hand goes quiet the second the tug captain stops talking, and for a moment all she can hear is water sliding along the hull below her boots. She is standing on the bridge wing of the Solveig Trader, loaded and waiting in the dark, and the call in front of her is hers alone to make. Six months a licensed harbor pilot, and this is the first time no one senior is standing beside her.

Behind her, on the chart table, two tide curves are pinned side by side, and neither one is comfortable. The rain that soaked her jacket on the walk down to the pilot boat has stopped, but the barometer in the wheelhouse hasn't moved the way she'd like. Somewhere below the waterline, the Solveig Trader's keel is closer to the harbor bottom than either of the ship's own officers seem to appreciate.

The tug captain wants an answer before the current turns (reverses direction, the way a tidal current naturally does twice a day). The shipping line wants the berth kept on schedule. And Amara — whose name goes on the pilotage log either way — wants the arithmetic to be right before she says a word into the radio.

She looks at the chart, then at the clock, then at the chart again.

"Two options," the tug captain says. "Tell me which one."

Background

Amara Osei is a harbor pilot for the Port of Kesterhaven Pilots' Association, licensed six months ago after a multi-year apprenticeship. Once she boards a vessel and takes the conn for a transit, she is legally responsible for its safe passage through the harbor channel — the ship's own captain retains command, but the pilotage log carries her name and her judgment.

The Solveig Trader is a loaded cargo vessel under charter to a shipping line with a fixed delivery window at the container berth (see Appendix C). Two transit options are on the table tonight.

The Dawn Transit would move the ship out through the channel during the marginal low tide before sunrise, keeping the delivery on its contracted schedule. The tide prediction for the station (see Appendix A) shows the water level at that hour is low enough that the under-keel clearance — the gap between the ship's loaded draft and the charted channel depth (see Appendix B) — is real but thin.

The Slack Tide would hold the ship at berth until the next high tide, several hours later, when the clearance is comfortable under any reasonable reading of the data. It takes its name from slack water, the short stretch near high or low tide when the tidal current is at its weakest, just before it turns. It guarantees a safe transit but breaks the shipping line's contracted delivery window, triggering a penalty (see Appendix C).

Two complicating conditions apply to either choice. First, the harbor's tide station reports water level as a predicted curve, not a fixed number — the actual level at any given moment can run higher or lower than predicted under real barometric and wind conditions (see Appendix D). Second, the Pilots' Association's own safety manual sets a minimum acceptable under-keel clearance below which a pilot may not authorize a transit, regardless of schedule pressure (see Appendix B).

Amara has the tide predictions, the ship's draft, and the contract terms in front of her. She does not have another pilot to check her arithmetic against. Where a figure below is not a matter of public record, it is stated as an explicit modeling assumption (see Appendix E) rather than guessed.

Appendix D — Barometric & Storm-Surge Variability

A nearby storm can move the water level away from its predicted tide curve in two different, opposite-signed ways, and getting the Dawn Transit's clearance right depends on telling them apart.

Value
Inverse-barometer water-level effectLow air pressure lets the sea surface rise — roughly 1 cm of rise per 1 hPa (millibar) of pressure drop, ≈0.39 in per mb [S8]. This is the mechanism behind ordinary storm surge.
Wind-driven setdownA separate, opposite-signed effect: when a storm's wind blows away from the coast, it physically pushes water offshore, lowering the local water level below the predicted curve — "reverse storm surge." Real and documented: Tampa Bay's water level dropped roughly 4 ft below prediction from wind-driven setdown during Hurricane Ian (2022) [S10].
Local water-level effect modeled for a nearby-but-offshore systemSee Appendix E, MA3. Kesterhaven's exposure on this specific night is modeled as a net setdown, consistent with an offshore, non-landfalling system whose wind pushes water away from the harbor rather than piling it in — the specific magnitude is not knowable in advance.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Angle table (D1)

    Convert a key clock time into the tide model's angle, in degrees and radians, then find its reference angle and the sine/cosine/tangent of that reference angle using the unit circle. This notebook's model places the first Low tide (04:33) at 180° on a cosine-style tide curve, and advances at 360° per 12.42 h (the M2 period, Appendix A).

    Step 2 — Amplitude & period of the tide model (D2)

    State the amplitude and period you'll fit your sine/cosine model to, sourced directly from Appendix A's real M2 harmonic data.

    Your model: level(t) = MSL − amplitude·cos(angle(t)), where angle(t) is what you built in Step 1. This is an approximation of the real predicted curve in Appendix A — a pure two-constituent sinusoid won't land exactly on NOAA's published highs/lows, and that's expected, not an error.

    Step 3 — Under-keel clearance table (D3)

    Using Appendix A's real predicted water levels and Appendix B's real draft and governing depth (MA4), compute the achieved under-keel clearance for each option, pre-twist. Clearance = governing depth + predicted water level − draft.

    Step 4 — Sensitivity check (D4)

    Using Appendix D's wind-driven-setdown mechanism and Appendix E's MA3 assumption, compute the Dawn Transit's clearance after the setdown is applied, and state whether it still clears the Pilots' Association's policy floor (3.28 ft, Appendix B). Try the slider below to see how the answer moves as the setdown assumption changes — this is exactly what Tier 2 probe 5 checks mechanically against sensitivity_model.py.
