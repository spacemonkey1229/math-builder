Overview

The west wall of monitors is doing the thing it does every clear evening: six generation curves sliding down together, and one number in the corner ticking downward faster than any of the others can climb to meet it. Lena Marsh doesn’t look away from it while she answers the phone one-handed. “I see it,” she says. “I’m looking at the same slope you are.” On the desk beside her keyboard, a procurement folder sits open to a bid page she has read four times tonight, a sticky note wedged in the margin in her own handwriting: how fast, not just how much.

Tomorrow’s evening ramp is forecast to be worse than today’s — clearer skies, a little more solar on the system than last month, the same stubborn need for something else to pick up the slack in the exact minutes the sun gives up. Cascade Grid Authority’s capital committee wants her recommendation by morning, and the committee does not want to hear about total megawatts. They already know both bids on her desk deliver plenty of megawatts. What they are going to ask, in the first sixty seconds of tomorrow’s meeting, is whether either one can actually keep up — not over an hour, not on average, but at the single worst instant of the drop.

She minimizes the phone call and pulls two folders side by side: one full of battery discharge curves, one full of a turbine’s startup log. Somewhere in the gap between how fast a system can respond and how fast the sun actually sets is the answer the committee is going to hold her to.

Her cursor sits on the tab she hasn’t opened yet — the raw hourly generation data from tonight, the actual slope she’ll need to beat.

Background

Lena Marsh is a resource-planning dispatcher at Cascade Grid Authority, a balancing authority responsible for keeping electricity supply matched to demand, second by second, across its service territory. Her specific accountability is the evening net-load ramp: the window each clear day when the sun sets and solar generation falls away while electricity demand is still high, and the rest of the system’s dispatchable resources have to increase output, in real time, to replace exactly what the sun stops providing. Cascade Grid Authority is fictional; the evening ramp pattern this case asks Lena to analyze is modeled directly on real, published net-load ramp statistics from CAISO, the real balancing authority most associated with the “duck curve” (see Appendix D), because this evening ramp is a real, well-documented feature of solar-heavy grids and not a hypothetical one.

The capital committee has two vendor bids in front of it for the same job — covering the evening ramp — and it is evaluating them on the same basis: cost per unit of ramp-rate capability delivered, not total price tag (see Appendix A).

The Solar Stack pairs 200 MW of new utility-scale solar generation with a 4-hour battery energy storage system, sized to the same 200 MW of power for its full 4-hour discharge duration. A battery can, in principle, respond to any rate of change almost instantly — its limit is not speed, it is how many total megawatt-hours it holds before it runs out (see Appendix A).
The Peaker Call is a new 120 MW natural-gas peaker plant — a simple-cycle combustion turbine built specifically to start quickly and run only during high-demand hours. A gas peaker has a real, physical minimum ramp rate: the fastest rate at which its turbine can safely increase output after starting, a mechanical limit that does not depend on how much fuel is in the tank (see Appendix A).

Both resources are candidates for the same job: covering the evening ramp. Neither bid is useful to the committee unless Lena can show, using the actual measured rate at which net system load rises on a clear evening as solar falls away, whether that resource’s response capability is fast enough to cover it — and, if so, what it costs per unit of ramp rate covered, which is the number the committee will actually compare (see Appendix A). The raw evening net-load data Lena must model is in Appendix B. Any figure in this case that is not a matter of public record is stated as an explicit modeling assumption (Appendix C) rather than guessed, and every sourced figure is listed in Appendix D. The vocabulary and the “how to…” skills this case will ask of you are listed in Appendix F — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.     Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 · Fit P(t) and Differentiate (D1)

    Fit a quadratic P(t) = a·t² + b·t + c through Appendix B's three steepest points, letting t = minutes after 16:00: (60, 19,500), (120, 22,500), (180, 29,000). Then use the power rule to write dP/dt = m·t + k.

    Power rule reminder: for a term a·tn, the derivative is n·a·tn-1.

    Step 2 · Tangent Line at the Steepest Instant (D2)

    Evaluate dP/dt at each end of the fitted window (t = 60 and t = 180) to find where the rise is steepest, then write the tangent line there in the form P = m·t + k.

    dP/dt is linear across the fitted window, so its extreme value over that window is always at one of the two endpoints — check both.

    Step 3 · Ramp-Response Capability Table (D3)

    Convert each option's own physical limit to MW per minute, then compare it to your Step 2 slope: does the option's own capability meet or exceed the rate the grid must cover?

    MW/min conversion: power (MW) ÷ response time (minutes). A response time given in seconds converts to minutes by dividing by 60.
