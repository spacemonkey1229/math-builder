Overview

The truck's cargo door rattles shut on the last box, and Jake Markenan wipes his palms on his jeans. “Can you hear me now?” he calls out, grinning, and Alyssa laughs from the porch — an old joke from a commercial neither of them can fully remember anymore. Then her phone buzzes on the kitchen counter, face-down, ignored for the second time in ten minutes.

Two years in Belgium taught them to stop thinking about phone bills. Vodafone just worked, everywhere, and neither of them ever opened the app to check. That phone on the counter isn't connected to anything now — the international SIM stopped working the moment their flight landed in Portland.

“Work starts Monday,” Jake says. “I need a phone that actually works before then.”

Alyssa picks hers up, turns it over, sets it back down screen-first. “Two phones. Two iPads. And Charity's been asking about a phone of her own for school.” She looks at the ceiling, doing math she can't finish out loud. “Pick one tomorrow. Whichever it is, we're stuck with it a while.”

Jake nods slowly, already reaching for his laptop.

Background

Jake and Alyssa Markenan are back in Eugene, Oregon after two years in Belgium, where Jake sold medical equipment to hospitals across Europe. Jake has just been hired at a medical device sales firm based in Eugene; Alyssa is looking forward to being close to her family again. Their two iPhones, brought back from Belgium, are unlocked and ready to use — what they don't have is an American carrier.

Extended family has recommended both of the country's two largest wireless carriers: Verizon and AT&T (see Appendices A and B). Both offer several tiers of “unlimited” plans, and the tier that actually fits the Markenans' needs is not automatically the cheapest one on the page.

Jake's new job has him driving to hospitals and clinics across Oregon — specifically Bend, Astoria, Eugene, Portland, Burns, and Florence — at least four days a week, with occasional trips to Las Vegas, Seattle, and Canada. On the road, his phone doubles as his internet connection: the Markenans are not installing home internet service, and Jake also needs to be able to turn his phone into a mobile hotspot for his laptop while working from hotel rooms and rental cars. Alyssa wants to be reachable the entire time he's driving, especially through Oregon winters, and she streams Disney+ for the kids (Charity, 12; Tommy, 8; Samantha, 5) — including a lot of Bluey — on her own phone at home.

For now, the family needs service for two phones and two iPads (four lines). Charity is old enough that a phone of her own — a third line — is a near-term addition, for school, lessons, and activities. A fourth phone, for one of the younger kids, is likely within the next four years, and Jake and Alyssa want to know what that will cost before they're locked into a plan (see Appendices A, B, and C).

Jake's new job pays well, but Eugene's housing market has the family watching every recurring expense closely. They want a plan that genuinely covers what they need — reliable coverage on Jake's routes, a real mobile hotspot, Canada roaming for his occasional cross-border trips (see Appendix E) — at the lowest monthly cost that actually delivers all of it, including Oregon's own wireless taxes and fees (see Appendix D). Curious about the real-world context behind this case — cell towers, hotspots, roaming, and what “unlimited” really means? See Appendix G, Learn More.

Appendix E — Canada Roaming and Coverage

Jake occasionally drives into Canada for work. Both carriers currently include unlimited talk, text, and data roaming in Canada and Mexico at no extra charge with an eligible unlimited plan — this is the same for both options and does not change which plan is cheaper, but it does rule out needing a separate international add-on for either carrier.

Nationwide network coverage. Verizon's 4G LTE network covers about 70% of U.S. land area, the largest nationwide footprint of any carrier. AT&T's 4G LTE network covers about 68% of the nation — a real but smaller footprint, particularly in rural and remote regions.

Appendix F — Modeling Assumptions

Tablet-line pricing. Appendix C's tablet add-on rate is applied per tablet line regardless of the number of phone lines already on the account, and regardless of which phone-plan tier is selected. The research for this case found a clear “additional tablet line” rate for each carrier, but could not confirm a reliable, currently-live “first tablet vs. additional tablet” price split for every plan tier — a flat per-tablet rate is the more defensible number to build on than a guessed split.

Tax and fee rate. Appendix D's combined 22.53% Oregon rate is applied to the full pre-tax monthly bill (phone lines plus tablet lines together) as a single combined multiplier, rather than trying to separately model which specific charges are percentage-based versus flat per-line fees.

AT&T Value 2.0's middle tiers. Appendix A does not give a 2-line or 3-line price for Value 2.0 — D1 rules it out for lacking hotspot before any deeper cost modeling happens, so this case does not require those figures.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    Jake's hotspot habit is not a hypothetical: he drives to clinics across Oregon at least four days a week, using his phone as his and Alyssa's home internet connection, and needs the hotspot specifically for his laptop. Verizon Plus's 30GB mobile hotspot allowance is a real, finite monthly cap — a working professional hotspotting a laptop for email, video calls, and account software four days a week for a full month can realistically approach or exceed 30GB, at which point Verizon throttles hotspot speed for the rest of the billing cycle. AT&T Extra 2.0's 50GB cap has meaningfully more headroom for the identical use pattern.

    This doesn't change which plan is cheaper on paper — it changes whether the cheaper plan on paper is actually the plan that reliably does the job Jake needs it to do every week.
