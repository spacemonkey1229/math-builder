Overview

Denise Okafor has the pipe spec sheet in one hand and her phone in the other, and neither one is telling her what she wants to hear.

The center-pivot line behind her throws a long shadow across the tailgate where she's spread two rolls of PVC catalog pages, corners flapping in the wind kicking up off the cut alfalfa. Denise designs irrigation systems for a small farm-contracting outfit, and this week's job is a mainline retrofit that has to do two things at once: pass government certification on paper, and beat a competitor's number on the invoice. The farmer standing twenty feet away, arms crossed, isn't going to ask her which pipe size clears the velocity limit or which one wins on the spreadsheet — he's going to ask what the check looks like, both ways.

Her laptop is open on the tailgate, the spreadsheet tab still blinking where she left it: two pipe sizes, two friction numbers, two totals that refuse to agree on a winner depending on which line she trusts. She's run the smaller pipe's velocity three times now, and gotten close enough to the limit each time that "close enough" has stopped feeling like an answer.

Her phone buzzes against the catalog pages — the competing contractor's quote, forwarded by the farmer, subject line just a dollar sign and three exclamation points. Denise doesn't open it yet. She sets it face-down, picks up her pencil, and looks at the two pipe sizes circled on the spec sheet, and doesn't write anything under either one.

Background

Denise Okafor is an irrigation systems designer at a small farm-irrigation contracting firm. She is responsible for producing the pipe specification that goes into her firm's bid on a mainline retrofit job, and she is personally accountable for two separate things the client cares about: whether her design certifies against the federal conservation-practice standard that governs the client's cost-share contract, and whether her firm's total price beats a competing contractor's bid. Missing on either one costs the job.

Two mainline pipe options are on the table for this stretch of irrigation main, both cut from the same class of PVC pipe stock her firm regularly installs.

The Lean Main. The smaller-diameter PVC option. It costs less per foot of pipe and weighs less per foot (SEE APPENDIX B, C), which matters twice over: less material spend up front, but also a smaller per-pound reimbursement under the client's cost-share program, since that program pays out by the pound of pipe actually installed (SEE APPENDIX E). Running the design flow rate through a smaller pipe also drives the water faster through the same cross-section, which pushes flow velocity — and, separately, friction loss — both higher than the wider option (SEE APPENDIX D).

The Margin Main. The next-size-up PVC option. It costs more per foot and weighs more per foot, which raises both the material bill and the per-pound reimbursement. The wider cross-section keeps flow velocity lower at the same design flow rate, and cuts the pipeline's friction loss substantially — which lowers the annual cost of running the pump that pushes water through it (SEE APPENDIX D, APPENDIX F).

The client's cost-share contract is administered under USDA's Environmental Quality Incentives Program (EQIP), which will only reimburse this pipeline if it certifies against NRCS Conservation Practice Standard, Irrigation Pipeline (Code 430) (SEE APPENDIX D). Code 430 sets a hard numeric ceiling on pipeline flow velocity at the design flow rate; a design that exceeds that ceiling fails certification outright, and the EQIP reimbursement is forfeited entirely — not reduced — regardless of how the rest of the design performs (SEE APPENDIX E). Code 430 also requires friction loss to be computed by the Hazen-Williams equation, using a roughness coefficient specific to the pipe material (SEE APPENDIX D).

The farm's field layout fixes the mainline's required design flow rate and total run length; neither is Denise's to change (SEE APPENDIX A). A competing contractor has already quoted the farmer a total out-of-pocket price for the job, and Denise's own bid has to come in below it once the client's own EQIP reimbursement is netted out (SEE APPENDIX A). Running the pump that pushes water through whichever mainline is installed costs the client money every year the system operates, at a rate that depends on the pipeline's own friction loss (SEE APPENDIX F).

    Step 1 — D4: Certification check

    Step 2 — D1/D2: Installed cost and EQIP reimbursement

    Build each option's material+labor cost from Appendices A/B/C, and its EQIP reimbursement from Appendix E (per-pound rate × total installed weight) — only if that option certifies in Step 1.

    Step 3 — D3: Annual pump energy cost

    Compute each option's annual pump energy cost from Appendix F's brake-horsepower formula and the Hazen-Williams friction loss you'd get from Appendix D's equation.

    Step 4 — D6: Break-even flow rate

    Set the two options' total-annualized-cost-per-acre-inch expressions equal to each other and solve for the flow rate at which they cost the same. State whether the farm's actual 440 GPM sits above or below it.
