Case Study: The Trailer Return

Overview

Denise Okafor is standing in the Public Works yard at 5:15 in the morning with a grease-stained route map bungeed to a clipboard, watching her breath fog over the diesel exhaust of Truck 4. The recycling truck idles behind her, hopper empty, waiting on the same tangled loop it runs every collection day through the Bay Fruit Streets — Strawberry, Nectarine, Peach, Plum, Pine, and Fig, crossed by Bay, Mason, Madison, Monroe, Jefferson, Randolph, and Tazewell. She already knows how this morning ends: the driver will finish the far corner of Monroe, then double back down blocks he already emptied, just to bring the truck home to the yard the way the route always has to. A courier company's flatbed quote is open on her phone — enough truck-hauling capacity to pick Truck 4 up wherever it finishes and haul it back light, no retracing required. Her supervisor wants a one-page recommendation on his desk before the Thursday budget meeting: keep closing the loop the way it's always been closed, or pay someone else to close it for them. Denise pulls up the street grid on her phone, starts counting corners, and does not put the truck in gear yet.

Background

Denise Okafor is the Recycling Route Coordinator for the Department of Public Works in the town of Averyton. Fifteen years on this route have made her genuinely proud of the work — she still keeps a hand-annotated paper street map bungeed to Truck 4's dash, because, as she puts it, the GPS “argues with her” about which streets are actually one-way or blocked by construction, and her paper map has never once been wrong. She is not chasing a badge for it; shaving even a few minutes off a route is a real, satisfying win to her, not just a box her supervisor asked her to check. Practical, a little stubborn about trusting her own eyes on the pavement over what a screen tells her, but no luddite — she just knows these streets better than any app does. She is responsible for the weekly single-stream recycling route through the Bay Fruit Streets district — a 68-intersection, 102-block-segment street grid along the town's original harbor-front plat — and is accountable to her supervisor for the truck's daily diesel cost, which comes out of the same line item as every other route in town (SEE APPENDIX A). She does not set the town's labor contract or its fuel budget, but her route-design memo is what her supervisor uses to decide whether Averyton pays an outside hauling contractor for anything beyond the truck's own tires.

Every collection route that has to cover every block exactly once and return to where it started is what a mathematician calls an Euler circuit — and a route can only close into one if, at every intersection in its graph, an even number of block-segments meet there (SEE APPENDIX G). Denise's own count of the Bay Fruit Streets grid found 34 intersections where an odd number of streets meet — corners where the truck cannot simply pass through and continue, because every incoming block needs an outgoing one to pair with it. Today, Truck 4 handles those odd corners by backtracking: at each one it re-drives a segment it has already emptied, so the loop still closes back at the yard. That backtracking is not free — every repeated block is diesel the town pays for and driving time down streets that produce nothing for that day's collection totals.

Denise has priced out a different fix. The Closed Loop is today's standing practice: Truck 4 runs the whole grid as one closed circuit, returning under its own power to the yard every morning, repeat mileage and all. The Trailer Return would keep the truck's collection path itself almost unchanged, but drop the requirement that it drive itself home — instead, a contracted flatbed hauler picks the truck up wherever its most efficient path happens to end and trailers it back to the yard. Mathematically, dropping the "must return to the start" requirement turns the question from does this graph have an Euler circuit? into does this graph have an Euler path? — a route that covers every block exactly once but is allowed to start and end at two different corners (SEE APPENDIX G). That relaxation lets Denise leave the single worst pair of odd corners unmatched instead of pairing all of them, which is real, computable savings in repeat mileage — at the real, quoted cost of a flatbed hauler (SEE APPENDICES B, C, D, E and F).

    Enter the number of odd-degree vertices you counted for the full Bay Fruit Streets grid (Appendix A). The notebook will tell you whether that count gives an Euler circuit, an Euler path, or neither — it will not tell you which option to recommend.

    Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    The $115/day hauling fee in MA4 is not a quote for hauling Truck 4. It is based on a published 2026 rate range for towing a standard passenger vehicle — a flatbed picking up a sedan, not a loaded municipal collection truck. A real quote for hauling a truck the size and weight of Truck 4 would almost certainly need a bigger, more specialized tow rig than a sedan needs, at a real premium over that consumer rate (SEE Appendix G, source S6).

    Does this change your dollar comparison — or just how confident you are in it? Appendix F's own sensitivity note already says the case isn't sensitive to MA4's exact direction — even the fee cut roughly in half still leaves Option B far more expensive than Option A. So if the real fee is likely higher, not lower, than $115, what does that do to Option A's dollar-side argument — does it get stronger, or does it just get more uncertain?
