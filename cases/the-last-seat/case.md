## Overview

Wilhelmina Ashgrove has two printed drafts of the same memo side by side on her desk, and a third scrap of paper that is not a draft at all.

The scrap is a yellow sticky note with a state's name on it and a row number from a spreadsheet, written in her own cramped handwriting. Down the hall a printer groans through its warm-up and the whole floor smells of toner and burnt coffee. Twenty minutes ago a junior analyst leaned in the doorway and asked, not unkindly, whether it was really true that a seat in Congress could come down to a decimal.

It is true. On the monitor behind her sits the sheet where every seat in the House is handed out one at a time, in order, until the last one is gone. Near the bottom, two rows sit almost touching. One belongs to Minnesota and one to New York. One state got the final seat, and the other landed just on the wrong side of the line. Higher up, in a row she has stopped needing to look at, Montana's second seat sits safely in place, the first time in three decades that state has had two. Nobody in those rows did anything but get counted.

She does not choose the method. Congress chose it long before she was hired. All she can do is tell the Director what she thinks the numbers say, and the Director will pass it to the people who can change the law, or not.

One draft is headed "No change." The other is headed "Amend the statute." She squares the pages once more, picks up the stapler, and holds it open over the corner of the first page. The only question left is which stack it goes through.

## Background

Wilhelmina Ashgrove is a fictional mathematical statistician on the apportionment team at the U.S. Census Bureau. **This scene is constructed.** The 2020 apportionment figures in this case are real and published; Wilhelmina, her memo and its date, 1 March 2027, are the author's invention (SEE APPENDIX F).

The House of Representatives has 435 seats [S3] [S6]. The Constitution gives every state at least one [S7]; the other 385 seats (435 minus one for each of the 50 states) are divided by population in a job called **apportionment** [S11]. The population used is each state's *apportionment population*: its residents plus federal military and civilian employees stationed overseas, counted to their home state [S3]. Federal law has told the President to divide the seats "by the method known as the method of equal proportions," also called the **Huntington-Hill** method, since 1941 [S5] [S6]. The Bureau runs that method; it does not choose it. Any other method requires a new law from Congress (SEE APPENDIX D).

By 1 March 2027 Wilhelmina must send the Director a one-page memo, to be passed to the congressional committees with census jurisdiction, advising what the Bureau should say about the 2030 apportionment. She is accountable for defending her recommendation with numbers rather than preference. The two options:

**Keep Equal Proportions** (Option A): recommend that the Bureau tell Congress to leave the Huntington-Hill method in law for 2030. No change in law is needed.

**Amend To Webster** (Option B): recommend that the Bureau tell Congress to change the law, before the 2030 count, to **Webster's** method, which gives each state the whole number of seats nearest its share (SEE APPENDIX A).

Both methods are well known, and experts disagree about which is fairer, partly because they define "fair" differently (SEE APPENDIX D). Appendix C therefore gives two yardsticks, each lower-is-better, computed on the 2020 populations in Appendix B: the *worst gap* and the *total distance from exact share*. The memo must say which yardstick it adopts and why. A method also has a cost beyond its numbers: the current one is already law, so Option B must argue that whatever it gains is worth persuading Congress to act.

How close was 2020? The last seat awarded and the first seat left out are listed in Appendix E [S2]. All figures here are cited in Appendix G or declared in Appendix F. The vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case asks you to research are listed in Appendix H.

## Appendix A — Two Ways To Hand Out Seats

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D1, D2). Each state first receives one seat. The other 385 seats are then awarded one at a time. The two methods differ only in how they decide who is next.

**Huntington-Hill (equal proportions) [S5] [S12].** For a state with population *p* that already holds *n - 1* seats, the *priority value* of its *n*th seat is

priority value = *p* divided by the square root of (*n* x (*n* - 1))

For example, a state's 2nd seat uses the square root of 2 x 1, its 3rd seat the square root of 3 x 2. Seats go out in order of priority value, highest first, from the 51st seat through the 435th. The 435th seat is therefore the last one awarded. The Census Bureau publishes the full ordered list [S2]. Use a calculator or spreadsheet for the square roots.

**Webster (Sainte-Lagu&euml;) [S13].** Choose a number called the *divisor*. For each state, divide its population by the divisor and round to the nearest whole number (a result exactly halfway rounds up), but never give a state fewer than one seat. (A *divisor method* is any method built this way: pick one number, divide every state by it, round, and adjust the number until the seats add up.) If the 50 results add to exactly 435, that divisor works. If they add to more than 435, try a larger divisor; if fewer, a smaller one. The national average persons per representative in Appendix B is a sensible first trial.

## Appendix C — The Two Yardsticks And The Margin Convention

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D3 to D6). These two definitions are this case's own choice of measures (SEE APPENDIX F); other measures exist (SEE APPENDIX D). Both are lower-is-better.

| | Yardstick 1 — worst gap | Yardstick 2 — distance from exact share |
|---|---|---|
| What it measures | How unequal the most-represented and least-represented states are | How far the seats, taken together, sit from each state's exact share |
| How to compute | For each state, persons per representative = population divided by seats. Divide the largest by the smallest. 1.0000 would mean perfectly equal. | For each state, exact quota = population x 435 / total population. Add up, over the 50 states, the unsigned difference between seats and quota. |
| In plain words | Persons per representative is how many people one seat stands for. A bigger number means each person's voice in that seat is spread thinner. | The exact quota is the fractional number of seats a state would get if seats could be cut into pieces. |
| Rounding | Report to four decimal places | Quotas unrounded; report sums to two decimal places |

**Margin convention (used everywhere in this case).** Margin = (gap between the two methods on a yardstick) divided by (the better, i.e. smaller, of the two values on that yardstick), written as a percent. Compute the margin from the unrounded values of the yardstick, and round only the final percent to two decimals.

## Appendix D — What The Law Says And What Experts Say

Referenced in Background and Challenge 3 (D6).

**The law.** The Constitution requires at least one Representative for each state [S7]. Title 2 of the U.S. Code, section 2a, tells the President to report each state's apportionment of the existing number of Representatives "by the method known as the method of equal proportions" [S5]. The Census Bureau's own history page says the 1941 act made reapportionment automatic after each census using that method, with the House at 435 seats [S6], and the Bureau's 2020 calculations cite sections 2a and 2b [S3]. Changing the method therefore means Congress amending the law.

**The Supreme Court, 1992.** In *United States Department of Commerce v. Montana*, 503 U.S. 442, the Court held unanimously that Congress acted within its constitutional authority in using equal proportions after the 1990 census. Montana had argued that a different divisor method, named for Dean, would have made district sizes closer to equal by a measure of the plain gap in people per seat, and would have kept its second seat [S8]. The Court ruled on constitutionality, not on which method is best.

**Experts disagree, and partly about what "bias" means.** Here a method is called *biased* toward small states if, over many censuses, it tends to leave small states with more seats per person than large states.
- *For a change.* Ruoxi Li (2022) argues that Huntington-Hill is biased against more populous states and recommends Webster's method as the only unbiased divisor method [S9]. A Congressional Research Service report quotes H. Peyton Young as saying the method "systematically favors the small states" [S10].
- *The other side.* The same CRS report notes that a Census Bureau mathematician, Lawrence R. Ernst, filed a declaration for the government in a 1991 case disputing that finding, and that the report's own author found the bias evidence the more persuasive of the two [S10]. The report also lists several different measures of how equal district sizes are, and describes the choice among them as arguably a matter of judgment rather than an indisputable mathematical test [S10].

This case does not take a side. A method can look best on one reasonable measure and not on another.
