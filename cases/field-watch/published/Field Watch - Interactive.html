Overview

Talia Brennan has three satellite images of the same wheat field open on two monitors, and a claim file that closes at five this afternoon. The field's owner says drought stress killed a section of the crop. The insurer needs a number, not a feeling — and the number has to come from the pictures.

Every few days, a free public satellite passes over the same patch of Nebraska and measures how green it still is. Healthy wheat reflects light one way. Stressed, dying wheat reflects it another way. Three passes in, the trend is clearly downward — the only question is how fast, and on exactly which day the field's condition crosses the line that turns “stressed” into “damage,” a line the insurer's own claim file is waiting on.

Talia has two ways to answer that question, built from the same three pictures. One trusts the field's whole recent history. The other trusts only its most recent, most alarming stretch. They don't agree with each other — and by the time the file closes this afternoon, she has to put just one of them in it.

She opens a blank page, writes down the numbers from all three passes, and starts building the equation that will actually decide this.

Background

When a farmer's crop is damaged, the insurance payout isn't automatic — a claim fileThe paperwork and evidence record an insurer builds to verify a loss before paying a claim — it isn't approved just because a farmer says the crop is damaged.See the real source → first has to be verified: someone has to confirm, with evidence, that real loss actually happened before the insurer cuts a check. This is a real mechanism, not a plot device. Crop insurance in the U.S. is heavily backed by the USDA's Federal Crop Insurance Program, administered by the Risk Management Agency (RMA), and the RMA has genuinely used satellite-based remote sensing — including the same kind of optical imagery this case relies on — to check claims against what the ground actually shows (NASA reports on this directly; see Appendix G). Both sides have something real at stake in getting the verification right: the farmer is waiting on money to cover a genuine loss, and the insurer has to avoid paying out on a claim the evidence doesn't support, without unfairly stalling or denying one that's real. Talia's satellite analysis is the objective number both sides are counting on.

Talia Brennan is a remote-sensing analyst for a crop-insurance claims service, verifying a damage claim on a wheat field in Nebraska using nothing but publicly available satellite imagery. From just those two ordinary bands, optical satellites like Sentinel-2 and Landsat compute a real, standard quantity called the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI)A real, standard measurement of plant health computed from the ratio of near-infrared to red-light reflectance — roughly 0 (bare ground) to 1 (dense, healthy vegetation).NASA Earth Observatory explainer → — a number between roughly 0 (bare ground) and 1 (dense, healthy vegetation), essentially a ratio comparing how much near-infrared light a surface reflects to how much red light it reflects (NASA's own explainer walks through the formula; see Appendix A). As real vegetation comes under real stress — drought, disease, fire — its NDVI genuinely declines, which is exactly why insurers use it as evidence (see Appendix A).

That leaves Talia with a real choice about which two passes to trust for her trend line. Model A uses Pass 1 and Pass 2 — the field's longer, more established history. Model B uses Pass 2 and Pass 3 — only the most recent stretch, which captures the accelerating decline but rests on fewer data points. Both are real, defensible lines through real data; they simply don't agree (see Appendix B, C). Each model's raw, point-slope form has a slope — the rate its NDVI reading changes per day — and an anchor point; turning that raw form into a single simplified expression takes the distributive property and combining like terms (see Challenge D1, D2).

The insurer's claim file has a real threshold NDVI value below which the field counts as damaged — though as Appendix E shows, there is no single universal NDVI damage threshold in real use; every insurer sets its own, calibrated to the crop and region, so this case states its own value as a modeling assumption (see Appendix D). Whichever model Talia trusts, she has to solve it — as an actual equation, not a guess — for the exact day the field's NDVI is predicted to cross that threshold. Solve both, and a Hero will find something worth noticing: both models predict the field crosses the threshold with real safety buffer left before the claim file's real filing deadline — this isn't a race against missing that deadline. The actual dilemma is which prediction Talia can defend in the file: the model anchored in the field's longer, more established history, or the model that captures its most recent, most alarming stretch (see Appendix C, D).

Appendix C — Why the Models Disagree

Model A's slope (−0.02 NDVI/day) is shallower than Model B's slope (−0.022 NDVI/day) — the decline sped up between Pass 2 and Pass 3. Model A, anchored in the field's longer history, predicts a later crossing day than Model B, which is more responsive to the field's most recent, most alarming stretch. Neither model is simply “wrong” — they are built from different, both-real subsets of the same three passes. See Challenge D3, D4, D5.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 · Model A, Simplified (D1)

    Appendix B gives Model A's raw point-slope form. Distribute the coefficient across the parentheses, then combine like terms so the equation reads as a single NDVI = (intercept) + (slope)·day expression. Enter both simplified coefficients.

    Step 2 · Model B, Simplified (D2)

    Do the same for Model B's raw point-slope form from Appendix B: distribute, then combine like terms. If you've revealed the Twist below and checked its box, this re-checks against the corrected reading.

    Step 3 · Model A's Crossing Day (D3)

    Using your simplified Model A (D1) and Appendix D's threshold, solve the resulting equation for the day Model A predicts the field crosses the threshold.

    Step 4 · Model B's Crossing Day (D4)

    Do the same for Model B (D2), solving for its own crossing day. If you've applied the Twist, this re-checks against the corrected reading.

    Step 5 · Comparing The Models (D5)

    Write a single simplified expression for how much Model A's and Model B's predicted NDVI differ from each other, on any given day — Model A's simplified expression (D1) minus Model B's simplified expression (D2), combined into one expression. Enter its intercept and its slope (the coefficient of day). If you've applied the Twist, this re-checks against the corrected reading.

    Step 6 · The Safety Buffer (D6)

    Using D3, D4, and Appendix D's filing deadline, find how many days of buffer each model gives before the deadline (deadline minus crossing day). If you've applied the Twist, this re-checks Model B's buffer against the corrected reading.

    Step 7 · The Verdict (D7)

    Using D6's two buffers, find the percentage margin between them — how much larger the winning buffer is than the losing one, as a percent — and recommend which model to trust. If you've applied the Twist, this checks against the corrected margin instead.
