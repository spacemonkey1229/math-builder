Overview

The wind coming off the river keeps trying to turn the pages, so Nadia Brennan holds the binder flat with her forearm and reads the same column again.

She is standing on the levee crown at the reach the district board has taken to calling the soft spot, boots in wet crown gravel, watching a fisherman's truck idle on the access road below. Behind her the town's water tower is close enough to read. In front of her the channel is running low and brown and completely harmless-looking, which is the part she has never been able to get the board to feel.

Nadia is the district's engineer of record. When the design certification goes out, her seal is the one on it — not the board's, not the contractor's — and that certification says this levee will hold what the river sends. She has one construction season and one appropriation, and she has spent the better part of a month learning that the river does not send the same thing twice.

Two binders, flat on the tailgate. One holds a storm that arrived like a slammed door, up and over and gone before the district's own crews had finished sandbagging the boat ramp. The other holds a storm that never looked frightening on any single morning and simply refused to leave.

Her phone buzzes against the truck bed. The board chair. The bid package goes out at the end of the week, and it can describe exactly one design.

Nadia looks at the channel, then at the binders, then at the channel again.

Background

Nadia Brennan is the district engineer for the Marrow Bend Levee District, a small special-purpose district responsible for a single leveed reach on Henderson Creek in western Illinois. The board controls the appropriation; the design certification carries Nadia's professional seal, and she is personally accountable for the standard she certifies to.

Henderson Creek is gaged continuously by the U.S. Geological Survey, which publishes a daily mean discharge — the average rate of flow past the gage, in cubic feet per second — for every day of record (see Appendix A). Two real floods from that record are the district's design storms this season: The Slammed Door, a short, violent event, and The Long Guest, a flood that ran for days (see Appendix A).

Below a certain discharge — the reach's channel capacity — the channel carries the creek without loading the levee at all. Above that rate, every additional cubic foot per second is water the district's works have to handle (see Appendix B). That water, and only that water, is what this case calls floodwater: the volume above channel capacity. Flow at or below channel capacity is not floodwater, and it never enters the board's number for either bid. Appendix B also carries the gage's published flood-frequency record, which the district's engineering standards require the certification to reference.

A design safely manages floodwater when that water is either carried inside the channel the design provides or held in the storage the design provides. Floodwater that is neither is a shortfall. Two bids are on the table and the appropriation pays for exactly one (see Appendix C).

The Crest Standard raises and armors the levee crown so the channel itself carries a higher discharge. It handles any flow at or below its design discharge for as long as the storm lasts — duration is free — and everything above that design discharge is a shortfall, no matter how briefly it lasts.

The Basin Standard leaves the crown where it was built and buys two upstream floodwater detention structures. They absorb flow above channel capacity at any rate at all — peak is free — but only until their storage is full; everything after that is a shortfall.

The board scores the two bids on one number, stated in the bid package: acre-feet of floodwater safely managed per one thousand dollars of appropriation, totalled across both design storms (see Appendices C and D). Appendix D also carries the conversion the district's reports use to turn a discharge rate into a volume, and the appropriation the board has voted.

Nadia may not change either bid's scope and may not change the river. Where a figure below is not a matter of public record it is stated as an explicit modeling assumption (see Appendix F) rather than guessed, and every sourced figure is listed in Appendix E. The vocabulary and the "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in Appendix G — that is where Challenge 1 sends you. Curious what any of this looks like in the field? See Appendix H for further reading.

A levee is only as high as its lowest point. Bid A raises the whole 2.50-mile reach or the reach is not certified at all — the height in its scope line is the height it holds everywhere, and it is what the contractor bid against the published appropriation, not a figure the district derived (see Appendix F, MA1).

Shortfall, per option. Bid A's shortfall on a storm is the volume of flow above 5,927 cfs. Bid B's shortfall on a storm is the floodwater above channel capacity that its usable storage cannot hold. The two design storms are ten weeks apart in the real record, and the detention structures' outlet works draw a full pool down in far less time than that (see Appendix F, MA3), so each storm meets the structures with their usable storage available.

Reading Appendix A as a function of time. Every row of Appendix A is the mean discharge over one whole day, not a reading taken at an instant. Let t be measured in days, with t = 0 at the start of the first row's day; a storm of n rows then occupies the window 0 ≤ t ≤ n days, and each row is the average value of Q on one one-day subinterval of it. Two things follow, and the district's reports use both:

  • Sum with Δt = one day and one subinterval per row. Work out for yourself, from the USGS sentence above, what that sum returns and whether the choice of left, right or midpoint placement changes it — D1 asks you to say which, and why.
  • A smooth curve fitted through the rows is a different object. Place each daily mean at the midpoint of its day (t = 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, …) if you fit one. Compare its integral against your sum, and be ready to say what any gap between the two is — and whether it is an error.

The district scores the bids on the published daily record, so every acre-foot in the D4 table comes from Appendix A's daily values, not from a fitted curve.

Two lines this case states rather than measures. Neither is in the table above, because neither is an estimate of an unknown quantity — each is a choice, made in the open, that the arithmetic then obeys. Both move the answer further than anything in the table does.

  1. Channel capacity is taken to be the discharge at the National Weather Service's published minor flood stage — 21.50 ft on Appendix B's rating. That is a choice of convention, not a measurement. Raising it shrinks both storms' floodwater volumes; lowering it grows them — and it does not grow the two storms' volumes at the same rate, which is something you can check and should. Every row of Appendix B's rating excerpt is a different convention, and moving one row is enough to move the recommendation.
  2. The height in Bid A's scope line is a bid's scope, not a property of the river. It selects a second row of the same rating, and the discharge in Appendix C is whatever that row says. A different raise is a different bid, not a corrected number.

A Hero who wants to attack the recommendation should attack these two lines first, and should say which row of Appendix B they are standing on when they do.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to certify; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Riemann sum of total volume (D1)

    Appendix D's convention: an n-row storm spans n one-day subintervals, Δt = 1 day, one subinterval per row. Pick a storm, add up its daily mean discharges (cfs-days), and convert to acre-feet using Appendix D's factor (1.98347). Then say whether your sum over-, under-, or exactly reproduces the published record.

    Why "exact" is a defensible answer here (and isn't, for most Riemann sums): each row is already a daily mean, not a sampled instant, and USGS's own definition (Appendix D, [S7]) says the daily mean numerically equals the daily volume in cfs-days. Summing n daily means over n whole days reproduces the published total precisely — the approximation, if there is one, is in how USGS built the daily mean, not in your arithmetic.

    Step 2 — Antiderivative and FTC for a fitted Q(t) (D2)

    This notebook fits one reasonable quadratic Q(t) = at² + bt + c through Storm 1's 8 daily-mean points, each placed at the midpoint of its day (Appendix D): a = −256.01786, b = 1586.55952, c = 1440.01637, over the window 0 ≤ t ≤ 8. Write the general antiderivative F(t) using the power rule (don't forget the constant of integration), then evaluate the definite integral over [0, 8] by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

    Compare this FTC value to your Step 1 sum for Storm 1. They will not match — a smooth least-squares curve necessarily clips a one-day spike like 2019-02-24's 8,060 cfs, so the fitted model's integral runs under the true published total. That gap is real information about the model, not an arithmetic mistake in either calculation.

    Step 3 — Area between Q(t) and channel capacity (D3)

    Using the same Step 2 model, find where Q(t) = 1,934 cfs (channel capacity) within [0, 8], then compute the area between Q(t) and the horizontal capacity line over that interval — the floodwater your curve model predicts. Then compare it to the daily-block figure read straight off Appendix A.

    Step 4 — Floodwater safely managed, both standards (D4)

    D4 is scored from Appendix A's daily values, not a fitted curve (Appendix D says so). Using Appendix A/B/C/F, compute each standard's managed floodwater (acre-feet) across both storms, and the acre-feet-per-$1,000-of-appropriation figure the board actually scores. Try the slider to see how MA2's storage-reservation assumption moves the picture — this is exactly what Tier 2 probe 5 checks mechanically against sensitivity_model.py.
