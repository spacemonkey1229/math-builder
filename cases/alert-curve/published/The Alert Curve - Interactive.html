Overview

The dashboard's third monitor throws blue light across Jordan Lee's face, and the coffee next to his keyboard went cold an hour ago. It's the dead middle of the overnight shift, the SOC bullpen empty except for the hum of the server room through the wall, and the alert queue just kicked out a spike he can't ignore. Somewhere in the network, traffic to one edge node has been climbing for the last several minutes, and the automated dashboard just painted the tile red.

Jordan's cursor hovers over two things at once: the escalation button that would put the on-call incident commander's phone buzzing on a nightstand three time zones away, and a second browser tab, still loading, where the SOC's own analytics console can plot the actual rate of change of that alert curve — traffic volume and threat score multiplied together, a real live function he could differentiate right now if he waits one more sampling interval for the data to fill in.

He's been burned both ways before. The senior analyst who paged the incident commander over a traffic spike that turned out to be nothing. The other one — quieter, worse — who waited to be sure, and by the time anyone woke up, the real thing had already been running loose for longer than anyone wanted to admit.

The red tile blinks. Jordan's hand is still on the mouse, hovering between the two tabs, deciding which one to click first.

Background

Jordan Lee is a Tier-2 Security Operations Center (SOC) analyst on the overnight shift at a financial-services company, responsible for triaging automated alerts before any human above him gets paged. Escalating incorrectly costs him credibility with the rest of the SOC team; missing or delaying a real escalation risks a live compromise inside a network that moves other people's money.

The SOC's monitoring platform tracks, for the edge node in question, two live quantities every sampling interval: traffic volume — the raw rate of incoming requests — and a threat score computed by the platform's detection engine from pattern-matching against known attack signatures. Together they define a composite alert-rate function: the number of automated alerts the system is generating per minute, equal to traffic volume multiplied by threat score at every instant. Jordan has two ways to decide whether tonight's spike is real:

Option A — The Threshold Trip. The platform already has a built-in rule: if the raw alert count in the current hour crosses a fixed threshold, escalate immediately, no further analysis required.

Option B — The Curve Check. Wait one more sampling interval, then use the composite alert-rate function's actual instantaneous rate of change — its derivative at this moment — to confirm the alert rate is truly accelerating, not just elevated by a volume spike that never touches the threat-scoring engine.

Both options work from the same underlying data; neither requires new instrumentation. What differs is timing and false-positive risk. The Threshold Trip escalates the instant the raw count crosses the line, whether the driver is a genuine coordinated attack or an ordinary traffic surge — a batch job, a marketing push, a monitoring tool's own polling — that happens to push the count over without the threat score ever moving. The Curve Check waits exactly one sampling interval before it can even evaluate a derivative, and during that wait a genuine accelerating attack keeps running, uninterrupted, for that same extra interval. Every escalation decision the SOC makes over a stretch of past shifts is logged, along with whether it was later confirmed a real incident — that log is what will ultimately show which policy actually protects the SOC's credibility and the company's exposure at once.

Appendix A — The Composite Alert-Rate Model

Let t be the number of minutes elapsed since the start of the current one-hour monitoring window, with t₀ the instant Jordan is deciding, "now."

Traffic volume V(t) = p·t² + q·t + r (requests/min, a smooth function of time).

Threat score is computed by the detection engine in two steps — a raw pattern-match count g(t) = k·t + m (inner function), run through a diminishing-returns scoring transform f(x) = c·√x (outer function), so S(t) = f(g(t)) = c·√(k·t + m). This composition is exactly why the chain rule is required to differentiate it.

The composite alert-rate function is the product of the two: A(t) = V(t)·S(t). Differentiating A(t) requires the product rule; differentiating S(t) inside that requires the chain rule — this function is deliberately built to force both.

Appendix B — Why the Raw Count Isn't Differentiable

The SOC's dashboard displays the raw alert count as a running tally, incremented by whole numbers only at the instant each individual alert fires — a step function in time, not a smooth curve. A step function has no defined instantaneous rate of change at the instant it jumps: no tangent line exists there, because the function is not differentiable at that point.

The composite alert-rate function A(t) built from the platform's underlying continuous telemetry (Appendix A) is a different object. Because V(t) and S(t) are each differentiable at every t, and because a product and a composition of functions that are differentiable everywhere is itself differentiable everywhere both pieces are, A(t) is differentiable across the whole sampling window. That gap — a step function with no instantaneous rate of change anywhere, versus a continuous model that has one everywhere — is exactly what the Curve Check policy exploits and the Threshold Trip policy cannot: only a differentiable function has an instantaneous rate of change to compute in the first place.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — Chain rule: dS/dt at t₀ (D1)

    S(t) = f(g(t)) = c·√(k·t + m). Use the chain rule (dS/dt = f′(g(t))·g′(t)) to find dS/dt at t₀ = 42 minutes, using Appendix A's coefficients (p, q, r, k, m, c) and Appendix C's t₀.

    Identify the outer function f(x) = c·√x and the inner function g(t) = k·t + m explicitly in your write-up — the calculator only checks the number.

    Step 2 — Product rule: dA/dt at t₀ (D2)

    A(t) = V(t)·S(t). Use the product rule (dA/dt = V′(t)·S(t) + V(t)·S′(t)), substituting your Step 1 answer for S′(t) = dS/dt.

    Step 3 — Why the raw count can't do this (D3)

    Using the definition of differentiable (a function has an instantaneous rate of change at a point only if it is differentiable there), which object in this case actually HAS a defined instantaneous rate of change at t₀?

    Step 4 — Tangent line prediction (D4)

    Write the tangent line to A(t) at t₀: L(t) = A(t₀) + (dA/dt)·(t − t₀), using your Step 2 slope. What does it predict for A at the next sampling instant, t₀ + 5 minutes (Appendix C's sampling interval)?
