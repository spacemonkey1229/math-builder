Overview

“We will now vote on City Ordinance 322b,” says Mayor Briggs Ulstein, loud enough to carry over the crowd pressing against the chamber doors, “titled ‘Designation Of Public Space For Long-Term Homeless Housing Encampment.’” Outside, protest signs bob above the glass. Inside, six council members’ hands go up. One stays down. The gavel cracks.

“Ordinance 322b has passed,” Ulstein announces, “and we look forward to hearing the report and proposal from the Office of City Planning at our next meeting.”

City Planner Paul Cordon doesn’t move from his seat in the front row. Eighteen years in this office, the last four running it, and he already knows what’s coming: two folders on his desk by morning, one site named in each, and a deadline that doesn’t care how many angry letters arrived this week. He built the bus system overhaul. He got the Willamette tunnel funded. None of that is on anyone’s mind right now.

A council aide catches his eye on the way out and mouths, two weeks. Cordon nods, gathers his coat, and heads for the door the protesters are still blocking.

Background

The city council suspended the removal of homeless encampments in mid-2020 and, as the encampment population grew, designated several downtown streets as temporary sites — a stopgap that drew complaints as tents and trash accumulated in city parks and on public sidewalks (see Appendix A). In early 2021 the council passed Ordinance 322b, committing the city to a longer-term solution: converting one existing public space into a permanent, serviced homeless encampment, with city-funded drinking water, restrooms, and an on-site medical care point.

City Planner Paul Cordon has been tasked with recommending exactly one site to the council within two weeks. Two candidate sites have survived the council’s own initial screening, each carrying real, opposing tradeoffs (see Appendices B, C and D):

  • The Westmoreland–Eastmoreland Site — Westmoreland Park together with the adjoining Eastmoreland Golf Course, the closest candidate to downtown’s existing shelters, outreach offices and medical services, sitting inside a higher-income residential neighborhood.
  • The Hayden Island Nature Preserve — the northwest half of Hayden Island, the most remote candidate from downtown services, sitting in a far less densely residential part of the city.

Cordon’s recommendation is not a private judgment call — every argument he brings to the council has to survive being checked against real acreage, real distances, and the city’s own published cost data for building out a site like this (see Appendices B, C and D). A recent poll put his approval rating below 35%, and this recommendation is the one his job most likely turns on either way he calls it.

All figures in this case are sourced or explicitly stated as scenario assumptions (see Appendices F and G). The vocabulary and “how to…” skills this case will ask you to research are listed in Appendix H — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

Appendices

Appendix A — Ordinance 322b and the Sites Under Consideration

Ordinance 322b (early 2021) authorized the city to convert one publicly owned open space into a permanent, serviced homeless encampment. The council’s original site list included a third park (Powell Butte Nature Park), which was dropped from consideration during the council’s own initial screening as the weaker fit of the three; only the two sites below remain live options for Cordon’s recommendation.

  • Site A — The Westmoreland–Eastmoreland Site: Westmoreland Park plus the adjoining Eastmoreland Golf Course, in southeast Portland’s Sellwood-Moreland area.
  • Site B — The Hayden Island Nature Preserve: the northwest half of Hayden Island, an island in the Columbia River between North Portland and Vancouver, Washington, reachable only via Interstate 5.

Background reading on the situation this ordinance responds to (real, publicly available context, not required for the math):

  • Portland Bureau of Planning and Sustainability, general homelessness planning resources: portland.gov

Appendix B — Distance and Access Data

City Hall (where Ordinance 322b was voted on) sits at 45.5150°N, 122.6791°W [S1]. Placing City Hall at the origin of a coordinate plane, with east and north as the positive axes, each site’s real geographic coordinates convert to a location in miles as follows, using the standard approximation that one degree of latitude spans 69.0 miles and one degree of longitude spans 69.172 × cos(latitude) miles [S2]:

SiteLatitude, LongitudeCoordinate pair (mi east, mi north of City Hall)Quadrant
City Hall (origin)45.5150°N, 122.6791°W [S1](0, 0)—
A — Westmoreland–Eastmoreland45.4706°N, 122.6397°W [S3](1.91, −3.07)IV
B — Hayden Island Nature Preserve45.6119°N, 122.6761°W [S4](0.15, 6.69)I

Use the distance formula (equivalent to the Pythagorean theorem applied to the east-leg and north-leg of each site’s coordinate pair — two segments that meet as perpendicular lines, exactly the way Portland’s own numbered avenues, laid out as parallel lines running north-south, meet the city’s east-west streets at right angles) to compute the straight-line distance from City Hall to each site. Show your work — this is D1.

For reference, Hayden Island is publicly described as roughly nine miles north of downtown Portland by road [S5]; your straight-line (not driving) distance will come out shorter than a road-route figure, and Appendix F explains why that is expected, not an error.

A real reference density. Portland’s Reedway Safe Rest Village sits on a 130,000-square-foot site and holds 120 sleeping units [S8] — a real, built example of how many residents a serviced encampment site can support per acre of land. Convert Reedway’s site size to acres, then set up a proportion to find the units-per-acre density this real site achieves, and apply Appendix F’s stated developed-footprint assumption (MA1) to each candidate site’s total acreage to estimate how many residents each site could hold. This is D2.

Sketch each site’s combined outer boundary (Site A is two adjoining, separately named parcels; Site B is a single island-half). Classify each boundary as convex or concave, and explain in your appendix how that classification affects whether you can find its area with a single rectangle formula or need to break the shape into pieces first.

Appendix D — Cost Data

Build-out cost. The City of Portland’s own Safe Rest Village program had a total budget of $56.3 million covering 2021–2024, for the six villages originally authorized [S9, S10]. Portland’s built villages average 49 sleeping units each [S11]. Use these two real figures to compute an average build-out-and-early-operating cost per resident-unit, then scale it to your Appendix C capacity estimate for each site.

Outreach-transportation cost. A remote site needs a daily outreach shuttle connecting residents to downtown medical, legal and social services. Using the 2026 federal business standard mileage rate of $0.725 per mile [S12], and Appendix F’s stated shuttle-frequency assumption (MA4), compute each site’s annual round-trip transportation cost using your Appendix B distance.

Add build-out cost and annual transportation cost for each site, then divide by your Appendix C capacity estimate to get a cost-per-resident figure for each option. This is D3.

Appendix E — The Weighted-Criteria Scoring Method

For each of the three criteria below, give the stronger option a score of 10, and give the weaker option a score of 10 × (its own value ÷ the stronger option’s value), using whichever of the two numbers actually makes it the better outcome (for accessibility and cost, a smaller number is better; for capacity, a larger number is better):

CriterionWhat you’re comparingData source
AccessibilityStraight-line distance to City Hall (smaller is better)Appendix B (D1)
CapacityEstimated residents housed (larger is better)Appendix C (D2)
Cost-efficiencyCost per resident (smaller is better)Appendix D (D3)

Then choose your own weight for each criterion (they must total 100%) and justify your choice in your pitch. Multiply each option’s three scores by your weights and add them up for a final weighted score per site. This is D4. Different Heroes choosing different, defensible weights are expected to land on different recommendations — that is the point of the exercise, not a flaw in it.

    Reveal the Twist

    Paul Cordon just got a call from the city’s environmental review office. The northwest half of Hayden Island the plan was counting on turns out to sit mostly within the Columbia River’s active floodplain and existing levee-protected wetland habitat — the same land conservation groups have been fighting to preserve for years. Building on it isn’t off the table, but the buildable footprint just got cut in half again. You now have 60 seconds. Does your recommendation still hold — or did it just flip?
