Case Study: The Downbeat Split
MS | Math | Case Study | 2026 | Adventure

## Overview

The click track loops in Elena Cross's headphones for the ninth time, and every time it loops, the
take underneath it is a hair long. Not enough that anyone at the network would flag it just by ear
— you'd need a stopwatch, and Elena is the only one in the booth anyone trusts with one. The
ensemble is on a break, brass mutes scattered across the risers, and the pencil marks on Elena's
score are starting to look less like notation and more like frustration. On the console in front of
her sits the client's spot, dropped into the session as a locked video reference, its outgoing
black frame burned into the timeline like a deadline that already happened. The producer, headset
draped around his neck, leans through the booth door for the third time this hour and says only,
"Whenever you're ready" — which everyone in the room understands to mean the take is due before
lunch.

Elena has two versions of the cue open side by side on her music stand. One is the version the
ensemble already knows cold, clean straight subdivisions they could sight-read blind, and it lands
the cue a hair off the mark — close enough that most arrangers would just nudge the tempo and call
it a day. The other is scrawled in pencil in the margin of the last measure, a syncopated rewrite
that splits one beat cleanly against the grain of the rest of the phrase, hits the mark exactly, and
would mean starting the rehearsal clock over from zero with an ensemble that is already watching the
wall clock over the concertmaster's shoulder.

She picks up her pencil, taps it once against the stand, and looks up.

## Background

Elena Cross is a staff arranger at Larkspur Sound, a commercial audio production house,
responsible for delivering a finished orchestral cue that fills the client's broadcast ad spot
exactly, frame for frame, and accountable to the session's contracted producer for both the take's
musical quality and the studio's booked rehearsal-hour budget (SEE APPENDIX A). The client's
broadcast slot has one fixed, non-negotiable target duration the finished cue must fill (SEE
APPENDIX A) — the network will not air a spot that runs long, and will not pad black frames to
cover one that runs short.

Two arrangements of the same sixteen-bar cue are on Elena's stand tonight, and the ensemble has
already worked through the first one in this session:

**The Straight Cut** — the arrangement already rehearsed, built entirely from straight, repeating
mixed-number note values (SEE APPENDIX B). The ensemble can sight-read it cleanly, but multiplying
the phrase's mixed-number beat length by the mixed-number seconds-per-beat at session tempo across
the whole cue lands its total running time close to, but not exactly on, the broadcast slot's
target — the remaining gap has to be absorbed as a small across-the-board tempo adjustment (SEE
APPENDICES A and B).

**The Downbeat Split** — a rewrite of one beat in the final measure, splitting that beat by
dividing a fraction of it by the ensemble's normal fractional subdivision unit (SEE APPENDIX D),
sized to close the Straight Cut's timing gap exactly rather than approximately. The syncopated
subdivision reads correctly on the page and hits the target slot precisely, but it is new material
the ensemble has not rehearsed, and every additional read-through of an unfamiliar subdivision like
this one is booked, and paid, at the studio's standard union rehearsal rate (SEE APPENDIX C).

Every rehearsal minute the studio books, on either arrangement, is paid at the same union scale
(SEE APPENDIX C), so booked rehearsal time is only part of what either option really costs. The
network's frame-accuracy delivery requirement is not negotiable, and a take that misses it does
not get a warning — it gets rejected on delivery, and the ensemble has to be re-booked for a whole
new session to re-cut the spot from scratch. Elena's recommendation has to be judged on one number:
expected total studio minutes required to deliver a broadcast-ready take — each option's booked
rehearsal minutes, plus the probability-weighted cost, in re-booked session minutes, of exactly
that happening (SEE APPENDICES A, C, D and E).
Every sourced and assumed figure in this case is listed in **APPENDICES E and F**, and the
vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in **APPENDIX G** — that is
where Challenge 1 sends you.

## Dilemma

You are Elena Cross.
Do you:
A.) Recommend The Straight Cut — ship the already-rehearsed straight-subdivision arrangement and
absorb the timing gap with a small tempo compromise OR
B.) Recommend The Downbeat Split — rewrite the beat with a syncopated subdivision that hits the
target duration exactly, at the cost of additional rehearsal time
