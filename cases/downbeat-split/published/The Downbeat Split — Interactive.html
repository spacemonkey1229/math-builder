Case Study: The Downbeat Split

Overview

The click track loops in Elena Cross's headphones for the ninth time, and every time it loops, the take underneath it is a hair long. Not enough that anyone at the network would flag it just by ear — you'd need a stopwatch, and Elena is the only one in the booth anyone trusts with one. The ensemble is on a break, brass mutes scattered across the risers, and the pencil marks on Elena's score are starting to look less like notation and more like frustration. On the console in front of her sits the client's spot, dropped into the session as a locked video reference, its outgoing black frame burned into the timeline like a deadline that already happened. The producer, headset draped around his neck, leans through the booth door for the third time this hour and says only, "Whenever you're ready" — which everyone in the room understands to mean the take is due before lunch.

Elena has two versions of the cue open side by side on her music stand. One is the version the ensemble already knows cold, clean straight subdivisions they could sight-read blind, and it lands the cue a hair off the mark — close enough that most arrangers would just nudge the tempo and call it a day. The other is scrawled in pencil in the margin of the last measure, a syncopated rewrite that splits one beat cleanly against the grain of the rest of the phrase, hits the mark exactly, and would mean starting the rehearsal clock over from zero with an ensemble that is already watching the wall clock over the concertmaster's shoulder.

She picks up her pencil, taps it once against the stand, and looks up.

Background

Elena Cross is a staff arranger at Larkspur Sound, a commercial audio production house, responsible for delivering a finished orchestral cue that fills the client's broadcast ad spot exactly, frame for frame, and accountable to the session's contracted producer for both the take's musical quality and the studio's booked rehearsal-hour budget (see Appendix A). The client's broadcast slot has one fixed, non-negotiable target duration the finished cue must fill (see Appendix A) — the network will not air a spot that runs long, and will not pad black frames to cover one that runs short.

Two arrangements of the same sixteen-bar cue are on Elena's stand tonight, and the ensemble has already worked through the first one in this session:

The Straight Cut — the arrangement already rehearsed, built entirely from straight, repeating mixed-number note values (see Appendix B). The ensemble can sight-read it cleanly, but multiplying the phrase's mixed-number beat length by the mixed-number seconds-per-beat at session tempo across the whole cue lands its total running time close to, but not exactly on, the broadcast slot's target — the remaining gap has to be absorbed as a small across-the-board tempo adjustment (see Appendices A and B).

The Downbeat Split — a rewrite of one beat in the final measure, splitting that beat by dividing a fraction of it by the ensemble's normal fractional subdivision unit (see Appendix D), sized to close the Straight Cut's timing gap exactly rather than approximately. The syncopated subdivision reads correctly on the page and hits the target slot precisely, but it is new material the ensemble has not rehearsed, and every additional read-through of an unfamiliar subdivision like this one is booked, and paid, at the studio's standard union rehearsal rate (see Appendix C).

Every rehearsal minute the studio books, on either arrangement, is paid at the same union scale (see Appendix C), so booked rehearsal time is only part of what either option really costs. The network's frame-accuracy delivery requirement is not negotiable, and a take that misses it does not get a warning — it gets rejected on delivery, and the ensemble has to be re-booked for a whole new session to re-cut the spot from scratch. Elena's recommendation has to be judged on one number: expected total studio minutes required to deliver a broadcast-ready take — each option's booked rehearsal minutes, plus the probability-weighted cost, in re-booked session minutes, of exactly that happening (see Appendices A, C, D and E).

Every sourced and assumed figure in this case is listed in Appendices E and F, and the vocabulary and "how to…" skills this case will ask of you are listed in Appendix G — that is where Challenge 1 sends you.

    Working Notebook

    This is a calculator, not an answer key. Type your own numbers first — then hit Check to see if your arithmetic matches. It won't tell you which option to choose; that part is still yours.

    Step 1 — The Straight Cut's total running time (D1)

    Multiply the phrase's mixed-number beat length by the mixed-number seconds-per-beat, then multiply across every phrase repeat (Appendix B). Enter mixed numbers as decimals if that's easier — a mixed number converts to a decimal by adding the whole part to the fraction part.

    Step 2 — Partial-phrase check (D2)

    Multiply a fraction of the final phrase's mixed-number beat length by the session tempo, to locate exactly where the broadcast slot's target cutoff falls (Appendix B).
    Does your Step 1 total, plus this partial-phrase time, land on 21 7/8 seconds? That's the target from Appendix A — the tool won't print it for you, but you can add it up yourself.

    Step 3 — The Downbeat Split's beat-subdivision calculation (D3)

    Divide the timing gap (as a fraction of the final phrase's beat length) by the ensemble's normal fractional subdivision unit (Appendix D).

    Step 4 — Rehearsal read-through fit-check (D4)

    Divide the mixed-number rehearsal time remaining in the booked session by the length of one read-through of the syncopated passage (Appendix D). How many complete read-throughs fit?

    Step 5 — Decision table and expected total minutes (D5)

    Rehearsal minutes required (minutes-per-read-through × whole-number read-throughs), plus probability-weighted redelivery-risk minutes (a stated fraction × the total booked session length), for both options.     Twist

    Ask your facilitator before revealing this — in a live discussion, the Twist is dropped at a specific moment, not read in advance.

    Reveal the Twist

    At roughly the 15-minute mark: the producer, checking his phone, mentions — almost as an aside — that this is not the first time Larkspur Sound has delivered a cue to this exact client on a tight tempo compromise. The last time, the network's traffic department rejected the take on delivery for running outside frame-accuracy tolerance, and Larkspur had to re-book the ensemble and re-cut the spot on the studio's own dime, after the ad buy's air date had already been locked.

    This does not hand you a new number — Appendix E's MA11 (the redelivery-risk probability for the Straight Cut) was on the page the whole time, and D1–D5 still compute exactly as stated. What changes is the weight you put on that number: it's easy to read MA11 as an abstract, hypothetical "what if" and discount it while doing the arithmetic. The Twist confirms it is not hypothetical — it already happened, to this exact client, on this exact kind of compromise.
